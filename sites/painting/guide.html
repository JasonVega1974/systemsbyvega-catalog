Your first week

Painting has two seasons running at once. Exterior work is weather-bound and the window closes; interior work runs all year and is what fills the months when it does. Decide early which one you are starting in, because it changes what you buy first.

  1. Decide interior, exterior, or both. Exterior coatings have a temperature and moisture window written on the can, and when the weather closes it you cannot paint through it. Interior work has no such limit. If you start in warm weather, plan now for what you will be doing when it turns — the operators who struggle are the ones who spend the whole exterior season quoting instead of working.
  2. Buy the prep tools before the pretty ones. A sprayer is what everybody buys first and it is not what makes a job look good. Buy a 5-in-1, a carbide scraper, sanding sponges and a pole sander, a caulk gun and caulk you actually like, joint compound and a set of knives, canvas drop cloths for floors, plastic and tape, and a stable ladder. Then one excellent angled sash brush and two roller frames with good covers.
  3. Open an account at an independent paint store. Not the big box. They will tint fast, take back a mistint, put you on contractor pricing, and — the part that actually matters — tell you which product holds up on the siding and the fascia in your climate. Pick one line and learn it: its coverage, its recoat window, how it sprays.
  4. Paint one room properly and time every stage separately. Masking, prep, cutting in, rolling, clean-up. You cannot estimate a job until you know how long your own hands take to cut a room, and no one else's number is any use to you.
  5. Build your quote sheet. The inputs are always the same: wall square footage, ceilings yes or no, linear feet of trim, number of doors and windows, the condition of what is already there, how big the colour change is, occupied or empty, and how high off the ground. Write them on a sheet you carry. Everything after that is arithmetic.
  6. Find out what the lead-paint rule requires of you. Work that disturbs paint in older homes and in child-occupied buildings is federally regulated in the United States through the EPA's Renovation, Repair and Painting rule, and some states run their own authorised version. Find out what certification it requires of a firm and of the person doing the work, and sort it out before you take a job on an old house rather than after.
  7. Photograph the first finished job like it matters. Same position for the before and the after, lights on, curtains open, shot from the doorway so you get the corner and two walls. Take the before shot before you move a single thing — you cannot go back for it.

What to charge

Your site ships three price ranges rather than three fixed prices: a room refresh, a full interior repaint, and a whole exterior, each with a low and a high end. Change the figures to your market. The range is the point — it is honest about the fact that two houses of the same size are not the same job.

A range only works if you say what moves it. Publish the number and then explain the variables out loud on the phone: the condition of the existing paint, how far the colour is moving, whether trim and ceilings are in, whether the house is lived in, and how high the work is. A range without an explanation reads as a guess; a range with one reads as experience.

Price the prep, not the paint. Paint is the cheapest part of a paint job. Washing, scraping, sanding, patching, caulking, masking and protecting the house is where the labour goes, and it is exactly what the cheapest quote in the driveway is leaving out. Put prep on the quote as its own line so the customer can see what they are actually comparing.

Never quote an exterior you have only seen from the street: chalking, failed caulk, soft fascia, and a previous owner's cheap latex sitting over oil are all invisible from thirty feet. Get the ladder on the wall, scrape a test patch in a couple of places, and check what the existing coat actually is before you commit to a number. Everything you miss on that walk you pay for yourself later.

Colour change costs coats. Deep colour over white, or white over deep colour, may need a tinted primer and a third coat. Decide that at the quote, price it there, and say so — your site already promises two coats minimum and three when the shift needs it. Discovering it on day two is a conversation nobody enjoys.

Change orders in writing, every time. "While you are here, could you do the hall closet" is how a three-day job becomes five. Say yes gladly, then write down what it adds and get a reply before you open the can.

Finding customers

Painting is a referral trade with two large exceptions, and the exceptions are what keep a calendar full between referrals.

  • An exterior is a month-long advertisement. The whole street watches an exterior job happen. Put a yard sign up while you work, keep the site tidy every evening, and knock on the two houses either side before you pull the ladders down. Nothing you can buy converts like the house next door.
  • Realtors and property managers. Pre-listing repaints and rental turnovers are empty-house work with a hard deadline, which means they buy on whether you will be finished on the day you said, more than on price. Introduce yourself with photographs of an empty house turned around fast. Property managers are the steadiest way to fill the gaps between homeowner jobs.
  • Remodelers and general contractors. A kitchen or bath remodeler needs a painter who turns up the day the drywall is ready and does not hold up the trades behind them. Be reachable, be on the day, and you end up on a rotation.
  • The colour visualizer is a conversation, not a gimmick. Somebody tapping through swatches has already decided to paint; they are only deciding what. Use it on the phone — ask them to open your site and tap while you talk. It turns a vague enquiry into a specific one, and a specific enquiry is a job.
  • Sell the interior season in autumn. Tell every exterior customer, while you are still on their ladder, that you paint interiors when the weather turns. Those customers have already seen your prep and already trust you, which makes them the easiest winter work you will book.
  • Cabinet refinishing is its own lead source. People search for it separately from painting, it is priced differently, and it photographs better than almost anything else you will do. Show it separately rather than burying it in a list of services.

Running the work

Prep is the job and paint is the end of it. Wash, scrape, sand, patch, caulk, spot-prime the repairs, mask. When the prep is right the painting itself is the quick part; when it is not, no product on the shelf saves you.

Protect the house before you open anything. Canvas on floors, plastic over furniture, hardware off or taped, and a path from the door to the room. The jobs people remember badly are almost never about the colour — they are about something that got splattered.

Never open a can of a colour the customer has not seen on their own wall: paint a two-foot square, leave it, and make them look at it in the morning and again under the lamps at night. A chip in a store lies about how a colour behaves in a north- facing room. Once a full room is on the wall, a change of mind is a new job — and that sample square is the cheapest hour you will ever spend.

Box your paint and keep a wet edge. Mix the gallons of a colour together in one bucket before you start so there is no shade break halfway down a wall, and work so you are always rolling into paint that is still wet. Both of these are invisible when you do them and glaringly obvious when you do not.

On exteriors, work to the weather and the can, not the calendar. Surface temperature, dew point, direct sun on a hot wall and the recoat window are all on the label for a reason. A coat that goes on in the wrong conditions fails months later, long after you have been paid, and the customer will still call you.

Label the leftovers and leave them. Write the room, the product, the sheen and the colour code on each can with a marker before you stack them in the garage. That is the touch-up call two years from now, and it is one of the main reasons a homeowner rings the same painter twice.

Walk the job with the customer and a bright light. Hand them a roll of blue tape and tell them to flag anything they see. Fix every flag on that visit. That walk is the difference between "it's finished" and "it's finished, here's the final invoice", and it is the last chance to catch what you stopped being able to see three days ago.

Using your site

Your admin is at /admin on your own address. Everything you change there is live within a minute or two.

  • Photos. The hero at the top, and your own photograph for the section about the person who quotes the job. Replace the hero with your own work as soon as you have a clean shot — a crisp cut line against a ceiling is a better hero for this trade than a wide room, because it shows the thing customers actually judge you on.
  • Pricing. Three tiers, each carrying a low and a high figure, plus the line items under it. Both ends of every range display on your site, so put real numbers in both. Keep the feature lines specific — "two coats, colour-matched" and "furniture moved and covered" are the sentences that win a comparison against a cheaper quote.
  • Details. Owner name and the paragraph about your business, which appear in their own section on the page. Write it as a person, not as a company; that section is there precisely because homeowners are letting a stranger into their house.
  • Reviews. Three slots, and they ship empty on purpose — the site contains no invented reviews. Fill them as you earn them, with the customer's permission, and use their real words even when they are less polished than what you would write.
  • Social links. Add only the accounts you keep current. An abandoned page is worse than no link.
  • Marketing kit. Generates a social image and a printable flyer with a QR code pointing at your site. Print the flyer for the doors you knock either side of an exterior job.
  • Leads. Every enquiry from your quote form is emailed to you and kept as a running list, so nothing is lost when the inbox fills up mid-season.

Your site also carries a Terms of Service and a Privacy Policy for your customers, generated from your business details. The Legal section of the admin is where a deposit rule, a change-order policy and your colour-approval rule belong — that last one saves an argument at least once a year.

Licences and insurance

This is orientation, not advice, and the rules vary considerably by state and by city. Check what applies where you work before you take money.

  • Business registration and contractor licensing. A registered business name and a local business licence are usually the first step. Some states license or register painting contractors, sometimes only above a job-value threshold, and some do not license the trade at all. Your state's contractor board is the place to ask.
  • Lead paint. This is the regulation that matters most in this trade. Work that disturbs paint in older housing and in child-occupied facilities is covered in the United States by the EPA's Renovation, Repair and Painting rule, and several states run their own authorised programme instead. Find out what certification it requires of your firm and of the person holding the scraper, and get it before you quote an old house. Enforcement here is real.
  • Liability insurance. You work in occupied houses, on ladders, with products that travel further than people expect. Overspray on a neighbour's car is the classic claim in this trade. Talk to a broker before your first paid job and be accurate about spraying and about working at height.
  • Workers' compensation. The moment you put a second person on a ladder this becomes a question, and the rules differ sharply by state including for casual help.
  • Disposal. Leftover paint, wash water and anything solvent-based have disposal rules, and wash water does not belong in a storm drain. Ask your paint supplier what the local practice is; they deal with it daily.
  • Sales tax. Whether labour is taxable, and whether materials are treated differently from labour, varies by state. Ask an accountant once, early, rather than guessing for a year of invoices.

Do not claim on your site that you are licensed, insured or lead-certified until each one is true. Certification in particular is checkable against a public register, and the homeowners most likely to check are the ones with the old house you want the job on.
