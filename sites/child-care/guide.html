Your first week

Babysitting is the easiest business in this catalog to start badly. The work itself is familiar to almost everyone, so people skip straight to taking bookings — and the two things that actually matter, the licensing question and the screening, are the two things that cannot be fixed afterwards. Do them first.

  1. Find out where the licensing line sits in your state, before you take a single booking. Child care is licensed everywhere in the United States, and what moves you across the line is some combination of how many children you have, how many families they come from, whose house you are in, and how many hours. Sitting in a family's own home is usually treated differently from care in yours — but "usually" is not "in your state". Call your state's child care licensing agency, describe exactly what you intend to do, and take notes. Some arrangements cannot be operated at all without a licence, and finding that out from a parent is far too late.
  2. Get paediatric CPR and first aid. Not adult CPR. Choking, infant CPR, allergic reaction, febrile seizure. Card in your wallet, copy in the folder you take to a meet-and-greet, and know it well enough that you do not have to think.
  3. Have yourself background-checked, and hold the result. Start it early, it takes time. Parents will ask. If you ever put a second sitter in front of a family, that sitter does not take a booking until their own check is back and you have looked at it — no exceptions for a friend, a cousin, or somebody who is very good with kids.
  4. Build the intake sheet and the fridge card. The sheet is the long version: allergies and what the reaction looks like, where the epi-pen or inhaler lives, doctor and preferred hospital, who is authorised to collect, house rules, screen rules, bedtime order of operations. The fridge card is the short version — parents' mobiles, the address written out for a 911 call, and the two emergency contacts. You will not be able to recall a street address in a panic.
  5. Do three meet-and-greets with families you already know, for nothing. The meet-and-greet is the sale and it has a shape: you arrive, you get on the floor with the kids, you let the parents interview you, and you leave with the sheet filled in. Practise it until you stop rushing the questions.
  6. Write your rate card and your minimum, and say them out loud. Base rate, the amount per additional child, the weekend bump, your shortest booking. Saying a number to a friendly parent is much harder than writing it, so rehearse it before it matters.
  7. Decide the hard nos now. Whether you drive children at all, and if so under what rules. No new medication that is not on the sheet. No handing a child to anyone not on the authorised list, ever, regardless of who says they are. No overnight until you have decided separately that you do overnights. Written down in advance, these are easy; improvised at the door, they are not.

What to charge

Your site does not carry a price list in the usual sense — it carries a planner. A parent picks age bands, care times and needs, and the planner adds it up from the rates you set in the admin: a base rate for one child, an amount per additional child, and a weekend evening bump. Change all three to your own market.

Per-child rather than flat is the right shape. Three children is not the same evening as one, and every parent knows it. Pricing it openly is easier than quoting a flat number and quietly resenting the third child.

Agree the number before the first booking, not on the night. This is what the note under the rates on your site promises, and it is the single most useful habit in the trade. Settle the hourly rate, the additional-child amount, the minimum, and how you get paid, at the meet-and-greet while everyone is relaxed.

The mistake nearly every sitter makes once: leaving payment as "we will sort it out later". Later is midnight, the parents are home from a wedding, nobody has cash, and you are standing in a hallway doing arithmetic in your head. Fix the method at the meet-and-greet — which app, or cash on the counter — and fix what happens if they get home after the booked end time. Rounding to the next quarter hour, stated in advance, ends that conversation before it starts.

Have a minimum for a Saturday night. You turned down the whole evening for that booking. A two-hour Saturday and a five-hour Saturday cost you the same Saturday.

You arrive early, and that is on you. Ten minutes before the booked start for the handoff is your promise, not billable time. Say so, so nobody wonders.

Cancellations. Your site says plans can change without drama, and for a regular family that is the right answer — but decide your own line for a weekend evening cancelled an hour out, and say it once at the meet-and-greet rather than inventing it while annoyed.

Finding customers

Parents do not hire a sitter from an advertisement. They hire the name another parent said out loud. That makes this a reputation business with a very short fuse: one good evening travels through a neighbourhood group in a week, and one bad one travels through it in an hour.

  • Answer the "anyone know a sitter for Friday?" post, fast. Local parent groups on Facebook and Nextdoor run on those posts. Reply with your real name, the town you cover, and one sentence about meeting first — not a wall of marketing. Speed matters more than polish here; the third reply rarely gets the booking.
  • The places parents already gather in person. Preschool and school parent groups, the dance studio waiting room, sports team sidelines, church nursery rosters, the MOPS-style morning groups. Volunteering in a church nursery or at a school event is how a stranger becomes a known quantity — and known is the entire qualification.
  • Other sitters. Counter-intuitive and the most underrated channel in this trade. A sitter who is already booked on Friday still gets asked about Friday. Be the person she passes it to, and pass work back when you are full. Two sitters who cover each other are worth more than either alone.
  • The families you already have. Rebooking is the business. Ask for the next date before you leave the house — parents book date nights in a rhythm, and the sitter who is already in the calendar does not get shopped for. A family that books twice a month is worth more to you than four families who book once.

What your site does is settle the question after your name has been mentioned. A parent gets a recommendation in a group thread and looks you up while the kids are eating. What she needs to see is a face, the towns you cover, an honest description of what you do and do not do, the rates in the planner, and a form that reaches you. The planner earns its place because it lets her sketch Friday night without having to ask a stranger a price question.

Skip the apps that take a cut. They train families to see a sitter as interchangeable, which is the opposite of everything this business sells.

Running the work

The ten minutes before the parents leave is the job. Walk the house: exits, alarm code, where the fire extinguisher is, which door is deadbolted, where medication and the epi-pen live, whether the gate latches. Confirm the return time. Confirm who is allowed in the house. Then let them go without a long goodbye — long goodbyes are what make small children cry.

Run their routine exactly, do not improvise a better one. Bath night or not, teeth then book or book then teeth, which stuffed animal, how far the door stays open. Children behave for a familiar routine run by a new person, and misbehave for a new routine run by anyone. Your job is to be boring in the right order.

Your phone is not yours during a booking. One text to the parents mid-evening — a line about how it is going — and otherwise it stays in your pocket. If a parent walks in and you are on your phone, nothing else you did that night registers.

Carry the bag. A deck of cards, a couple of books, stickers, a simple craft. Screens off by default, because that is what your site promises and it is also what makes you memorable. The sitter who brought something to do is the sitter the kids ask for.

Never post a photograph of somebody else's child: not to your feed, not to a story, not to a "look how cute" text to a friend, not with the face cropped out. Send photos to that child's own parents and nowhere else. Parents make different choices about their children's images than you would, and it is not your choice to make. A single post can end a sitting business in a neighbourhood.

If a child is hurt or ill, you call immediately. Not at the end of the evening, not when you are sure it is nothing. Call the parents, then work the sheet, and write down the time and what happened while it is fresh. A parent who hears about a bump from you at 8pm is grateful; a parent who finds it at 11pm is not.

Leave the note. What they ate, what they played, how bedtime landed, anything worth flagging. It takes three minutes and it is the single strongest reason a family books you again — it turns an evening they were nervous about into an evening they now have a record of.

Leave the house the way you found it. Toys in bins, dishes in the dishwasher, counters wiped. Nobody hires a sitter for cleaning, and everybody notices.

If you start using other sitters, screen them like a parent would. You have met them, you have their check, they have their own paediatric CPR, and the family meets them before they are ever left alone in that house. The moment you hand a family somebody you have not verified, you have taken on their risk and your own.

Using your site

Your admin is at /admin on your own address. Everything you change there is live within a minute or two.

  • Photos. Two slots — the hero and your own portrait. The portrait is the important one and it should be you, outdoors, looking at the camera. Do not use photographs of children you have sat for, even with permission; a stock hero is more honest and carries none of the risk.
  • Rates and the planner. Your pricing lives in the rate lines — the base, the per-additional-child amount, the weekend bump — and the planner does the arithmetic for the parent live. Edit those three and the estimate updates everywhere. Keep the note that says every rate is confirmed together before the first booking; it is the promise the whole page rests on.
  • Services and the evening rundown. Date night, after school, occasional daytime, mother's helper — delete the ones you do not offer rather than leaving them there hopefully. The rundown of an evening is what turns a stranger into somebody a parent can picture in their kitchen; rewrite it to match how your evenings actually go.
  • Details and hours. Put in the real answer to "when are you available" — which evenings, whether you do weekday mornings, how far ahead you take bookings. That is the question behind most enquiries.
  • Reviews. Empty until you add them, deliberately — nothing invented ships on this site. Add real ones only, with the parent's written permission, and never use a child's name in a review even if the parent wrote it that way.
  • Marketing kit. Generates a social image and a printable flyer with a QR code pointing at your site. The flyer belongs on a church noticeboard or a preschool pickup board, not on lamp posts.
  • Leads. Every enquiry from your form is emailed to you and kept as a running list. Reply the same day. A parent asking about Friday is asking several people about Friday.

Your site also carries a Terms of Service and a Privacy Policy for your customers, generated from your business details. Use the Legal section of the admin to add your own clauses — your cancellation window, your booking minimum, your late-pickup rule, and a plain statement that you provide in-home babysitting and not licensed day care.

Licences and insurance

Read this as orientation only. Care of other people's children is the most heavily regulated activity in this catalog. The rules are set state by state, they are strict, and some arrangements cannot be operated at all without a licence. Nothing below tells you what the rule is where you live — it tells you which questions to ask. Ask them of your state's child care licensing agency, an insurance broker and an attorney before you take a single client.

  • Child care licensing. Every state licenses child care and every state draws its own lines. The factors that typically decide which side you are on are how many children are in your care, how many separate families they come from, whose home the care happens in, and how many hours or how regularly. Sitting in the child's own home is often exempt where the same care in your own home would not be — but the thresholds, the exemptions and the definitions are all state law. Describe your exact plan to the licensing agency and get their answer before you build a business on an assumption.
  • Ratios and supervision rules. Where licensing does apply, states set limits on how many children one adult may supervise, often varying by age, and those limits are enforced. Do not guess at a number, and do not accept a booking because a parent tells you it is fine.
  • Background checks and registry checks. Criminal history checks and checks against child abuse and neglect registries are standard for people caring for children, and required outright in licensed settings. Which check, who runs it, and how often it is repeated are set by the state. The same applies to anybody you bring in — before their first booking.
  • CPR and paediatric first aid. Expected by parents everywhere and required in licensed settings. Keep the certification current rather than letting it lapse quietly.
  • Mandatory reporting. In many states anyone caring for children is a mandated reporter of suspected abuse or neglect, with a specific process and a deadline. Find out whether that is you where you live, and learn the process before you ever need it. This is a duty, not a judgement call.
  • Driving children. Car seat and restraint law is state law and it is specific. Separately, carrying passengers in the course of paid work may fall outside a personal auto policy. Ask your insurer directly before a child is ever in your car.
  • Insurance. Ask a broker specifically about care of children — many general liability policies exclude it, and that exclusion is exactly the thing you need covered. Ask too about working inside a client's home.
  • If you use other sitters. Whether they are your employees or contractors, whether workers' compensation applies, and whether you are placing them or employing them are serious questions with different answers in different states, and referring a sitter you screened is a different legal position from employing one. Get real advice before you build a roster.
  • Tax. Depending on the arrangement, household-employee rules can make the family your employer rather than you being self-employed. Ask an accountant once, early, rather than finding out in April.

Do not describe yourself on your site as licensed, certified, insured or background-checked until each one is true and you hold the document. Parents in this trade ask to see it, and they are right to. Claiming a credential you do not have is worse here than in any other business in this catalog.
