Your first week

Tattooing is regulated almost everywhere in the United States, usually through a health department, and the regulation lands on two separate things: you, and the room. Both are gates you pass before a client sits in the chair, not paperwork you catch up on afterwards. Everything else in your first week comes after that.

  1. Clear the licensing before you touch a machine. The categories you are likely to hear about are bloodborne-pathogen training, a registration or licence for the artist, a permit for the premises itself, and a first-aid or CPR certificate. The health authority commonly inspects a studio before it will license it. Phone yours, ask exactly what a single-chair private studio has to file and in what order, and get the answer from them rather than from another artist's memory.
  2. Set the room up for the inspection, not for the photographs. Hard non-porous surfaces you can actually wipe down, a dedicated hand sink, closed storage, a mounted sharps container, a clean setup area separated from where you eat or take payment. A room built to pass is also a room that works; a room built to look good on Instagram often is not.
  3. Write down what you will not tattoo. Decide before anyone asks: hands, necks and faces; cover-ups outside your skill; script in a language you do not read; anyone under the legal age; anyone who has been drinking. Saying it as a policy is easy. Improvising it with somebody sitting in front of you is not.
  4. Draw a flash sheet and price it. Your site ships a flash board — designs that are already drawn, priced flat, and can be claimed without a consultation. Flash is how a new book fills, because it removes the two things that stall a first-time client: not knowing what it will look like, and not knowing what it will cost.
  5. Set the deposit and cancellation rule before the first booking. A deposit holds the date and comes off the final price; it covers the drawing time you lose if they vanish. Decide the notice window for moving an appointment, write it down, and put it in the Legal section of your admin so it is on your own site rather than only in a message thread.
  6. Build the portfolio from healed work. A fresh tattoo is red, swollen and shiny, and every tattoo looks good the day it is done. Ask every client to send a photograph once it has settled, and build the site's portfolio from those. Shoot them yourself when you can: same light, same distance, limb straight, no filter.
  7. Decide how you open and close the books. Your site has a switch for it and a message that shows when they are closed. Pick how you will announce openings — a date, a form, a post — and use the same method every time, so people learn where to look instead of asking you.

What to charge

Your site prices two things differently on purpose. Flash pieces carry an individual price, because they are already drawn and the size and placement are decided. Custom work is quoted, because until you have seen the reference, the placement and the skin you are guessing.

Set a studio minimum and hold it. Setup and breakdown, a fresh needle and tube, ink, barrier film, the drawing time and the room cost you the same for a tiny piece as for a large one. The minimum is not greed; it is what makes a small tattoo worth sitting down for.

Quote custom work after you have seen it on the body. Not from a text message describing an idea, and not from a reference photo alone. Placement changes the size, size changes the hours, and a design that reads flat on paper may not sit on a forearm at all. If you must give a number early, give a range and say plainly what will move it.

Price the whole piece, not the first sitting. For anything multi-session, agree what the finished thing costs and how many sittings you expect, then book them. A client who thought they were buying one afternoon and discovers they are buying four is a client who stops halfway, and half a sleeve is nobody's portfolio photograph.

Do not discount to fill a quiet month: the reflex is a half-price flash sheet, and it works exactly once. It brings people who chose you on price rather than on the drawing, and it resets what your existing clients believe your work costs — a number that is very hard to walk back up. Draw a new sheet instead, or post the work you already have. Empty weeks are part of this trade, not an emergency.

Write the touch-up policy down. What you fix free and for how long, what depends on aftercare, and which placements you exclude. Say it before the session, not after somebody sends you a photograph of a peeling forearm.

Finding customers

Nobody picks a tattoo studio. They pick an artist whose work already looks like the tattoo they want. That single fact decides every channel in this trade.

  • The portfolio is the whole pitch. Post healed work, shot the same way every time — consistent light, cropped tight, the limb in the same position. Twelve pieces that obviously came from one hand will book more work than fifty that do not. If you tattoo blackwork and fine line, your feed should look like blackwork and fine line, not like a shop sampler.
  • Instagram is the window; your site is the counter. People find you scrolling and then need somewhere to actually ask. Put the site link in the bio and answer enquiry messages by pointing at the booking form — it collects placement, size, reference and availability in one go instead of forty separate replies, and it puts the request somewhere you can find it again.
  • Guest spots and conventions. A week in another artist's shop, or a weekend at a convention, puts your drawing in front of people who came out specifically to look at tattoos. It is also how a private one-chair studio meets anybody at all. Check what tattooing in that city or state requires of a visiting artist before you book the travel — it is frequently its own registration.
  • The person already in your chair. Book the next piece before they leave the room. Sleeves grow, pairs get matched, and the friend who came along to watch is sitting right there. A single-chair studio runs on returning clients far more than on new ones.
  • Artists you turn away work to. When a concept is not your style, send it by name to someone whose style it is. Do that consistently and it comes back. Between professionals, that referral is the most durable channel in this trade because it is built on being right about your own limits.

The healed-photo request doubles as your review request. When somebody sends you the photograph, that is the moment to ask whether you may use it and whether they would write a line about the experience.

Running the work

Setup and breakdown are the job. Barrier film on every surface a gloved hand will touch — machine, clip cord, bottles, lamp handle, chair, armrest. Needles and tubes come out of a sealed pouch in front of the client, every time, and go into the sharps container rather than the bin. Wipe down, then set up the next client from a clean surface, not from the last one.

The stencil is where you prevent the disaster. Place it on the body, let them stand up and look in a mirror, and move it as many times as it takes. A design that works on paper may not follow the curve of a calf. You are the one who has to live with it in the portfolio.

Never tattoo through a doubt: yours or theirs. If the client is hesitating, has been drinking, is being pushed by the friend who came with them, or is still arguing the design once the stencil is on, stop and rebook. The half hour of awkwardness is nothing next to a permanent piece neither of you wanted, on skin you cannot undo.

Give aftercare in writing as well as out loud. Nobody retains a spoken instruction after two hours in a chair. Hand them a card or send it as a message, then check in once while it is healing. A tattoo that heals badly is a portfolio photograph you never get and a story you never get to correct.

Protect your own body. Chair height, your posture, magnification, and a real break between clients. This trade ends careers through hands, necks and backs, not through lack of work. Set the room up around your body now, while it is cheap to change.

The calendar is the business. One chair means the day is the constraint, so guard it: confirm the day before, keep a short-notice list so a cancellation gets filled, and leave a gap between long sessions rather than stacking them and running late into somebody else's afternoon.

Using your site

Your admin is at /admin on your own address. Everything you change there is live within a minute or two.

  • Photos. Two zones: the hero at the top and your own portrait for the artist section. For the hero, your workspace or your machines in flat, even light suits this design better than a tattoo does — the tattoos have their own grid below it.
  • Pricing. The Pricing section edits the flash board: the name of each design and its price. Rewrite them as your sheet changes. Custom work stays quoted, which is why there is no rate card on the page.
  • The portfolio and flash artwork. Both grids ship with drawn placeholders rather than photographs, so nobody's real work is being passed off as yours. Send us your healed photographs and they replace the placeholders.
  • Details and the books. Studio name, town, phone and the inquiry email live in Business. The booking form has an open and a closed state with a message people see when the books are shut — use it rather than leaving the form open and answering nobody.
  • Reviews. This design does not carry a reviews section, and the admin will tell you so. Anywhere else you quote a client, use only words a real person said, with their permission — never a composite, never invented.
  • Marketing kit. Generates a social image and a printable flyer with a QR code pointing at your site. The flyer is worth having at a convention table and at any shop where you guest.
  • Leads. Every booking request from your form is emailed to you and kept as a running list, so an enquiry does not disappear under a week of messages.

Your site also carries a Terms of Service and a Privacy Policy for your customers, generated from your business details. The Legal section of the admin is where your deposit rule, your rescheduling window and your touch-up policy belong, so they are written down somewhere you can point at.

The site deliberately ships with no credential badges on it — nothing claiming licensed, certified or insured. Ask us to add yours once the certificates are on your wall, and not before.

Licences and insurance

This is orientation, not advice. Tattooing is a regulated trade in most of the United States and the rules vary sharply — some states run it at state level, some delegate it to counties, and the names of the permits differ everywhere. Find out what applies where you work before you tattoo anybody for money, and take the answer from the authority itself.

  • Bloodborne pathogens. Training and certification in bloodborne-pathogen control is one of the near-universal requirements in this trade, and it usually has to be current rather than one-and-done. Ask your health authority which course they accept and how often it must be renewed.
  • Licensing you, and licensing the room. These are commonly two separate approvals: a registration or licence for the artist, and a permit for the premises that follows an inspection. A home studio may not be permitted at all in your jurisdiction. Find this out before you sign a lease or convert a spare room.
  • Apprenticeship and hours. Some states require a documented apprenticeship, a minimum number of hours, or a supervising licensed artist before they will license you at all. If yours does, that is a timeline, not a formality.
  • Client age and consent. Every state sets a minimum age, and the rules on parental consent differ enormously — some allow it with a parent present, some do not allow it at all. Know yours exactly, check identification every single time, and keep the consent forms. This is the rule that ends studios.
  • Records and waste. Health authorities typically specify how long you keep client records and consent forms, and how sharps and biohazard waste are disposed of. Sharps disposal usually means a contract with a licensed hauler, not your household bin.
  • Insurance. Talk to a broker about general and professional liability before your first paid client, and be accurate about what you actually do. A landlord will often require cover as a condition of the lease.
  • Business registration and sales tax. A registered business name and a local business licence are usually the first step, separate from the health permit. Whether a tattoo service is taxable varies by state; ask an accountant once, early.

Do not describe yourself as licensed, certified or insured anywhere — site, social, shop window — until each one is true and current. In a regulated trade those claims are checkable against a public register, and a client who checks and finds nothing will tell people.
