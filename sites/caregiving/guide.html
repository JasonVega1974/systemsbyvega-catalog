Your first week

This trade differs from every other one in the catalog in a single way: before you sell anything, somebody has to decide they will leave their mother alone in a house with you. Everything below is about earning that, and about the paperwork you need in hand before you can honestly ask for it. Do these in order, and do not take a client until the first two are done.

  1. Find out what your state calls what you are doing. Non-medical home care is a regulated category in many states — sometimes a registration, sometimes a licence, sometimes different rules for an agency than for one person working alone. Call your state's health or licensing department, describe exactly what you plan to do, and write down what they tell you. Some arrangements cannot be operated at all without a licence. This is the first call of the week, not the last.
  2. Write your scope on one page, and put the word "no" in it. Non-medical means companionship, meals, light housekeeping, reminders, errands and rides. It does not mean giving medication, wound care, injections, or anything a nurse is trained for. Your site's FAQ already draws that line — copy it onto the page you hand a family, because you will be asked to cross it in the first month and it is far easier to point at a sentence than to invent one at a kitchen table.
  3. Get yourself checked, and get the card. Whatever criminal-history and registry checks your state uses for people working with vulnerable adults, start them now — they take time to come back. Add CPR and first aid. Put the certificates in a folder you carry to consultations. The adult daughter is going to ask, and she should.
  4. Talk to an insurance broker before your first visit. Tell them plainly: alone in a client's home, handling their property, possibly driving them. Ask about liability cover and about bonding. Do not put "bonded and insured" on anything until the policy exists — that claim is checkable, and families check it.
  5. Build the intake sheet. One page per client: daily routine, mobility and what a transfer looks like, diet and allergies, doctors and pharmacy, emergency contacts in order, who else holds a key, and — the question nobody asks and everybody needs — what a bad day looks like, so that you recognise one.
  6. Do one free consultation with a family you already know. The in-home consultation is the whole sale in this trade, and it is a conversation, not a pitch. Practise sitting down, letting the older person answer first, and writing while they talk. Practise until you stop filling the silences.
  7. Decide your limits before somebody asks you to break them. Maximum hours a week. Whether you take overnights. How far you will drive. Whether you lift. Write the answers down while nobody is waiting on them.

What to charge

Your site ships with three tiers: hourly with a two-hour visit minimum, a discounted rate for daily visits, and a flat overnight rate. Rewrite every figure for your own market — they are a shape, not a recommendation.

The two-hour minimum is not greed, it is arithmetic. A forty-minute visit across town costs you the drive twice and most of an hour either side. Keep the minimum, explain it once at the consultation, and it never comes up again.

Price the daily plan lower on purpose. A family visiting five days a week is a schedule you can build a life around; an occasional Tuesday is not. The discount buys predictability, which in this trade is worth more than the difference.

Overnight is a different product and needs a different word. Decide and write down whether an overnight means awake and working, or present and asleep unless needed. They are not the same job, and families will assume whichever one is cheaper.

The customer is usually not the person in the room: the daughter three states away is often the one who calls, worries and pays, while her father is the one you actually spend the afternoon with. Quote to both. Send the written plan to her the same day, and keep her informed after every change — a family that feels informed almost never argues about an invoice.

Mileage and errands. Driving a client to an appointment is billed time; the miles are a separate line at a rate you set once. Groceries and prescriptions bought on their behalf are reimbursed at cost with the receipt stapled to the log — never rounded, never marked up, and never out of your own pocket without a record.

Raising rates. In writing, with real notice, never mid-week and never to a client's face at the end of a shift. Many of these families budget to the month on a fixed income, and a letter six weeks out is received completely differently from a conversation on a Friday.

Finding customers

Almost nobody chooses care from an advertisement. They ask a professional who has seen this before, and they ask a neighbour. So the work of finding customers here is mostly the work of being known to a short list of people who get asked.

  • Discharge planners and hospital social workers. The strongest channel in this trade by a distance. When an older person is going home after a fall or a hospital stay, somebody in that building is asked "who do we call?" and answers from memory. Go in during business hours, ask for case management or discharge planning, leave a single page with your scope, your coverage area and your phone number, and ask them directly what they need to see before they will give out a name. Then send them exactly that.
  • Home health and hospice agencies. They cover the medical hours; you cover the twenty-two hours between them. You are not competing with them and they know it. A nurse who visits twice a week and finds the house cold and the fridge empty is looking for precisely what you sell.
  • The places older people already are. Senior centres, adult day programs, the Area Agency on Aging, meal delivery routes, church visitation committees. Show up more than once. A senior centre's volunteer coordinator speaks to more families in a month than a year of advertising reaches.
  • Elder-law attorneys, financial advisors and senior move managers. They meet families at the exact moment care becomes a question — a house being sold, a power of attorney being signed. One good relationship here is worth a great deal of doorknocking.

Your website's job in all of this is confirmation, not discovery. Somebody has been given your name and is looking you up at eleven at night from another state. What she needs to find is a real face, a plainly stated scope, a coverage area with her father's town in it, and a way to send a message that gets answered before morning. Answer it before morning.

Keep a note of every referral and where it came from, and thank the person who sent it — a phone call, not a gift card. Referral sources send a second one when they hear the first one worked out.

Running the work

Same caregiver, as far as you can hold it. This is the promise on your site and it is the actual product. Continuity is what lets you notice that she ate less this week, that he is holding the rail differently, that the mail is piling up. A rotating stranger cannot notice any of that, and the noticing is what families are buying.

Write a log every visit, before you leave the driveway. What was eaten, mood, sleep, anything that changed, anything you did. It takes four minutes. It is what you send the daughter, it is what you hand a nurse, and if a question is ever raised about a fall or a missing item, a log written at the time is the only thing that answers it.

Know your escalation rule and write it on the intake sheet. What makes you call the family. What makes you call 911 first and the family second. Decide it in advance, with the family in the room, because the moment you need it is the worst possible moment to be working it out.

The line that ends careers is money, not care: never accept a gift of value, never take cash outside the invoice, never accept a bank card, never let yourself be named on an account, a deed or a will. If a client offers, refuse warmly and tell the family the same day that it was offered and refused. Financial exploitation of older adults is taken extremely seriously, and a caregiver who accepted a "thank you" cheque has no way to prove that is what it was.

Scope creep arrives as a small favour. It starts with sorting the pill organiser, and the next week you are asked to hand him the pill. Say no the first time, kindly, and offer the alternative — a home health nurse, a pharmacy blister pack. A reminder is not administration, and the difference is not a matter of opinion; your state draws that line and it is narrower than most families assume.

Confidentiality is absolute, and small towns are small. No names in conversation, no photographs of a client or their home, nothing on social media, not even flattering. A diagnosis is not yours to mention to anyone the family has not named.

Have a named backup before you need one. You will get flu. Somebody who depends on a daily visit cannot simply not be visited. Line up another caregiver you would trust, introduce them to the family in advance, and never no-show — one unexplained missed visit does more damage here than a bad month does anywhere else.

Look after your own back and your own head. Refuse lifts you are not trained and equipped for; a fall with two people in it is twice the disaster. And this work means being close to people who are declining, and sometimes dying. Know that going in, keep hours you can sustain, and do not take a fifth overnight because somebody asked nicely.

Using your site

Your admin is at /admin on your own address. Everything you change there is live within a minute or two.

  • Photos. Two slots — the hero and your own portrait. Fill the portrait first. A face with a name under it does more work on this site than any other image, because the entire decision is about trusting a person. Never photograph a client or the inside of a client's home, even with permission, even with the face out of frame.
  • Pricing. The three tiers and every line inside them are yours to rewrite. State the minimum and say what an overnight actually means, so the awkward questions are answered on the page instead of at a kitchen table.
  • Care needs and details. The care-needs picker lets a family sketch a week before they call — companionship, meals, housekeeping, reminders, transport, overnights. Edit those to what you genuinely offer and delete what you do not. Put real availability in the details, including whether you cover weekends, because that is the question sitting behind half the enquiries.
  • Reviews. Empty until you add them, deliberately — nothing invented ships on this site. Add real ones only, with written permission, and ask the family whether they want their full name, an initial, or "daughter of a client in Nampa". Many will choose the last, and that is fine.
  • Marketing kit. Generates a social image and a printable flyer with a QR code pointing at your site. Print the flyer for the discharge planner's desk and the senior centre noticeboard.
  • Leads. Every enquiry from your form is emailed to you and kept as a running list. Response time is part of the service here — a family enquiring about care is usually enquiring because something happened this week.

Your site also carries a Terms of Service and a Privacy Policy for your customers, generated from your business details. Add your own clauses in the Legal section of the admin — a cancellation window, your visit minimum, your holiday policy, and a plain restatement that the service is non-medical.

Licences and insurance

Read this as orientation only. In-home care for older and vulnerable adults is among the most heavily regulated things in this catalog, the rules differ sharply from state to state, and some arrangements cannot legally be operated without a licence. Nothing below is advice, and nothing below tells you what the rule is where you live. Take every item to your own state's licensing office, to a broker and to an attorney before you take a single client.

  • Home care registration or licensure. Many states regulate non-medical home care as its own category, and the rules can differ depending on whether you run an agency, place caregivers with families, or work alone. Some states require a licence, some a registration, some neither. Ask your state health or licensing department directly, and describe your actual plan rather than a general one.
  • Background and registry checks. Checks against criminal history and against abuse or exclusion registries are commonly required for anyone working with vulnerable adults. Which check, who runs it, and how often it is repeated are all set by the state. If you ever bring on a second caregiver, the same applies to them — before a first shift, not after.
  • Mandatory reporting. Many states name people who care for vulnerable adults as mandated reporters of suspected abuse, neglect or financial exploitation, with a defined process and a deadline. Find out whether that is you where you live, and learn the process before you need it.
  • Scope of practice. Medication administration, wound care, injections and various hands-on tasks are nursing acts under state law. Where the line falls between a reminder and administration, and what an unlicensed caregiver may do at all, is set by your state's nurse practice act and its delegation rules. Find that line and stay well clear of it.
  • Insurance and bonding. Talk to a broker about liability cover for work performed alone inside somebody's home, and about a bond, which is what families ask for by name. If you will drive clients, ask specifically whether your auto policy covers carrying a passenger in the course of paid work — personal policies commonly do not.
  • Employment rules if you add a second person. Whether a caregiver is your employee or a contractor, whether workers' compensation applies, and how overtime and domestic-worker rules land are among the sharpest questions in this trade, and they are answered differently in different states. Get real advice before you build a team, not after.
  • Tax and payment sources. Whether your service is taxable, and how long-term care insurance or veterans' benefits pay a provider like you, both vary. Ask an accountant once, early.

Do not describe yourself on your site as licensed, certified, bonded, insured or background-checked until each one is true and you hold the paper. A family will ask to see it. Being asked for a document you do not have ends that relationship, and in a town this size it ends several others with it.
