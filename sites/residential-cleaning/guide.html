Your first week

You are not selling cleaning. You are selling a standing appointment in somebody's home, on the same weekday, with the same person walking through their bedroom. That is a trust business before it is a cleaning business, and the first week is about building the system that keeps a house on the schedule rather than about learning to mop.

  1. Write the checklist before you write the price. Room by room, task by task, in the order you will physically work: kitchen, bathrooms, bedrooms, floors last. This is the document that defines what "clean" means. Without it, every visit is judged against whatever the customer imagined, and you will lose that argument every time.
  2. Decide now what you do not do. You clean, you do not organise. Dishes, laundry, putting away a child's toys, moving heavy furniture, biohazard, exterior windows, ladders above a step stool. Put the list on paper, because you will be asked in the first month and it is far easier to have decided in advance.
  3. Deep clean one house and time every room. Your own, your mother's, a friend's. Write down the minutes per room. You cannot price a walkthrough until you know what your own hands cost you in time, and guessing that is how a beginner takes on a four-bedroom for a two-bedroom price.
  4. Buy for surfaces, not for brands. A vacuum with a real filter, microfibre in colour-coded sets so a bathroom cloth never touches a kitchen counter, flat mop with several washable pads, a caddy, gloves. Learn what not to use before you learn what to use: acid on natural stone etches it, vinegar dulls sealed stone, wet mopping soaks unsealed wood, and the wrong product on luxury vinyl leaves a haze. A ruined countertop ends the relationship.
  5. Practise the walkthrough as a script. Every room, ask what actually bothers them, write it on the checklist in their words, and give the flat per-visit number before you leave. The walkthrough is free, nothing gets signed, and the price does not change afterwards. That sequence is your entire sales process.
  6. Set up your key and access system on day one. Where codes are written, how a key is labelled — never with an address on the tag — and who else can see them. Do this before you hold anybody's key, not after you have three.
  7. Photograph a finished kitchen and a finished bathroom, empty. Daylight, wide, no people, no family photographs, no post, no prescriptions on a counter. Somebody else's home is not your portfolio unless you have removed everything that identifies them and asked.

What to charge

Your site ships with four rows: a standard visit, a one-off first deep clean, a discounted recurring visit, and a move-in or move-out flat quote after a walkthrough. Change every figure to your own market — the structure is what earns its keep.

Price the visit, never the hour. Hourly pricing punishes you for getting faster, and it makes the customer watch the clock instead of the work. A flat per-visit price agreed at the walkthrough means the fifth visit — quicker, because you know the house — pays the same as the first. That is the whole reward for learning somebody's home.

The deep clean first, then the recurring rate, and say why. A recurring price is priced against a house that has already been reset. Taking on a neglected house at the maintenance rate means eating the difference every visit for months, and quietly resenting the client. Put the deep clean first on the page as a requirement, in plain words, so it never has to be negotiated at a kitchen table.

Never re-price in the house. If a home turns out bigger, dirtier or more crowded than the walkthrough suggested, finish the visit you agreed and then send the new number in writing before the next one. Adjusting the price while standing in somebody's kitchen feels like a hostage situation to them, and it is the fastest way to lose a client you would otherwise have kept for years.

Discount cadence, not the work. Every-other-week pays less per visit than a one-off because the house is easier by then and the slot is reliable. Never discount by quietly doing less — a shorter checklist for a lower price is the thing clients notice and never mention until they cancel.

Write the cancellation and lockout terms into the site before your first booking. How much notice moves a visit, and what happens when you drive out and nobody can let you in. Every cleaner ends up needing this rule, and the ones who write it after the first incident have to have an awkward conversation about it.

Finding customers

House cleaning grows by referral and by proximity, in that order. The best schedule is four houses in one neighbourhood on one day, and you build it street by street rather than with advertising.

  • Ask your existing clients, specifically. Not "tell your friends" — ask whether anybody on their street or at their work has mentioned wanting a cleaner, and offer something real for the introduction. Clients talk to neighbours with the same size house and the same weekday needs, which is exactly the customer you want next.
  • Estate agents and property managers, for turnovers. Move-out cleans, listing preparation and rental turnovers are booked by people who need the same job done repeatedly and to a deadline. They are the opposite of your recurring work — bigger, one-off, on short notice — and they fill gaps in a week that recurring clients have not filled yet.
  • Neighbourhood groups, where you answer rather than advertise. Somebody asks for a cleaner recommendation in a local group most weeks. Being the name that a real neighbour types out is worth more than any post you make about yourself. Get your first clients to be the ones who type it.

Two things that quietly matter more than any channel. First, be reachable: this trade is won by whoever calls back the same day, because somebody deciding to hire a cleaner has usually just had a bad week. Second, do not undercut yourself into a schedule you cannot serve — a full diary of houses priced too low is harder to escape than an empty one.

Short-term rental turnovers look attractive and are a different business: same-day windows, linens, restocking, and a cancellation when a guest extends. Price them separately or leave them alone.

Running the work

Same weekday, same order, every time. Work top to bottom and dry to wet, finish with the floors, and back out of the house rather than walking across what you just mopped. A fixed route through the home is what stops you standing in a doorway wondering whether you did the mirror.

Colour-code the cloths and never break it. One colour that only ever touches bathrooms, another for kitchen surfaces, another for glass. It costs nothing, it is the professional standard, and it is a sentence you can say to a client that instantly separates you from somebody with a bucket.

Tell them before they find it. A chipped mug, a mark that was already on the worktop, a blind that came apart in your hand. Text the client from the house, with a photograph, the moment it happens. Damage disclosed by the cleaner is an accident. Damage discovered by the homeowner after the cleaner left is a character question, and there is no recovering from it.

Keep a note for every house, and read it in the car. Which door code, where the dog is shut in, that the guest bathroom is not used, that she hates the smell of lemon, that the vacuum stays there. Two minutes of reading before you walk in is what makes the fourth visit feel like a service instead of a stranger arriving.

Adjust the checklist out loud after early visits. Ask what to do more of, change it that day, and tell them it is changed. Most clients will not complain, they will simply stop booking, so you have to go and get the feedback while they are still happy enough to give it.

Hiring is the wall, and it is a decision about paperwork before it is a decision about people. Whether a second cleaner can be a contractor or must be an employee is not yours to choose freely — it is set by law and it decides your tax, insurance and workers' compensation position. Get that answered before you put anybody in a client's home, not after.

Protect the day off. This is physical work in a fixed weekly rhythm, and the failure mode is not going out of business — it is quietly filling every weekday and then every Saturday until you cannot keep the standard up. Leave a gap in the schedule on purpose.

Using your site

Your admin is at /admin on your own address. Everything you change there is live within a minute or two.

  • Photos. A hero and a before/after pair. Shoot in daylight, wide enough to read the room, and strip anything personal out of frame — post, photographs, a school timetable on the fridge. Ask the homeowner before you photograph their house at all, even for a before-and-after they will never be named in.
  • Pricing. Four rows, with the label, the price and the note yours to edit. Use the note lines to carry the conditions — what size of home the standard rate assumes, that the deep clean comes first, that the recurring rate has no contract behind it. Those notes are the answers to the questions you would otherwise field on the phone.
  • Job details. What is included and what is not. Put the boundary here: no dishes, no laundry, no organising, nothing above a step stool — whatever you decided in week one. This section prevents more awkward conversations than any other part of the site.
  • Details and hours. Put your real working days in, and say plainly if you do not clean on weekends. Add the areas you actually drive to, and be honest about the edge of your range rather than taking a booking you cannot keep.
  • Reviews. Empty by design — the site ships with no invented reviews on it. Add real ones as you earn them, with the customer's permission, and never use a client's full name or their street.
  • Marketing kit. Generates a social image and a printable flyer with a QR code pointing at your site. The flyer's best use is the four houses either side of a client who already books you.
  • Leads. Every enquiry from the walkthrough form is emailed to you and kept as a running list. Answer the same day, even if only to book the walkthrough for later in the week.

Your site also carries a Terms of Service and a Privacy Policy for your customers, generated from your business details. Add your own clauses in the Legal section of the admin — the cancellation window, the lockout rule, how you handle keys and codes, and what happens if something is broken.

Licences and insurance

This is orientation, not advice, and the rules vary enormously by state and by city. Check what applies where you live before you take money.

  • Business registration. Most places want a registered business name and a local business licence before you trade. Usually quick and cheap, and the first thing to do.
  • Liability insurance. You are alone in other people's homes around their possessions. Talk to a broker before your first paid job and describe the work honestly, including whether you hold keys and whether anybody works with you.
  • Bonding. Clients in this trade ask about it specifically, because it concerns theft rather than accidents. It is a different product from liability cover and it is bought separately. Ask a broker what it does and does not do before you use the word anywhere on your site.
  • Employees versus contractors. The most consequential question in this trade and the one most easily got wrong. Whether a helper is legally an employee is decided by tests set in law, not by what you call them or what they agree to, and getting it wrong reaches back into payroll tax and workers' compensation. Ask an accountant before you hire, not afterwards.
  • Workers' compensation. Triggered in most states by having employees at all, sometimes by a single one, and the thresholds differ sharply. Check your state specifically.
  • Keys, codes and background checks. There is no single national rule, but never advertise that your staff are screened unless you actually run and pay for screening. That claim is checked more often in this trade than in any other on this platform.
  • Sales tax. Whether residential cleaning is taxable varies by state, and in some places it depends on the type of clean. Ask an accountant once, early.

Do not claim on your site that you are licensed, insured or bonded until you are. A homeowner deciding whether to hand you a key will check, and in a town this size they will ask somebody who knows.
