Your first week

You are not in the business of selling used things. You are in the business of giving somebody their Saturday and their garage back. The client is usually moving in three weeks, or has just cleared a parent's house, or has finally lost the argument with their spouse about the third bike. They dread every part of this and they are hiring you to take it. Set up around that.

  1. Build the kit and keep it loaded. Eight to ten folding tables, sawhorses and a couple of sheets of plywood, tarps, a pop-up canopy, table cloths, sticker guns, a marker set, a cash apron, a float of ones and fives and quarters, a card reader, grocery bags and newspaper for wrapping, a hand truck, and a stack of corner signs with stakes and zip ties. It lives in the trailer, packed the same way every time, so a sale never starts with a hardware run.
  2. Decide the smallest sale you will take. That is what the minimum on your site is for. A two-table driveway takes almost the same staging day as a full one and a crew still gives up a Saturday for it. Learn to say "you don't need us for this" and mean it — that call comes back as a referral more often than a job you resented.
  3. Write the terms on one page. The commission, the minimum, the rain date, when they get their money, and what happens to what does not sell. Clients read the leftovers paragraph hardest, because that pile at 4 p.m. is the thing they are most afraid of.
  4. Line up the donation outlet before your first sale. Which charity takes furniture, which takes clothes only, which will not take mattresses or upholstered goods, whether they collect and on what days, and how the client gets the receipt. Get this wrong once and you own a trailer full of somebody else's things on a Sunday.
  5. Check your towns' sign and sale rules before you book a date. Most cities have something to say about signs on the verge and how many sales an address may hold in a year, and plenty of HOAs have more. This is your call to make, not the client's — your name is on the sign.
  6. Run one for a friend, free, end to end. Sort, stage, price, sign, sell, mark down, haul out, settle. Time the staging especially. Everybody underestimates it, and the number you learn on that job is what makes every quote afterwards honest.
  7. Photograph that sale properly. Shoot the driveway from the street at about nine in the morning when the tables are full and the light is still low and sideways. That photograph — full tables, cars parked up the street, people walking the corridor — is the whole pitch, and it goes on your site under Admin, Photos.

What to charge

Your site ships a percentage model: a commission of 40% on what sells and a $200 minimum. Both are editable in the admin. They are a starting shape for your market and nothing more.

Why the percentage is higher here than an estate company's. The totals in a driveway sale are small and the labour is not. Sorting, staging, pricing and signing a garage is a day, sale day is a day, and the haul-out is a third. A commission has to carry all of that on a number that was never going to be large. Say that plainly at the walkthrough — clients understand it immediately when you frame it as a day rate the sale has to cover.

The minimum is a floor, not a fee. You take the greater of the two, and you say so before the date goes on the calendar. It is what makes a small driveway worth the crew's weekend, and it is the number that lets you say yes to the jobs everyone else turns down.

The client will want to price too high, and it costs you both: that hedge trimmer cost real money when it was new and they remember it. Explain the alignment out loud — you are on commission, so you have every reason to get real money for the good things, and no reason at all to let the rest sit. Then price to clear, bundle the small stuff, and agree the markdown schedule in writing before sale day so the last hour is not a negotiation with the person whose things they are.

Never estimate the total. Your site says this in so many words and it is the right policy. Nobody can honestly guess what a driveway will bring, and the number you say at a kitchen table on a Tuesday is the number you get judged against on Saturday afternoon. What you promise is the work: the sorting, the staging, the signs, the staffing, the haul-away and the settlement sheet.

Charge separately for what leaves in a truck. A donation run is reasonable to fold in. A trailer of genuine rubbish going to the transfer station is a gate fee and a trip, and it belongs on its own line, quoted at the walkthrough.

Finding customers

Like an estate company, you are selling to two crowds — the household that hires you and the shoppers who turn up on Saturday. Unlike an estate company, your client is almost always on a deadline, and the deadline is what you market to.

  • Get in front of people who are already moving. That is the trigger. Apartment complex and self-storage managers, the counter at the truck rental place, realtors with a listing going live in a month, and the "moving, everything must go" posts in local buy-and-sell groups. Someone who has just booked a truck for the end of the month has a garage problem right now, and almost nobody is talking to them about it.
  • Your corner signs are the advertising. Four big words and an arrow for the shoppers, and your business name small along the bottom. Every driver who passes that corner on Friday night sees it, and the ones who do not need a sale this year remember the name when they do. Clean, square, professional signs also tell a passing homeowner that this is a company, not a family with a card table.
  • The sale day itself, on the street. A sign at the driveway saying who ran this sale, and cards at the checkout table. Neighbours walk to sales. They come over, they see a staffed, tidy, busy driveway, and half of them have a garage that has bothered them for three years.
  • The estate companies' overflow. Full-service estate firms turn down houses that are too small for their minimum, and those calls have nowhere to go. Introduce yourself to every one within driving distance. Refer the big houses back to them and the arrangement will hold.

Then market to the shoppers for each sale, because a thin crowd is a bad settlement sheet. Post the sale in the local yard-sale and buy-and-sell groups midweek with eight or ten photographs of the actual good items — the tool chest, the bike, the furniture. The photograph of the tool chest is what brings the man who buys the whole workbench. Post again Friday evening when the signs go up.

Running the work

Weather runs your calendar. Look at the forecast on Wednesday, not Friday night, and have a rain date already written into the agreement so the conversation is a reminder rather than a negotiation. Wind is worse than rain for a driveway sale — it takes the signs, the tablecloths and the light things. Bring weights.

Stage a corridor, not a wall. Tables down both sides so shoppers walk through and see everything, with the furniture and the tools nearest the street doing the advertising. Everything off the ground — a sheet of plywood on sawhorses outsells a tarp on the lawn every time, because people will not bend over for things they have not decided they want yet.

Have an early-bird policy and put it on the sign. They will arrive at half past six whether you advertise eight or not, usually dealers, usually friendly. Decide in advance whether you sell to them and at what price, and tell the crew, because the worst version is selling the best thing on the driveway for five dollars before the tables are even up.

Take your signs down the same night. Every one, including the two you put up in the dark on Friday. Signs left on a corner for a fortnight are what turn a tolerant code officer into an unfriendly one, and yours have your business name printed on them. Photograph the corners as you clear them if you have a crew doing it.

Two people minimum, and one of them never leaves checkout. One at the table with the apron and the card reader, one on the floor answering questions and watching the small valuables. Cash stays on your body, not in a box on a table.

Hold price early, discount on a schedule. The first two hours are when the people who want the good things arrive; that is not the moment to take half. Then run the markdowns you agreed with the client — a posted discount hour, then a fill-a-bag price at the end. The point of the last hour is an empty garage, not a full one.

Be a good neighbour on somebody else's street. Do not block driveways, keep the crew's vehicles off the narrow side, and knock on the two houses either side beforehand to tell them what is happening. Sweep the driveway before you leave and photograph it empty — that photograph goes with the settlement sheet.

Settle within days, not weeks. Itemised sheet, the donation receipt, and the payment. Keep the client's money separate from your own so that is never a question of timing.

Using your site

Your admin is at /admin on your own address. Everything you change there is live within a minute or two.

  • Photos. Replace the hero with your own driveway shot as soon as you have run a sale — full tables, low morning light, taken from the street. The photo that ships is honest placeholder work and nothing more.
  • Pricing. The commission and the minimum are editable. The note under them is deliberately free of numbers, so it stays true whatever you set the percentage to — if you rewrite it, keep it that way rather than repeating a figure that will go stale.
  • Details and hours. Put the two things a client needs: when you do walkthroughs and which days you run sales. "Walkthroughs weekday evenings, sale days Friday and Saturday" answers the only scheduling question they have.
  • Reviews. Empty until you add them, deliberately — the site ships with nothing invented on it. Add real ones as you earn them, with permission. Ask on the Sunday, when the garage is empty and they are still delighted about it.
  • Marketing kit. Generates a social image and a printable flyer with a QR code pointing at your site. Leave the flyer at the storage place, the truck rental counter and the apartment office.
  • Leads. Every enquiry from your booking form is emailed to you and kept as a running list. These are time-sensitive — a client with a moving date will book whoever calls back first.

Your site also carries a Terms of Service and a Privacy Policy for your customers, generated from your business details. Use the Legal section of the admin to add your own clauses: the rain-date rule, the leftovers policy, and what you will not put on a table.

Licences and insurance

This is orientation, not advice, and the rules vary enormously by state and by city. Check what applies where you live before you take money.

  • Business registration. A registered business name and a local business licence are usually the first step, and usually quick.
  • Sale permits and per-address limits. This is the one that catches people in this trade specifically. Many cities and counties require a permit for a yard or garage sale, cap how many an address may hold in a year, and restrict the hours. Some HOAs ban them outright or allow only a community sale weekend. Check the city and the HOA for every address before the date is booked — not the morning of.
  • Sign ordinances. Signs in the public right-of-way, on utility poles, on medians and on the verge are regulated in most places, and any penalty generally lands on whoever's name is printed on the sign. Yours is. Find out what is allowed, and take them all down the same night regardless.
  • Sales tax. A household clearing its own garage and a company selling other people's goods for a fee every weekend are not the same thing to a revenue department. Whether you need a seller's permit, and whether you must collect tax on sales you make as an agent, varies by state. Ask an accountant before your first sale.
  • Holding other people's money. The proceeds in your apron belong to your client until you settle. Keep them in a separate account from your own operating money and pay out on the timeline you promised in writing.
  • Insurance. You are inviting strangers onto somebody else's driveway and moving their furniture around. Talk to a broker about general liability before your first paid sale, and be specific about what you actually do.
  • Workers' compensation. The moment you put a second person on a crew this becomes a question, and the rules differ sharply by state, including for casual weekend help.
  • Things that should not go on the table. Firearms and ammunition, prescription medication, recalled cribs, car seats and helmets, counterfeit goods. Set them aside at the walkthrough and tell the client why rather than discovering them at eight in the morning.

Do not claim on your site that you are licensed, insured or bonded until you are. It is a checkable claim, and in a town where your signs go up on four corners every weekend, somebody will check it.
