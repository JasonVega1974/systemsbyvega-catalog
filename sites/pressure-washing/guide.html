Your first week

Exterior cleaning is sold by the surface and won by the photograph. It is also the trade where a beginner does the most damage in the first month, because the machine will happily carve a driveway, strip a deck or drive water up under siding. Spend the first week learning what not to point it at.

  1. Learn the difference between washing and soft washing before you sell anything. Concrete and masonry take pressure. Siding, painted wood, roofs and screens do not — those get chemistry at garden-hose pressure and a rinse. Roof streaking is algae, and it dies from the mix, not from the machine. Selling a "pressure wash" on a roof is how a beginner ends up buying somebody shingles.
  2. Buy a surface cleaner with the machine, not later. A wand alone on flatwork leaves stripes across the whole driveway that no amount of extra passes hides. The rotating surface cleaner is what makes concrete come out one even colour, and it is the difference between finished work and obvious first work.
  3. Check your water supply, not just your machine. Gallons per minute is what cleans; pressure only concentrates it. Fill a five-gallon bucket from the customer's outside tap and time it. If the tap cannot keep up with the pump you need a buffer tank between them, or you will starve the pump and cook it.
  4. Wash your own driveway and stop halfway. Leave the dividing line down the middle, dirty on one side and clean on the other, and photograph it from standing height in flat light. That single frame sells more work than any finished driveway ever will, because it shows what was there.
  5. Write down what you refuse. No roofs you would need to walk. No chalky oxidised paint. No cedar shakes. No unsealed stone you have never worked on. Decide it now, in writing, because you will get asked in week two while standing in somebody's yard.
  6. Price and measure three real surfaces. Take a measuring wheel to three driveways on your street, write down the square footage, and work out what you would charge. This trade quotes in square feet — you need to be able to say a number without going quiet first.
  7. Put your own before-and-afters on the site. The site ships with an illustrated hero and stock work. Replace the photographs with things you have actually cleaned as soon as you have three.

What to charge

Your site ships with three flat packages — a driveway refresh, a full exterior wash, and a deck and fence restore — each with a stated size limit and a note explaining what happens past it. Change every figure to your own market; the shape is what matters.

Flat pricing with a stated boundary is the strongest offer in this trade. Customers hate hourly quotes for exterior work because they cannot judge how long it should take. A flat number with "up to this many square feet, and here is the rate beyond it" gives them certainty and gives you a way out of the giant driveway you did not expect.

Measure before you quote, and measure from a map when you can. Satellite imagery is accurate enough to get a driveway close, which means you can quote most flatwork over the phone and keep the day for washing. Confirm the number on arrival, before the machine comes off the trailer, not after.

Quote the condition, not just the area: two driveways of the same size can be an hour apart. Rust from a sprinkler, dried transmission fluid, red clay, black algae in the shade under trees, paint drips from a previous owner — each of those is a pretreatment and a second pass. Ask for a photograph before you name a price, and put stains on their own line so nobody thinks you invented an extra at the end.

Charge for what will not come clean, honestly, before you start. Oil that has soaked into unsealed concrete may lighten and never leave. Oxidised siding will come clean and still look chalky. Say so at the quote, in front of the surface, and you keep the job. Say it afterwards and you are arguing about a refund.

Minimum service charge, and stick to it. Driving out, unrolling, washing one small patio and rolling back up is most of a half day whatever the surface measured.

Finding customers

This work is bought on sight. Almost everything that works in this trade is a way of putting a clean surface in front of somebody who has a dirty one.

  • The street you are washing, while you are washing it. Put a sign in the yard, work with the trailer visible, and knock the nearest doors before you leave with a "we are on your street today" price. Driveways are visible from the road in a way that most trades' work is not — the neighbours have already compared theirs to the one you just did.
  • Local groups, with the half-done frame. Post the driveway with the line down the middle, not the finished one. Say the town. Do not post a before-and-after so far apart that people think it is a different house — same angle, same standing spot, same weather.
  • Commercial route work. Restaurant dumpster pads, drive-thru lanes, gas station forecourts, the sidewalk in front of a strip of shops. It is grubby, it is usually done at night or early morning, and it repeats on a schedule instead of once every few years. Walk in mid-morning and ask who handles the exterior.

Two more worth setting up once: estate agents who want a house washed before the listing photographs, and property managers with a portfolio of the same building type. Both are single relationships that turn into repeat work, and both care more about being able to book you at short notice than about being cheapest.

Seasonal rhythm matters here. Spring pollen and the run-up to summer parties are when people look at their house and decide it is filthy. Book the commercial work into the quiet months on purpose.

Running the work

Protect the plants before you mix anything. Soak the beds and the grass around the work with plain water first, keep them wet while you wash, and rinse again afterwards. Killed shrubs are the most expensive mistake in this trade and the only one the customer sees a week later.

Water goes in from above, not underneath. Never drive a stream up under lap siding, into a soffit vent, into a dryer vent, or at a window seal from below. Water pushed into a wall does not show up on the day; it shows up as a stain on somebody's ceiling in a month, and that is your bill.

Test in a corner, every single time. Behind the downpipe, under the deck stairs, the bottom corner of a fence panel. Give it thirty seconds at the tip and pressure you plan to use and look at what happened. Oxidised paint, soft cedar, old stucco and stamped concrete all fail differently, and the test patch is the only thing standing between you and a mark somebody has to look at for years.

Wood is a low-pressure job with chemistry doing the work. Clean it, then brighten it — the brightener is what pulls the grey out and puts the colour back and it is the step that makes a deck look restored rather than merely wet. Keep the pressure low enough not to raise the grain, because furred wood cannot be un-furred.

Photograph everything, in the same standing spot. Before you unroll a hose, walk the job with the phone and shoot every surface you were hired for and every bit of existing damage you were not. The damage photos are for you. The before-and-afters are for the customer, and texting them the pair the same day is what gets you the referral to the neighbour.

Walk the finished job with them. This trade has a hard, visible finish line, so use it. Walk the surface together, in daylight, and fix anything they point at while the machine is still on the trailer. That is also the moment to ask whether they want the deck doing next year.

Cold shuts you down. Freezing means water in the pump, the hoses and the wand, and split lines are a spring you spend replacing gear. Learn how to drain and store the machine properly the first autumn, not the second.

Using your site

Your admin is at /admin on your own address. Everything you change there is live within a minute or two.

  • Photos. This site's front page is a drag-to-clean canvas rather than a before/after slider, so there is no comparison widget to feed — your real before-and-afters do their work in the marketing kit, in your local group posts and texted straight to the customer. Upload zones are in the admin and your uploads save; keep the same-angle pairs together and named so you can find them.
  • Pricing. Three packages. Edit the label, the price and the note on each. The note line is where the boundary lives — the size the flat rate covers, and what happens past it. Do not leave that line vague; it is doing real work.
  • Details and service area. Name, phone, email and the towns you actually drive to. This niche has no hours strip on the page, so the place to say you work early mornings or after close for commercial pads is in the package notes or in the copy — and say it somewhere, because that one sentence is why a restaurant calls you instead of the next name on the list.
  • Reviews. Empty by design — the site ships with no invented reviews on it. Add real ones as you earn them, with the customer's permission.
  • Marketing kit. Generates a social image and a printable flyer with a QR code pointing at your site. Leave the flyer at the doors either side of every driveway you wash, the same day.
  • Leads. Every enquiry from the booking form is emailed to you and kept as a running list. Reply the same day — exterior work is shopped on speed of response as much as on price.

Your site also carries a Terms of Service and a Privacy Policy for your customers, generated from your business details. Add your own clauses in the Legal section of the admin — what you do not warrant, how far in advance a wash can be moved for weather, what happens to a job rained out halfway.

Licences and insurance

This is orientation, not advice, and the rules vary enormously by state and by city. Check what applies where you live before you take money.

  • Business registration. Most places want a registered business name and a local business licence before you trade. Usually quick and cheap, and the first thing to do.
  • Wash water and run-off. The rule that catches this trade. Water carrying detergent, oil or a roof mix is generally not allowed to go into a storm drain, and what you are required to do about it is set locally. Ask your city or the sewer utility what they expect — capture, a berm and a vacuum, diversion to landscaping — before your first commercial pad job, because that is where somebody will notice.
  • Liability insurance. Non-negotiable in a trade whose whole tool is capable of destroying somebody's property. Talk to a broker before your first paid job and describe roof work, soft washing and the chemicals honestly. A policy sold on a vague description is a policy that may not answer.
  • Chemical handling. The mixes used for algae are common but they are still chemicals with a label. Read the label, store them the way it says, transport them upright and secured, and keep the safety data sheets with the trailer.
  • Contractor licensing. Cleaning is usually not licensed work, but some states pull anything above a dollar threshold, or anything involving a roof or a coating, into contractor licensing. Ask your state board what side of the line exterior cleaning sits on before you advertise roof work.
  • Workers' compensation. The moment you put a second person on a ladder or a roof, this becomes a question, and the rules differ sharply by state including for casual help.
  • Sales tax. Whether a cleaning service is taxable varies by state. Ask an accountant once, early.

Do not claim on your site that you are licensed, insured or bonded until you are, and do not promise a stain will come out in writing before you have seen it. Both are checkable, and both are the kind of claim a customer keeps a screenshot of.
