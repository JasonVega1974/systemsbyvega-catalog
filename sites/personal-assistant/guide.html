Your first week

This is a trust business with no credential attached to it. Nobody licenses personal assistants, which means the only thing standing between you and a client's inbox, their house key and their credit card is whatever discipline you set up before you start. Set it up before you start.

  1. Write your own version of "Where I stop". Your site ships with a boundaries section — no professional advice, no custody of money, no care work, no heavy labour, no signing in your name, no open-ended access to systems. Read every line, decide whether it is your line, and rewrite it in your own words. This is not a legal disclaimer; it is the thing you will point at in month two when a client asks you to do something you should not. Have it before you need it.
  2. Decide the money rule and never bend it. No bank logins, no stored card numbers, no signing authority, no paying bills from an account you can reach. Purchases go on a card the client controls, or you front small amounts and invoice them back at cost with receipts attached. Write that on your site and in your engagement letter, and then never make an exception for a nice client, because the exception is the whole problem.
  3. Get the paper written. A one-page engagement letter: what you do, what you do not, your rates and minimum, how you invoice, notice to end it, and a confidentiality clause with teeth. Have an attorney in your state look at it once. You are about to be inside somebody's email; a handshake is not the right instrument.
  4. Build the handover questionnaire. This is the document that makes you good rather than merely willing. Who to call and who never to call. What is urgent and what only sounds urgent. How they want to be asked — a text, a batched list on Friday, never before nine. What you must never touch. Preferred vendors and the ones they have fallen out with. Spelling of names. You will fill this in once, in person, and use it every week after.
  5. Set up time tracking on day one, not month three. This work is billed by the hour and it evaporates: fifteen minutes on hold with a warranty department, ten minutes rewriting an email, a phone call from the car. If it is not written down as it happens it never gets billed, and worse, you cannot tell a client where their block went. Start the timer when you start, not when you remember.
  6. Run one half-day for somebody you already know, and write the summary. The written summary at the end of a block is the product — the thing they hold, the reason they believe the money did something. Practise writing one: what happened, what is still open, what needs a decision from them. Keep it to a page.
  7. Decide your capacity before you sell a retainer. A retainer is a promise of availability, and two of them badly shaped will eat the week you needed for hourly work. Work out how many committed hours you can actually hold, and hold that number.

What to charge

Your site ships with three tiers: an hourly rate with a two-hour minimum, a half-day block, and a monthly retainer with a set number of hours. Rewrite every figure for your own market, but keep the ladder consistent — the block should be a little cheaper per hour than hourly, and the retainer a little cheaper again. If the numbers do not step down, a client will notice, and being caught out on arithmetic in a trust business is expensive.

You are selling uninterrupted time, and that is what the discount is for. A four-hour block lets you route the errands sensibly and finish the desk work in one pass. An hour here and an hour there costs you the setup twice. Say that plainly when somebody asks why the block is better value; it is a real reason, not a sales line.

The two-hour minimum protects the work from becoming a favour. The ten-minute errand is not a job. It is a job plus a drive plus a context switch out of whatever you were doing.

Never mark up a purchase, not even by a little: postage, parking, the thing you bought at the hardware store — passed through at cost with the receipt attached to the summary. The moment a client spots a markup they have not agreed to, every other line on your invoice becomes a question, and this is a trade where they can check. If handling purchases has a real cost for you, charge for the time openly and leave the receipt untouched.

Bill the waiting. Sitting in a house for a delivery window or a technician is the service. It is on your own list of what you do. Charge for it at your normal rate and say so in advance so nobody is surprised.

Say what does not roll over, before the sale. If unused retainer hours expire at the end of the month, that sentence belongs in the conversation where they decide to buy, not in the email where they complain. Your site already states it; keep it stated.

Mileage. Decide once what is inside the block — a defined local area is the clean answer — and what is billed beyond it. Then never bill it ad hoc, because a surprise mileage line reads as nickel-and-diming even when it is fair.

Raising rates. New clients first, at your new number. Existing clients in writing, at a natural boundary, with a month's notice and no apology and no explanation longer than two sentences.

Finding customers

Nobody hires a stranger off an advertisement to read their email. This work is bought on a recommendation from somebody the client already trusts, which means your acquisition problem is really an introduction problem — and the introductions come from a narrow set of professionals who spend their days with busy people.

  • The professionals already inside the household. Property managers — particularly anybody looking after second homes for absent owners — realtors, wealth advisors, CPAs, estate and elder-law attorneys, interior designers and general contractors. Every one of them regularly hears a version of "I just do not have time to deal with this", and none of them wants to be the one who deals with it. Take one to coffee, tell them exactly what you do and exactly what you refuse to do, and give them the sentence you want them to repeat. The refusals are what make you sound safe to recommend.
  • Small business owners who are on a job all day. A contractor, a dentist, a small clinic, a shop with one owner. They are not looking for a personal assistant, they are drowning in invoices and unanswered calls, and the back-office block on your site is written for exactly them. Approach the business, not the person — it is an easier conversation and it is often a steadier client.
  • Your own previous working life. The people who watched you run a desk, a schedule or a back office already know you are competent, which is the part that takes a year to prove to a stranger. This is where most first clients come from, and there is no shame in it.

Your website is the confirmation step, not the front door. Somebody was given your name at a dinner and is looking you up before they call. What has to be on the page is a real face and a real name, the towns you cover, the fee structure written out rather than hidden behind "contact for pricing", and the boundaries section — because the question in their head is not "can she do this?" but "what is this person going to be like inside my life?"

Two habits worth keeping. Say no publicly to the work you do not do, on the site and in conversation; it is the thing that makes the yes believable. And keep a short list of the people you refer out to — a cleaner, a bookkeeper, a caregiver, a handyman. Being the person who knows who to call is half of what this job actually is, and referrals travel both directions.

Running the work

The written summary is the product. At the end of every block: what was done, what is still open, what needs a decision from them, receipts attached. Without it the client has paid for four hours and has nothing in their hand, and a service they cannot see is the first thing cut. With it, they can forward the open items to their spouse and stop carrying the list in their head. Keep it to one page and write it before you leave.

Order the day by the driving, not by the list. Errands arrive in the order they occurred to somebody. Re-sort them into one loop through town — returns and shipping in the same trip, the pharmacy on the way back, the pickup last so nothing sits in a hot car. That resequencing is a real skill and it is invisible to the client, which is fine.

Access is granted per tool, in their name, and revocable. Delegated access to a mailbox, a shared calendar, your own user account on their system. They keep the passwords. Never a shared login, never a saved card, never anything that cannot be switched off the day the relationship ends — you want to be able to prove exactly what you could and could not reach. And draft rather than send until they explicitly hand you the send, in writing, for named categories.

Keep a decision log, not just a to-do list: what you did, when, on whose instruction, and what it cost. Somebody will ask in September about a charge from March — usually not because they suspect anything, but because their accountant asked them. The assistant who can answer that in four minutes from a log is the assistant who keeps the job for years. The one reconstructing it from memory never fully clears the question.

Confidentiality is the whole job, and small towns are the test. You will know who is selling the house, who is not speaking to their brother, and what is in the calendar. The temptation is never the data; it is the story. Do not tell it. Not anonymised, not to your spouse, not as a funny thing that happened at work.

Tell them when something is not worth paying for. If an errand costs more in your time than the thing is worth, or a task would take them four minutes and you forty, say so. It costs you an hour and buys years, and it is the single clearest signal that you are working for them rather than for the clock.

Know the hand-off list and use it early. A question that has become tax advice goes to an accountant. Anything that has become care goes to a caregiver. Deep cleaning and hauling go to the people who do that. You can still book them, meet them at the door and check the work — that is service, and it stays firmly inside your lane.

Protect the shape of your week. The failure mode of a retainer is a client who slowly starts treating you as staff — messages at ten at night, an expectation of instant response, a list that never closes. Fix it early and gently, by pointing at the rhythm you both agreed to rather than by getting quietly resentful and then quitting.

Using your site

Your admin is at /admin on your own address. Everything you change there is live within a minute or two.

  • Photos. Three slots — a logo, the hero photograph and your own portrait. There is no gallery here and no before-and-after slider, on purpose: there is nothing to photograph in this trade, and a page of stock office pictures would say nothing. Replace the portrait with a real one of yourself and leave it at that. If you ever swap the hero, keep it to an object and a surface — a desk, a planner — and never a client's actual paperwork, even blurred.
  • Pricing. Three tiers, and each one has a note field that this site actually renders. Use it: the retainer note is where the notice period lives, and the highlighted tier's note is the line a reader remembers. Keep the pricing note underneath accurate — pass-through at cost, and what happens to unused hours.
  • The tier picker. The three cards are selectable, and whichever one a visitor picks travels into the enquiry form with them. That means a lead arrives already telling you whether they think they want an hour, a block or a retainer. Read it before you call — it tells you what kind of conversation they are expecting.
  • Services, the week, and "Where I stop". All three blocks are editable. Rewrite the services board and the five-day rail to the work you actually take, and delete anything you would not want to be asked for — everything listed is a promise. Do not soften the boundaries section to win a client; it is the reason a careful person feels safe hiring you.
  • Reviews. Empty until you add them, deliberately — nothing invented ships on this site. Add real ones only, with written permission, and expect some clients to decline entirely, because part of what they are buying is that you do not talk about them. First initial and a town is a normal compromise here.
  • Details and hours. Your hours line is a service promise. If it says messages are answered the same day, answer them the same day.
  • Marketing kit and leads. The kit generates a social image and a printable flyer with a QR code; the useful copies are the ones you hand to a property manager or an accountant, not the ones on a noticeboard. Every enquiry from the form is emailed to you and kept as a running list, so nothing is lost when an inbox gets busy — which would be an unfortunate thing to happen to this business in particular.

Your site also carries a Terms of Service and a Privacy Policy for your customers, generated from your business details. Use the Legal section of the admin to add the clauses this trade needs — confidentiality, how purchases and reimbursements work, your notice period, and a plain statement that you do not provide legal, tax, medical or financial advice.

Licences and insurance

Read this as orientation only, not as advice. There is no personal assistant licence, but several of the things clients will ask you to do sit next to activities that are regulated, and the rules differ by state. Take these to an attorney and a broker before you take a client.

  • Business registration and local licence. A registered business name and a local business licence are the normal starting point almost everywhere. Quick, cheap, first.
  • The professional advice line. Gathering paperwork, booking the appointment and sitting in to take notes is administration. Telling somebody what to do about their taxes, their contract, their medication or their investments is not, and law, tax practice, investment advice and insurance sales are all regulated activities with penalties attached. Stay on the administrative side of that line deliberately, and say so out loud to clients who keep asking what you would do.
  • Handling other people's money. This is the sharpest question in the trade. Taking custody of client funds, holding signing authority, being added to an account, or paying bills from somebody's money can pull you into territory covered by power-of-attorney rules, fiduciary duties, and in some states the regulation of daily money managers and similar roles. The safe structure — purchases on a card they control, small amounts fronted and invoiced back at cost — avoids most of it. If a client wants more than that, get advice before you agree, not after.
  • Insurance. Talk to a broker about liability cover, and separately about professional liability or errors-and-omissions cover, because the damage you are most likely to cause is administrative — a missed deadline, a booking made wrong, a payment chased to the wrong vendor. If you will hold keys or enter homes, ask about a bond and about cover for property in your care.
  • Driving. Running errands for a fee may fall outside a personal auto policy. Ask your insurer directly about business use before you are running a week of errands on the wrong cover.
  • Confidentiality and personal data. You will hold names, addresses, calendars, correspondence and account details. Some categories — health information and payment card details in particular — carry their own rules and their own consequences. Never store card numbers. Keep client files separated and locked, and put a written confidentiality clause in your engagement letter.
  • If you subcontract. The moment somebody else does the work for your clients, employee-versus-contractor classification and workers' compensation become live questions with state-specific answers.
  • Tax. Whether your services are taxable, and how to treat reimbursed pass-through purchases on your books, are both worth an accountant's hour early rather than a correction later.

Do not describe yourself on your site as licensed, certified, bonded or insured until each one is true and you hold the document. There is no licence for this work, so implying one is both false and unnecessary — what actually wins the client is the boundaries section and a reference, not a badge.
