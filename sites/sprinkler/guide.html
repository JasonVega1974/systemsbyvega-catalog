Your first week

Irrigation is a diagnostic trade before it is a digging trade. Most of what you sell is finding things: a valve nobody can locate, a wire somebody cut, a zone that never had the pressure to run the heads on it. Spend your first week on the measuring end, not on the machinery.

  1. Buy the diagnostic tools before the trencher. A pressure gauge that screws onto a hose bib, a five-gallon bucket and a stopwatch for timing flow, a wire tracker, a valve locator, a soil probe and a good shovel. You can rent a trencher for a day. You cannot rent knowing where the wire goes.
  2. Learn on your own system first. Find every valve box on your own property, run each zone, draw the map, and then disconnect something on purpose and trace it back with the tracker. Do that until locating a fault is boring rather than exciting.
  3. Call your water purveyor and ask about backflow. An irrigation line drawing off a public main brings backflow-prevention requirements, and districts differ on who may install an assembly, who may test it, and whether they keep their own list of approved testers. Get that answer from the district in your service area before you quote your first install, not during it.
  4. Set your service call and your hourly rate before the phone rings. Your site publishes a service-call figure and a note saying repairs bill from it and then hourly. Both of those numbers get asked for on the first call you take, and inventing them while somebody waits is how you end up working for less than you meant to.
  5. Practise the walk on three properties for free. Read static pressure at the bib, time the flow into the bucket, find the meter, the point of connection and the outlet the controller will live near, and sketch the turf, the beds and the hardscape. Do it three times before you charge anyone, so that on the fourth you are looking at the property rather than at your own notes.
  6. Learn the blow-out before autumn arrives. Know what pressure your compressor actually delivers and what the manufacturers of the heads and valves on that system allow, because too much air pressure through a dry zone destroys the very seals you are there to protect. Get this right in September, on a system you own, not in November on a customer's.
  7. Start the as-built habit on job one. Photograph every trench while it is still open, measure the mainline and the valve boxes off two fixed points that will not move, and hand the customer a copy of the drawing. That map is why a repair two years later takes an hour instead of a morning.

What to charge

Your site carries exactly two numbers: a service-call figure, and a note under it saying installs are quoted in writing after a free walk while repairs bill from the service call and then hourly. The install panel deliberately carries no price at all. That is the honest shape for this trade — an install price given before a pressure reading is a number you invented.

The service call is the number that gets you to the door. Say on the phone what it covers: the drive, the first hour of finding out what is actually wrong, and a straight answer about what it will take. Then hourly starts. Saying that before you get in the truck prevents the argument that otherwise happens on the driveway.

Quote installs from the meter, not from the acreage. Static pressure and available flow decide how many heads a zone can hold, which decides how many zones, which decides how many valves, how much wire and how many hours. Two identical-looking lawns on different pressure are two different jobs with two different prices, and the only way to know is to measure.

Zones are the unit, not square feet. Price the manifold, then price per zone and per head, then add what the ground is going to cost you: rock, roots, a driveway you have to sleeve under, an established bed you cannot trench through. Walk the line you intend to dig before you write the number.

Never quote a system you have not been able to find: a repair call on somebody else's install starts as a map problem and only then becomes a plumbing problem. Locate the valve boxes and run every zone before you promise a price. On an unlabelled system that hunt can be most of the visit — which is exactly what the service call is for, and why quoting a repair over the phone is guesswork.

Seasonal work is route work. Start-ups and blow-outs should be a flat per-visit price, booked in geographic order and run as a route. Hourly pricing on a twenty-minute job means you are billing for driving, and the driving is what actually costs you.

Stop and re-quote when a repair turns into a redesign. Your site says exactly that; say the same sentence out loud when it happens. Running the clock on a system that needs rebuilding is how a customer ends up paying twice and telling people about it.

Finding customers

Irrigation has a shape almost no other trade has: the same customer needs you three times a year, every year, at predictable times. Build for that first and everything else is a supplement.

  • The blow-out list is the business. Every system you winterise is a property whose whole year you can hold — start-up in spring, a tune in summer, a blow-out in autumn. Keep the list with the address, the zone count, the controller model, the backflow type and the date you last touched it, and work down it by phone rather than waiting for the phone to ring.
  • Call before the season calls you. Book blow-outs in September and start-ups in late winter. Everybody in your county rings the same week the first hard freeze is forecast, and by then you cannot serve them all. The operator who called in September already has the route.
  • Landscapers, sod installers and fence crews. They get asked about irrigation constantly and most of them do not want to do it. Fence and deck builders also cut laterals regularly and need somebody to call the same day. One landscaper who trusts you is a steady feed of installs, and the relationship costs you nothing but turning up when they ring.
  • The open trench is the sign. An install means a week of torn-open ground on a visible street with people walking past it. Put a sign at the kerb while you are in the trench, and knock on the neighbours when you backfill — they have watched the whole thing and half of them have a dry corner of their own.
  • Small commercial and property managers. Church lawns, storage yards, HOA common ground, the strip of turf outside a small business. These systems are usually old, usually unmaintained, and sit against a budget line somebody is already expecting to spend.
  • Answer the phone in spring. Between March and June the trade's real competitive advantage is picking up and giving somebody a date. Set the voicemail honestly if you cannot, and return calls the same evening.

Running the work

Measure before you design. Static pressure at the bib and flow timed into a bucket, written down, on every property. Everything downstream — head count per zone, nozzle choice, whether that back paddock can even be added — comes off those two numbers, and no amount of experience replaces reading them.

Call for utility locates before every trench, including the small ones: marking buried gas, power and communications lines before digging is a legal requirement, and the notice period and the exact process are set where you live. Find out yours, follow it every time, and wait the full period even when the job is one trench across a front lawn. This is the one shortcut in this trade that can kill somebody.

Group zones by the pressure they can actually hold. Rotors and spray heads never share a valve. A shaded strip does not share a valve with a south-facing lawn, because they do not want the same water. Beds go on drip, on their own valve, with a filter and a regulator ahead of the line. Never add a head to a zone just to save a valve — that is the decision that produces the dry corner you get called back for.

Nozzle to the arc. A quarter-circle head running the same nozzle as a full-circle head next to it puts far more water on that corner in the same run time. Match the nozzles to the arcs so every head in the zone is laying down water at the same rate, or the schedule can never be right for the whole zone at once.

Overlap is the design. Spray has to reach the next head, not merely reach the gap between them. Run each zone with the customer standing beside you, adjust the arcs, swap nozzles until the throws meet, and only then set the schedule.

Wire discipline. Tag both ends of every valve wire, use waterproof connectors rather than tape, and pull a spare wire or two to each box while the trench is open. Tape and optimism is the reason somebody is paying you to trace a fault on a system that is only five years old.

Valve boxes you can open from the surface. Set them to finish grade, keep them out of the mow line where you can, and never bury a manifold. A buried manifold is a morning of digging every time anything goes wrong, and it will be you doing the digging.

Charge the mainline slowly in spring. Open the valve gradually so the surge does not blow a fitting you then have to find and dig up. Then walk every zone looking for what the winter moved before you tell the customer it is running.

Write the schedule where the next person will find it. Programme the controller with the owner watching, then write the seasonal run times inside the door of the box. The customer forgets; the box does not.

Using your site

Your admin is at /admin on your own address. Everything you change there is live within a minute or two.

  • Photos. Five zones: your logo, the hero at the top, a before and an after for the comparison slider, and your own portrait. Shoot spray in low morning or evening light — water reads as water when the sun is low and disappears at midday.
  • The before-and-after slider. The pair your site ships with is two different Payette Valley lawns, and the page says so in plain words. If you replace them, replace both, shoot from the same height in the same light, and only remove that disclosure if it genuinely is one property before and after your own work. Nothing on this site pretends, and that is worth more than a dramatic slider.
  • Pricing. Two fields: the service-call figure and the sentence beneath it about how installs and repairs are billed. Keep the sentence short — the box has a length limit, and the sentence is doing one job, which is preventing a surprise on the driveway.
  • Job details. What is included and what is not. This is the strongest section on your page and it is worth rewriting carefully — especially the "not included" list. Meter and tap fees, the district's own backflow test, sod repair beyond backfilling, lawn care, and electrical work beyond plugging the controller in are the five that otherwise become arguments.
  • Details and hours. Owner name, the paragraph about who walks the property, and your hours, which appear in the footer. Put the real ones in, including whether Saturday walks happen by appointment.
  • Reviews. They ship empty by design — there are no invented reviews on this site. Add real ones as you earn them, with the customer's permission.
  • Social links. Ship empty, and that whole line renders as nothing until you add one. Leave it empty rather than linking an account you do not use.
  • Marketing kit. Generates a social image and a printable flyer with a QR code pointing at your site. Print the flyer for the doors you knock when you backfill a trench.
  • Leads. Every enquiry is emailed to you and kept as a running list. The scope builder on your page writes a plain-language summary of the property into the request — read that before you call back, because it usually tells you what to put on the truck.

Your site also carries a Terms of Service and a Privacy Policy for your customers, generated from your business details. The Legal section of the admin is where a deposit rule, your winterisation booking window and a plain note that the annual backflow test belongs to the purveyor's own tester should go.

Licences and insurance

This is orientation, not advice. Irrigation sits across three different rule-makers — the state, the local building authority, and your water purveyor — and they do not agree with each other from county to county. Check all three where you work before you take money.

  • Business registration and trade licensing. A registered business name and a local business licence are the usual first step. Beyond that, states vary widely: some license irrigation contractors specifically, some fold the work under plumbing or landscape contracting, and some do not license it at all. Ask your state's contractor board what installing an in-ground system requires of you.
  • Backflow prevention. Connecting irrigation to a potable water supply brings backflow requirements from the plumbing code and from the water purveyor, and the person permitted to install an assembly is frequently not the person permitted to test it. Your district is the authority on both. Find out before you quote, and be clear with customers about which part is yours and which is theirs.
  • Utility locates. Marking buried services before you dig is a legal obligation with its own process and waiting period wherever you are. Learn yours, use it on every job, and keep the ticket.
  • Permits and inspection. Some jurisdictions require a permit and an inspection for a new irrigation connection or for the backflow assembly. Ask your local building department rather than assuming a sprinkler system is unregulated.
  • Water restrictions. Many districts limit watering days or hours, seasonally or permanently. Know the rules where you work, because the schedule you programme into a customer's controller has to be a legal one.
  • Insurance. You dig through other people's ground and other people's utilities. Talk to a broker about general liability before your first paid job, and be specific about trenching — a policy bought on a vague description of "landscape work" may not answer when you need it. Commercial auto for the truck and trailer is a separate question again.
  • Workers' compensation and sales tax. Workers' comp becomes a question the moment you hire, including for seasonal help, and the rules differ sharply by state. Whether parts and labour are taxed the same way also varies. Ask an accountant once, early.

Do not put "licensed", "certified" or "insured" on your site until each one is true and current. In this trade the backflow claim is the dangerous one — districts keep public lists of who is certified to test, and being on your own site but not on their list is a very short conversation.
