Your first week

A courier business can be started in an afternoon with a car you already own, and that is exactly why people get hurt doing it. Two things have to be right before the first paid run: the insurance, and the zone map the whole site prices from. Do them in this order.

  1. Phone a commercial insurance broker before you carry anything for money. A personal auto policy generally excludes carrying goods for hire, and the exclusion is found at the worst possible moment, after an accident, when a claim is denied and the vehicle and the cargo are both your problem. Say the words "delivering goods for payment" to the broker and ask what you need. This is not step one by accident.
  2. Drive your zone boundaries before you trust them. Your site prices from a seven-zone map. Zones are not city limits — they are drive time. Put a boundary where the traffic changes, where the bridge is, where the valley genuinely takes another twenty minutes. Do the drives at eight in the morning and at half past four, because those are the runs people will actually book.
  3. Set the minimum so the shortest possible run is worth the ignition. Remember that a run is three drives, not one: to the pickup, to the drop, and back toward wherever you want to be. A minimum that only covers the middle leg is a minimum that loses you the day.
  4. Build the proof-of-delivery habit on day one. A photograph of where it landed, the name of who took it, texted the moment it happens. Your site sells that as the product because it is the product; anybody can drive a parcel across town. Do it on the first run and every run after, including the ones for friends.
  5. Kit the vehicle. A folding hand truck, moving blankets, ratchet straps, a couple of lidded totes, document envelopes that seal, a phone mount, a charger that works, and a cool box if you intend to take clinic or lab work. Everything strapped — a load that slides in a hard stop is a claim.
  6. Write the will-not-carry list and keep it by the phone. Hazardous goods, anything alive, cash and high-value items that belong in a secured service, anything you cannot lift alone, anything illegal. Your site's FAQ already says this. Being able to say no quickly and without apology is a professional skill in this trade.
  7. Get one account, not one customer. Walk into ten counters in your first week with a card and the zone map: law and title offices, print shops, medical and dental labs, parts counters. You are looking for the standing route, not the one-off, and that only happens face to face.

What to charge

Your site has no price list, because the quoter is the price list. Pickup zone, drop zone, size and speed produce a number in front of the customer, and the admin lets you set the floor and the note that sits with it. What follows is how to think about the shape of it.

Price by zone, never by mileage. A mileage number invites an argument about which route you took and what the traffic was like. A zone map is a fixed answer that both of you can see before the run starts, and it is the reason the customer trusts the quote. It is also the reason you can quote in ten seconds on the phone.

The speed multiplier is not a surcharge, it is dedication. A scheduled run rides along with other work. A rush is a direct drive that takes your whole slot and displaces everything else in it. Price it as what it costs you — the other runs you cannot take — and explain it in exactly those words, because it is a reason rather than an excuse.

Size covers handling, not just weight. An envelope goes on the seat. A skid needs a ramp, a strap, a hand truck and somebody at the other end who is expecting it. That is the difference the size band is describing, and it is worth saying so when somebody asks why a light but awkward item is not the cheapest tier.

Charge for waiting, and decide the rule before you need it: the run that destroys a day is not a long one, it is the twenty-minute pickup where the paperwork "will be ready in five minutes" three times in a row. Set a free wait, set what happens after it, and put it in writing with your account customers. Nobody argues about a waiting charge they were told about in advance. Everybody argues about one they were not.

Decide your after-hours and weekend position deliberately. Either you do not do it, or it is priced as the interruption it is. What you must not do is take Saturday runs at weekday prices to be helpful, because that is the day you will be asked for from then on.

When fuel moves, revisit the zone bands rather than bolting a surcharge onto every invoice. Your page promises no fuel surprises. Keep that promise and adjust the map.

Finding customers

This one is different from every other trade in the catalogue: your customer is almost never a household. It is a business with something that has to be across town before the end of the day, and you find them by walking through their front door.

  • The counters, in person, in the morning. Law and title offices with filings and originals. Print and sign shops with finished jobs that make them look fast. Auto parts counters where one gasket is holding up a lift bay. Dental and medical labs moving work between locations. Take the zone map, take cards, ask for the person who books couriers, and go back a second time. This trade is won on the fourth visit, not the first.
  • The office manager, not the owner. The decision to try a new courier is almost always made by whoever is standing at the front desk when something goes wrong. Learn her name, spell it right, and be the number in her drawer. The owner will never know you exist and it will not matter.
  • The other couriers. Every established courier is over capacity at two in the afternoon on the wrong day, and the alternative to handing you the run is telling their customer no. Introduce yourself as overflow rather than as competition. This is the fastest way to get paid work in the first month, and it costs nothing but a phone call.

What to chase, above everything: the standing route. A daily bank run, a transfer between two branches every morning, a Friday collection. It fills a known slot on a known road, it is booked once instead of every time, and it makes the rest of the week plannable. Ask every account you win whether there is something they already do at the same time every day.

Do not spend money on general consumer advertising for this. Nobody searches for a courier the way they search for a plumber, and the households who do want one parcel moved are the hardest work in this trade to make worth doing. Your site is the credibility somebody checks after you have walked into their office, and the quoter is what convinces them you are not going to invent a price later.

Running the work

Never accept a run you cannot confirm the pickup window on. "Sometime this afternoon" from the sender turns into a promise you made to the receiver. Get the window, get a name and a phone number at both ends, and get the address confirmed as a place a vehicle can actually stop.

Batch by geography, protect the rushes. Scheduled work gets sequenced into a loop; a rush breaks the loop by design, which is what it was paid for. Keep a realistic sense of how many scheduled stops you can hold and still say yes to a direct drive, because saying yes to the direct drive is where your reputation is made.

Sealed, signed and separated for documents. Originals, filings and anything with a deadline on it travel in a sealed envelope, in the cabin, not in the boot with the parcels. If the sender wants a chain of custody, give them one and keep your copy.

Decide the failed-delivery rule before it happens, not in the doorway: nobody home, the office shut early, a gate code that does not work. The rule is simple and it should never bend — you do not leave it unless the sender has told you in writing that you may, and you phone the sender before you do anything else, from the kerb, while you are still there. A return trip is a second run at a second price, and the sender needs to hear that from you at the time rather than see it on an invoice.

The two texts are the whole service. One when it is in your hand, one with the photograph when it is at the door. Send them without being asked and send them every time, and you become the courier nobody has to chase. That is the entire pitch your owner page is built on.

Your vehicle is the business, so service it early. A breakdown is not an inconvenience, it is a missed run for an account that trusted you, and accounts are won slowly and lost in one afternoon. Book the service before it is due, keep the spare inflated, and know who you would call to cover a run you cannot make.

Keep the mileage and the fuel logged from the first day. You will need it, and reconstructing a year of driving in April is miserable.

Invoice accounts monthly and on time. Your site already offers a single monthly invoice for recurring routes. An invoice that lands on the same day every month gets paid; one that arrives whenever you got round to it teaches people that you are casual about money.

Using your site

Your admin is at /admin on your own address. Everything you change there is live within a minute or two.

  • Photos. Two slots that matter, the hero and the owner photo. Replace the hero with your own vehicle, clean, in a real loading spot in a town people recognise. A courier's photograph is doing one job: proving there is a real driver and a real vehicle behind the phone number.
  • Pricing. There is no price list to fill in, because the quoter is the pricing. The admin gives you the floor price and the note that sits with it. That note is where you put the thing customers ask you most — how waiting works, what happens outside the map, what is not carried.
  • The zone quoter. It is the most important thing on your site and the number it shows is a promise: the customer expects it on the invoice. Before you publish, price a handful of real runs by hand and check the quoter agrees with you, including the awkward ones — same zone, the far edge, and the heaviest size on the slowest speed.
  • Details and hours. Put the real cut-off in. A same-day promise with a booking deadline attached is honest and useful; the same promise with no deadline is a complaint waiting to happen. Say which towns the map covers and what "outlying" means.
  • Reviews. Empty until you add them, deliberately. The site ships with no invented reviews. Add real ones as you earn them, with permission — and in this trade ask the office manager, because she is the one who can describe the difference you made to her afternoon.
  • Marketing kit. Generates a social image and a printable flyer with a QR code pointing at your site. The flyer is the thing you leave on a front desk after the introduction, with the zone map explained on the back if you add one.
  • Leads. Every enquiry from the booking form is emailed to you and kept as a running list. Reply fast — courier enquiries are urgent by definition, and the first person to answer usually gets the run.

Your site also carries a Terms of Service and a Privacy Policy for your customers, generated from your business details. The Legal section of the admin matters more here than in most trades: your limit of liability for goods in transit, the will-not-carry list, your waiting and failed-delivery rules, and how a recurring account is cancelled. Get those read by somebody qualified before you rely on them.

Licences and insurance

This is orientation, not advice. Courier work is often unlicensed as a trade, which makes it easy to believe there is nothing to check. The insurance questions are the serious ones, and they are the ones people get wrong. Check what applies where you are before you carry anything for money.

  • Your personal auto policy almost certainly does not cover this. Carrying goods for hire is a standard exclusion, and delivery use is generally excluded even on a policy that allows other business driving. Assume you are not covered until a broker tells you in writing that you are. This is the single most important line in this guide.
  • Commercial auto is the replacement, not an add-on. A commercial policy written for delivery use is what answers when something happens on a run. Tell the broker what you actually carry, how far, how often, and whether anyone else drives the vehicle.
  • Cargo cover is a separate policy again. Liability protects other people. It does not pay for the customer's parcel, documents or freight that were in your vehicle. Ask specifically about cover for goods in transit and about what the limit is per item, because "insured" without that number means very little to a law office handing you an original.
  • Business registration and local licensing. A registered name and a local business licence is usually the whole trade requirement for a small courier, but some cities do license delivery or vehicle-for-hire operations. Ask before you assume.
  • Vehicle size and crossing state lines change the picture. Federal motor carrier rules — registration numbers, driver qualification, hours of service — turn on things like vehicle weight, what is being carried and whether you operate across state lines. A car in one valley is usually well below all of it. If you buy a bigger van or start running out of state, find out where those thresholds sit before you do it, not after.
  • Specialised cargo brings its own rules. Medical specimens, pharmacy work, legal originals and anything temperature-controlled all carry handling, privacy and chain-of-custody obligations that come from the customer's regulator rather than yours. Do not take that work until you have asked the customer what they are required to require of you.
  • If you add a driver, the classification question is real. Whether somebody driving for you is an employee or a contractor is decided by law and by how the work actually operates, not by what the paperwork calls it, and the rules differ sharply by state. Getting it wrong is expensive in back taxes and penalties. Ask an accountant or an employment lawyer before the second vehicle, not after.
  • Workers' compensation. Attached to the same question, and again state by state.

Do not put "insured and bonded" on your site until both are true and you know what the limits are. In this trade the claim is not decoration — it is the thing a business customer will ask you to evidence before they hand you anything that matters.
