Your first week

Mobile food is a permitted trade. You do not open and then catch up on the paperwork — the permit is a gate you pass before you sell one plate, and in most places the truck itself is inspected before it is permitted. That reality sets the order of this list. Work it in this order.

  1. Call the health authority before you spend anything. Ask your county or city health department what a new mobile food unit has to file. The categories you are likely to hear about are a mobile food unit permit, a plan review or inspection of the truck, a food-handler or food-manager certification, and a commissary agreement. Which apply, in what order, and how long they take is local — ask them, not a forum. Their answer changes what truck you buy.
  2. Find the commissary before you sign for a truck. A licensed commercial kitchen for prep, fresh-water fill, grey-water dump and often overnight parking is commonly part of what a mobile unit must show. Kitchen space in a small town can be scarce. Find out what is actually available near you, get the agreement in writing, and only then commit to a vehicle.
  3. Learn your fire on your own smoker. Every pit runs differently. Light it, hold it through a full overnight cook, take the meat off, rest it, and write down every time and temperature as you go. Note what wind and cold do to your fuel burn. Fire management is the whole trade, and you cannot learn it at four in the morning on your first service day.
  4. Run a yield test and keep the number. Weigh the packer raw, weigh it after trim, weigh what you actually slice. Meat loses weight in the trim and again in the cook, and until you know how many plates come off one brisket you cannot order and you cannot price. Do the same for pork and for ribs. Write it inside the truck.
  5. Cut the menu to what one window can serve. Your site ships four boards — plates, sandwiches, sides and drinks. The constraint is not the smoker, it is the window and the pair of hands behind it. Start with fewer items than you want to sell and add back once the line moves fast.
  6. Confirm your spots in writing. For each one: the days, the hours, whether the host takes a percentage or a flat fee or nothing, where you plug in or whether you run the generator, and where trash and grease go. Then put those spots into the weekly schedule on your site so people can find you.
  7. Open somewhere forgiving. Pick the quietest of your spots for the first service and cook less than you think you will sell. Get the line moving before you show up anywhere with a queue. Photograph the truck open in daylight with food on the board — that is your hero image, and the clean first-day shot only happens once.

What to charge

You have two price lists and they behave differently. The menu board is what the window sells, item by item, to whoever walks up. The site's three catering packages are per person, quoted ahead against a known head count, with the larger package costing less a head. Both are yours to rewrite — they are a starting shape, not a recommendation.

Price from your own yield. You know what a packer costs you and how many plates it slices into, because you ran the test in week one. That is the number a plate has to carry. Pricing off the truck across town is guessing at their trim, their supplier and their cook, none of which you can see.

Selling out is not proof the price is right: it only proves you cooked less than the day wanted. A plate has to carry the meat, the wood, the propane, the commissary, the permit, the fuel to the spot, the ice and the paper goods, plus the hours you spent overnight with the truck closed. If it only carries the meat, a busy day costs you more than a slow one.

Catering needs a floor. A per-person number only works above a minimum head count — below that, quote the job flat, because the drive, the setup and the cook barely shrink for a small party. Set a cutoff date for the final count, put it in writing, and cook and bill to that number. Take a deposit to hold a Saturday; you are taking that date off the market.

Travel and staffing are lines, not goodwill. Mileage past your metro, a staffed serving line for a set number of hours, a second server, a second drive back for pickup. Write them on the quote so nothing is a surprise at the end.

Change the board between seasons when your costs move, print a clean new one, and do not apologise for it in the caption.

Finding customers

You are not generating leads. You are being physically in the right place at the right hour, and then being findable enough that people come back on purpose. Nearly all of this is placement.

  • The standing spot. A brewery or taproom with no kitchen is the best partner in this trade: they sell drinks, you sell food, and neither of you is taking the other's money. Walk in mid-afternoon between services with a menu and a photo of the truck and ask for the owner — never during their rush. Agree the terms in writing before you post the date.
  • Match the spot to the hour. Business parks, industrial yards and hospital campuses want a fast lunch in a short window. Taprooms want evenings and weekends. Markets want mornings. Build the week so the truck is where people already are at that hour, instead of hoping they drive to you.
  • Events and markets, applied for early. Fairs, festivals and farmers markets take applications months ahead and will ask for your permit, a certificate of insurance often naming them, and your fire paperwork. Read the whole application before you commit the date, including the fee and whether they take a percentage. A bad Saturday at a big event is a Saturday you cannot get back.
  • A following that knows where you are. People need to know before they leave the house. The weekly schedule on your site is the record; social posts are the reminder. Post the night before, post when the window opens, and post the moment you sell out. "Sold out at 1:15, thank you" is the most useful thing you will write, because it teaches people to come earlier.
  • Catering comes out of the window. The person booking a company lunch or a wedding almost always ate off your truck first. Keep cards at the window, say out loud that you cater, and hand a card to anyone who tells you the brisket is good.

Never no-show a posted spot. If the smoker fails or the truck will not start, tell the host first, then post it loudly and early. A truck that is not where it said it would be is the one thing regulars do not forgive, and that story travels faster than the good one.

Running the work

Build the week backwards from the window. A long brisket cook means lighting the smoker the night before. Work back from the hour you open: prep, load, light, hold, drive, park, serve, break down, dump, refill, clean. Write the week as times, not as intentions.

The inspection is the routine, not the exam: inspectors turn up when they turn up, and everything they look at is something you should be doing every service anyway. Temperatures logged, hand sink stocked with soap and towels, sanitiser made and dated, thermometer calibrated, food covered and off the floor, hair covered. Set the truck up so an unannounced visit is a normal Tuesday.

Cook counts are a record, not a mood. Decide before you light how many plates you are cooking for. Keep a tally of what sold at each spot each week and cook to that history. Under-cook and you close with people still in line; over-cook and you are eating it yourself.

Water, power and waste. Fresh tank full and grey tank empty before you park, not after. Nothing goes on the ground — not grease, not grey water, not melted ice. That is the fastest way to lose a spot, and somebody always sees.

Two people at the window. One cooking and plating, one taking money and talking. A single operator at a lunch rush loses more to a slow line than the second pair of hands costs, and the line is what people remember about you.

Assume the card reader will fail. Lunch pods and event fields are where signal dies. Carry a reader that queues transactions offline, keep a float of change, and know your fallback before twenty people are waiting.

Put the truck's own maintenance on a calendar. Fire suppression service, extinguisher tags, propane hoses and regulators, generator oil, tyres, brakes. A truck that will not start is a service you cannot run and a host you disappointed, and both of those cost you more than the part did.

Clean at the commissary, not the next morning. Cold grease is far worse than hot grease, and a truck you break down tired is a truck you set up dirty.

Using your site

Your admin is at /admin on your own address. Everything you change there is live within a minute or two.

  • Photos. One zone, the hero at the top. Shoot sliced brisket in daylight so the bark and the smoke ring read — a flash flattens both. The truck with the window open and somebody being handed a tray works too. Replace the stock photo as soon as you have one of your own.
  • Pricing. The three catering packages: the name of each, the per-person figure and the note under it are yours to rewrite. Say what is included in each, so the difference between them is on the page and not in your head.
  • Details. Business name, tagline, town, phone and the inquiry email that catering requests land in all live in Business. Your hours in this trade are the weekly schedule panel on the front page, and that is what people check before they drive.
  • The schedule and the menu board. These are built into your site rather than edited in the admin today — send us the changes when spots or prices move. Keep the schedule accurate above everything else on the page; it is the one part people act on.
  • Reviews. This design does not carry a reviews section, and the admin will tell you so. Wherever you do quote a customer — social, a flyer, the side of the truck — use only words a real person actually said, with their permission. Nothing invented, ever.
  • Marketing kit. Generates a social image and a printable flyer with a QR code pointing at your site. Print the flyer for the window, for the taproom noticeboard, and to hand to anyone who asks about catering.
  • Leads. Every catering enquiry from your form is emailed to you and kept as a running list, so a booking is not lost when the inbox gets busy.

Your site also carries a Terms of Service and a Privacy Policy for your customers, generated from your business details. Add your own clauses in the Legal section of the admin — the deposit, the final head-count cutoff and your cancellation window are the three worth writing down.

Licences and insurance

This is orientation, not advice. Mobile food is among the most heavily regulated small businesses there is, and the rules differ by state, by county, and sometimes by the individual city you park in. Check what applies where you operate before you take money, and take the answers from the authority itself.

  • Health permits. The categories to ask about are a mobile food unit permit, a plan review or inspection of the truck and its equipment, a food-handler or food-manager certification for you and your staff, a commissary agreement, and routine inspection once you are open. This is a gate, not paperwork you file later: in most places you may not legally serve a plate until it clears. Ask your local health authority what applies to you.
  • Fire. A cooking line usually answers to the fire marshal as well as to the health department — suppression over the equipment, extinguishers, propane storage and hose inspection. Separate authority, separate paperwork, and events will ask to see it.
  • Where you may park. Vending on a street, in a lot, or on private property is a zoning and permitting question quite separate from health, and some cities want their own vending permit per location. Ask before you promise a host a weekly slot.
  • The vehicle. Registration, and the class of driving licence the loaded truck's weight requires, are worth confirming rather than assuming. Commercial auto cover is not the same policy as the one on your pickup.
  • Insurance. Talk to a broker about general liability and product liability before your first service, and be honest with them about live fire and a generator. Events routinely require a certificate naming them, so the policy has to exist before you can even apply to some of them.
  • Business registration and sales tax. A registered business name and a local business licence are usually the first step. Prepared food is often taxed differently from other goods, and catering can be treated differently again. Ask an accountant once, early, rather than guessing for a year.
  • Workers' compensation. The moment you put a second person in the truck this becomes a question, and the rules differ sharply by state, including for casual weekend help.

Do not put "licensed", "permitted" or "insured" on your site, your truck or your social accounts until each one is true. A health permit is a public record with a number on it, and in this trade people do check.
