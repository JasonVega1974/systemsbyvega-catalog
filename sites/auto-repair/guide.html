Your first week

This is a shop with a door, a lift and an address. That address is the business: it is where the work happens, where the customers come, and what people search for. Your first week is spent making the bay able to take a car and the paperwork able to take a customer.

  1. Get the lift signed off before it lifts anybody's car. Anchors, slab thickness, arm restraints, the safety locks, and an inspection by somebody qualified to do it. Then write the date on the column and put the next one in your calendar. A lift is the one piece of equipment in the building that can kill somebody, and it is also the one most likely to have been left by the previous tenant.
  2. Set your labour rate and pick your time guide. Flat-rate book hours or your own measured times, one or the other, decided before your first quote rather than argued out per car. Everything downstream of this, including the ranges on your site, is built from it.
  3. Open two or three parts accounts and learn their delivery times. The counter that runs a truck at 8am and 1pm is worth more than the one that is a dollar cheaper. A car on your lift waiting for a part is your bay standing still.
  4. Buy diagnostic capability, not a code reader. A bidirectional scan tool and a subscription to real service information: wiring diagrams, service bulletins, torque and procedure. Guessing from a code is what gives the trade its reputation, and your site's whole pitch is the opposite of that.
  5. Set up the waste accounts before the first oil change. Used oil, oil filters, coolant, batteries, tyres. Every one has somewhere it must go and somebody licensed to take it. Sort this in the quiet week, not on the day the drum is full.
  6. Check the site's published ranges against your own numbers. Your home page ships with six job ranges and a diagnostic fee. Work each one out on your own labour time and your own parts cost, then edit them in the admin. They go on the internet with your name on them, so they need to be honest for the worst common version of that job, not the easiest.
  7. Run three cars through the whole process. Intake, road test, written estimate, approval, repair, photograph of the old part, hand it back. Do it on your own vehicles or a friend's before a stranger's. The process is the product here, and it needs to be smooth before anybody is watching.

What to charge

Your site publishes six job ranges and a diagnostic fee applied to the repair. That is unusual for this trade and it is the whole positioning: somebody can see roughly what a brake job costs before they phone. Change every figure to your own market in the admin. They are a starting shape, not a recommendation.

A published range binds you, so write it for the hard version. The range has to survive the seized caliper bolt and the rusted rotor, not just the clean three-year-old sedan. If your low number only works on the easy car, you will spend every Tuesday explaining why the actual number is higher, and the page that was supposed to build trust will be spending it instead.

Defend the diagnostic fee. A free diagnosis makes you the free second opinion for every shop in town and for every customer who intends to fix it themselves in their own driveway. Charging for the hour and applying it to the repair, the way the site already does, is the fair version: the person who lets you do the work pays nothing for the diagnosis, and the person who does not pays for your time.

One written number, and work stops if it changes: the estimate you gave is the estimate you honour. If the car opens up and the job is genuinely different, tools down and the phone rings, and nothing else happens until the customer approves the new number. A shop that quietly adds to the number at the counter has traded a customer for one invoice, and in a small town they tell everybody.

Do not compete on the oil change. It is the cheapest thing on your board, everybody sells it, and cutting it further does not win a customer who was going to go to the quick-lube anyway. Sell it as the visit where somebody actually looks at the car, and show them what you found.

Parts margin and labour are two different decisions. Know what each one is doing for you, and give the customer an honest read on new versus good used parts on an older vehicle. Your site already promises that conversation. Have it the same way every time.

Finding customers

You are a fixed location, so most of your work arrives through a map and a neighbourhood, not through advertising. Three channels do the heavy lifting.

  • Your Google Business Profile, treated as a real job. "Mechanic near me" is how this trade gets found, and the map result is what people pick from. Real hours, kept accurate at holidays. Photographs of your actual bay and your actual sign, not stock images. Answer every review, including the unhappy one, calmly and in public. This is the single highest-return hour a shop owner spends in a week, and it is not something a mobile operator can do the same way.
  • Small fleets and the businesses that run trucks. The plumbing outfit with five vans, the landscaper's trailers, the church bus, the farm pickups. They need somebody who will take the vehicle today and give it back tomorrow, they pay on invoice, and they fill the quiet middle of the week. Go and see them; do not wait to be found.
  • The shops that do not do what you do. The transmission specialist, the body shop, the tyre place, the dealer that will not touch a fifteen-year-old truck. Every one of them turns work away every week. Introduce yourself, be specific about what you want sent over, and send work back. Reciprocal referral is how a small shop fills its Tuesdays.

Pre-purchase inspections deserve their own mention. Somebody buying a used car is meeting you at the calmest possible moment, with no emergency and no anger, and a reasonable share of them become the customer who brings that car back for everything else. Say yes to those bookings even when the bay is busy.

Your site's sound tool is a reason for somebody to land on the page and stay. Point people at it when they describe a noise on the phone, and let it do what it says it does: give them the usual suspects so they are not walking in blind. It is not a diagnosis and the page says so. Keep saying so.

Running the work

The lift is the constraint. Everything about how you schedule follows from that. Stage the parts before the car goes up, not after, and keep one slot a day open for the tow-in and the car that will not start in somebody's driveway. A day with no gap in it becomes a day where every customer is told a different lie about when their car will be ready.

Road test before and after, every time. Before, because the noise the customer describes and the noise the car makes are frequently two different noises. After, because the only person who should discover that the job is not finished is you. Take the customer along on the first one when they are willing; it is the fastest diagnosis you will ever do.

Photograph the failure while it is still on the car. The cracked boot, the worn pad against a new one, the play in the joint. Send it to their phone with the estimate. This is what turns "they said I needed it" into "I saw it", and it is the single habit that separates a shop people trust from a shop people suspect.

The comeback is your reputation, not your inconvenience: a car that returns with the same complaint gets looked at first, that day, ahead of the schedule, and nobody argues about who pays until after it is fixed. Every shop has comebacks. The ones with a good name are the ones where the comeback was easy for the customer.

Keep the old part. Bag it, label it and offer it back. Your site promises this. Most people say no and every one of them remembers being asked.

Write the estimate in the bay, not from memory at the desk. With the car up and the flashlight on the problem is when you know what the job actually is. Ten minutes later at the counter is when you start guessing.

Sweep the floor. A customer cannot judge your torque wrench. They can judge the state of the waiting area, whether the oily rag is on the seat and whether the toilet is clean, and they will use those to decide whether you are careful. Unfair, and entirely real.

Raising prices. Change the ranges on the site, in the admin, between jobs. Never renegotiate with a person standing at your counter holding their keys.

Using your site

Your admin is at /admin on your own address. Everything you change there is live within a minute or two.

  • Photos. There are two slots that matter here, the hero and the owner photo. Replace the hero with your own bay, your own lift and your own sign as soon as you can. For a fixed-location shop, a photograph that proves the place exists and is tidy does more work than anything else on the page.
  • Pricing. The six published ranges and the diagnostic row are all editable: the label, the price text and the note under each one. Ranges stay ranges, written as text, so "applied to the repair" says exactly that. Edit them the week you change your labour rate, not six months later.
  • Details and hours. Put your real hours in and keep them right. More people are turned away by wrong hours on a map than by any price you publish. The owner paragraph is where you say who is turning the wrench, which matters more in this trade than most.
  • Reviews. Empty until you add them, deliberately. The site ships with no invented reviews. Add real ones as you earn them, with the customer's permission, and ask on the day you hand back the keys and the car is fixed.
  • Marketing kit. Generates a social image and a printable flyer with a QR code pointing at your site. Print the flyer for the fleet visits and leave one at every parts counter that will let you.
  • Leads. Every enquiry from your booking form is emailed to you and kept as a running list, so nothing is lost when the phone has been going all morning.

Your site also carries a Terms of Service and a Privacy Policy for your customers, generated from your business details. The Legal section of the admin is where you add your own clauses: your storage policy for cars left after completion, how long an estimate stands, and the wording of your workmanship guarantee.

Licences and insurance

This is orientation, not advice. Automotive repair is regulated in a lot of states and barely regulated in others, and the difference matters a great deal. Check what applies where you are before you take money.

  • Repair facility registration. A number of states register auto repair shops specifically, on top of a general business licence, and attach rules about written estimates, authorisation before work starts and what you must give the customer. Find out whether yours is one of them, first.
  • Certification is not a licence. Industry technician certification is the norm in this trade and is worth having, but in most places it is a credential, not permission to operate. Do not let the two blur on your website, and never imply that you hold one you do not.
  • Inspection and emissions work is separate. If you want to be a station that signs off safety or emissions tests, that is its own certification, its own equipment and its own audit, in the states that have it at all.
  • Air conditioning refrigerant. Handling refrigerant is federally regulated and there is a certification attached to doing it. Find out what applies to you before you open a system, and buy the recovery equipment before you buy the first can.
  • Waste oil, coolant, batteries and tyres. Every one of these has collection and disposal rules, usually at state level, and used oil in particular is handled as its own category. A licensed collector and a paper trail is the shape of the answer everywhere.
  • Insurance. General liability is the baseline. The one shops forget is cover for customers' vehicles in your care, custody and control, including the test drive, which is a separate thing from liability. Talk to a broker who has written a repair shop before, and be specific about road testing and about who is allowed to drive.
  • Workers' compensation. The moment there is a second person in the shop this becomes a question, and the rules differ sharply by state.
  • Mechanic's lien. Every state has a process for an unpaid repair and for a car nobody comes back for. Read yours before the first car is abandoned in your lot, because one will be.
  • Sales tax. Whether tax lands on parts, on labour, or on both varies by state. Ask an accountant once, early, rather than guessing for a year.

Do not claim on your site that you are licensed, certified, insured or bonded until each of those is true. Certifications in this trade are checkable by anybody with a phone, and in a town this size somebody will check.
