Your first week

You are not being hired to walk a dog. You are being hired to hold a key to somebody's house and be alone in it while they are at work. The dog is the easy part. Set up the trust part first.

  1. Sort your key system before you accept a single key. A lockbox on the client's own door is the best answer — you never carry their key, and they can change the code the day they stop using you. Where you do hold keys, label them with a code that means something only to you, never with a name or an address, and keep them somewhere locked. A ring of tagged house keys in a car door pocket is the nightmare version of this business.
  2. Call a broker about pet care cover, and use the right words. Ask about liability, and then ask specifically about care, custody and control — cover for injury to or loss of the animal that is in your hands, which plenty of general liability policies exclude and which is the exact thing most likely to go wrong. Ask about being bonded too, because clients who hand over keys ask about that by name. Do not put "bonded and insured" on the site until the paperwork is real.
  3. Write the dog file and the meet-and-greet script. Vet name and number, whether you are authorised to seek emergency treatment and who pays, vaccination status, allergies, feeding, medication, leash and harness setup, recall, what they are reactive to, gate and alarm codes, where the leash and towels live, and where the dog hides when frightened. Ask about bite history plainly and without apology — an owner who will not answer that question has answered it.
  4. Walk five dogs for nothing. Friends, neighbours, family. Learn what two leashes feel like before you find out what four feel like, on a wet pavement, next to a road. Photograph every one of those walks; that is your gallery for the first month.
  5. Draw your service area on a map and make it small. This trade is a routing problem wearing a dog costume. Midday walks all land in the same ninety minutes, and a client fifteen minutes outside your circle quietly costs you a whole slot. Pick a tight area, say so on the site, and grow the circle later.
  6. Buy the kit that stops disasters. A slip lead as a backup on every walk, a second clip so the dog is attached to you two ways, poo bags in every pocket, a towel and old blankets in the car, water and a collapsible bowl, a basic pet first aid kit, and treats you have cleared with the owner. Nothing here is expensive and any one of it can save you a client.
  7. Decide your pack rules before you have a pack. How many dogs, what sizes go together, that every new dog does solo walks first, and what makes a dog permanently a solo dog. Write it down while it is theoretical.

What to charge

Your site prices by service and duration — solo, pack and drop-in, each at twenty, thirty and forty-five minutes — with a discount for three-day and five-day weeks, and a separate overnight rate. Rewrite every number for your own market; the shape is what matters.

Solo costs more than pack because it costs you the same slot. A pack walk is the only lever in this business that lets one hour serve several clients. Price it so it is the obvious choice for a sociable dog and so a solo walk is genuinely worth doing for the dogs that need one.

The multi-day discount buys you a schedule, not a favour. A five-day dog is a fixed block in your week that you can build the rest of the route around. That is what the discount is for. Say so.

The mistake that quietly kills this business: taking clients in the order they enquire instead of in the order they sit on a map. Three good clients in three different towns will fill your midday and leave you driving most of it. Before you accept a new client, look at where they live against the walks you already have that day, and be willing to say "I am not covering that street yet — can I put you on the list for when I am?" A tight route is the whole difference in this trade.

Charge for the calendar, not just the walk. Holidays, last-minute bookings and same-day requests are worth a stated surcharge, decided in advance and written on your terms so it is never a negotiation on Christmas Eve.

Cancellations. A dog cancelled at 9am for a noon walk leaves a hole nobody else can fill. Decide your notice window, put it in writing at the meet and greet, and apply it the same way to everyone.

Overnights are a different business. You are sleeping in somebody's house, and you are not walking six other dogs that day. Price it as a night, not as a stack of visits, and be honest with yourself about what it does to the rest of your week.

Raising rates. In writing, with a month's notice, at a natural point in the year. Existing clients almost never leave over a modest increase announced properly; they leave over one sprung on them in a text.

Finding customers

Dog people ask other dog people, and they ask the professionals they already pay. Three channels do most of the work, and none of them is a paid advertisement.

  • Vets, groomers, trainers and boarding kennels. They are asked "do you know a walker?" constantly and they do not offer walks themselves, so there is nothing competitive about it. Go in mid-morning when it is quiet, ask who handles the front desk, leave cards and a single page. The trainer referral is the best of the lot, because a trainer has already assessed the dog and will only send you ones you can handle — and being the walker a trainer recommends is a reputation you cannot buy.
  • The route itself. You are visibly walking dogs in the same neighbourhoods three times a day, which no other trade in this catalog can say. Wear something with your name on it, keep cards in your pocket, and talk to people at the park and on the pavement. Somebody who watches you handle four dogs calmly past a barking terrier has seen a better advertisement than you could write.
  • Apartment buildings, HOAs and property managers with pet policies. A building full of dogs and residents who work in an office is a route in one postcode. Ask about a noticeboard, a resident newsletter, or being the name at the leasing desk.

Then there is the channel most walkers ignore: the clients you already have. The midday drop-in client goes on holiday and needs overnights. The pack-walk client breaks an ankle and needs a week of solos. Existing clients are the cheapest growth in this business, and a short note when their routine changes is all it takes. Offer a free walk for a referral — dog owners talk to each other constantly and one thank-you walk buys a lot of goodwill.

Your website's job is to answer the questions a nervous owner has before they will hand over a key: who you are, what happens at a meet and greet, how many dogs are in a pack, what happens if something goes wrong, and which streets you actually cover. The week builder earns its place because it lets someone price their own Monday-to-Friday without having to ask.

Running the work

Check the collar at the door, every single time. Two fingers under it, clip the backup slip lead as well as their own leash, and only then open the front door. Dogs back out of collars in the first thirty seconds of a walk, at the moment they are most excited, usually within sight of a road. Everything else in this section matters less than this.

Know your loose-dog plan before you need it. Do not chase — running at a loose dog pushes it further. Turn and move away, get low, use the treats, use the word they actually respond to, which you asked about at the meet and greet. Then, in order: call the owner, call the vet, post in the neighbourhood group, and keep looking. Have that order written in your phone, because you will not be thinking clearly.

Same walker, same route. It is the promise on your site and it is also simply true about dogs — they do not want variety, they want the trail they know and the person they know. It is also what lets you notice that he is favouring a back leg or that she did not finish breakfast, which is a thing owners will pay for long after the novelty of the service wears off.

Send the photo and the note before you start the car. One picture, two lines: where you went, how they did, anything worth flagging. If you leave it until the end of the day it does not get sent, and the report is a large part of what the client is actually buying — proof that somebody came.

Lock-up discipline is the thing that loses keys and clients: after every visit, physically turn the handle to confirm the door is locked, check the gate latch, and confirm the dog is on the correct side of both. Photograph the closed door and send it with the walk note. It takes five seconds and it ends every "did you lock up?" text forever — and the one time a gate was already broken when you arrived, you will have the picture.

Heat and ice are route decisions. Put the back of your hand on the pavement and hold it there — if you cannot, neither can they. On those days shorten the walk, find grass and shade, or swap in indoor play, and write in the note exactly what you did instead. Owners are fine with a changed plan and not fine with silence.

Never put your hands between dogs. If a scuffle starts, use the leash and your body position and distance, not your fingers. Any bite — to you, to a dog, to a person — is reported to the owner the same day and written down, with the time, the location and what led up to it. Do not soften it and do not wait.

Pick it up. All of it, every property, every time. This is what a neighbourhood judges a walker on and it takes exactly one complaint to a HOA to make you the problem.

Have a backup walker before you get flu. Dogs still need letting out on the day you cannot drive. Find somebody you trust, introduce them to your clients while nothing is wrong, and get the owner's advance permission in writing rather than sending a stranger to their door.

Using your site

Your admin is at /admin on your own address. Everything you change there is live within a minute or two.

  • Photos. Two slots — the hero and your own portrait. Replace the hero with your own dogs on your own trail as soon as you have permission to use them. Ask before you post any client's dog; most owners are delighted, and asking is what makes them delighted rather than surprised. Shoot in the morning or the late afternoon and get down to the dog's eye level — a photograph taken from standing height looks like a security camera.
  • Services and rates. Solo, pack and drop-in, each priced at twenty, thirty and forty-five minutes, plus your three-day and five-day discounts and the overnight rate. Those numbers drive the week builder, so edit them there and the calculator follows. Keep the pack cap visible — it is a selling point and it also sets expectations you will be held to.
  • Details and hours. Put your real walking windows in, not "9-5". A morning, midday and afternoon slot structure tells an owner far more, and it stops people asking for a 7pm walk you were never going to do.
  • Reviews. Empty until you add them, deliberately — nothing invented ships on this site. Add real ones only, with permission. Dog owners write good reviews if you ask right after a holiday you covered.
  • Marketing kit. Generates a social image and a printable flyer with a QR code pointing at your site. Print it for the vet's noticeboard, the groomer's counter and the apartment lobby.
  • Leads. Every enquiry from your booking form is emailed to you and kept as a running list. Reply the same day and open with the meet and greet — it is free, it closes the sale, and it is where you find out whether the dog is one you can walk.

Your site also carries a Terms of Service and a Privacy Policy for your customers, generated from your business details. Use the Legal section of the admin for the clauses this trade actually needs — your cancellation window, your key handling, your bad-weather policy, and the fact that you may seek emergency veterinary treatment at the owner's cost if you cannot reach them. Get that last one looked at properly and mirror it in your signed service agreement.

Licences and insurance

Read this as orientation only. Dog walking is not a licensed profession in the way that care work is, but the rules that do apply are mostly local, which means they vary from one city to the next rather than one state to the next. Check with your own city before you take a client.

  • Business registration and local licence. Most cities want a registered business name and a general business licence. Usually quick and cheap, and it is the first thing to do.
  • Commercial animal-care rules. Some cities and counties regulate walking dogs for hire specifically — a permit for commercial animal services, a cap on how many dogs one person may handle at once, rules about which parks may be used commercially. These are genuinely local, they differ between neighbouring towns, and the dog limit in particular is the one that catches walkers out. Ask your city's animal services or licensing office directly.
  • Leash, nuisance and waste ordinances. Leash law, where off-leash is allowed, and waste rules are local and enforced against you as the handler, not the owner.
  • Insurance. Liability cover is the baseline. The part to raise explicitly with a broker is care, custody and control — injury to or loss of an animal in your care — plus cover for the client's property while you hold a key. Ask what is excluded, in writing.
  • Bonding. Clients ask about this because you are alone in their house. Understand what a bond does and does not do before you describe yourself as bonded.
  • Driving dogs. Transporting animals for a fee may fall outside a personal auto policy, and some places have rules about how animals must be restrained in a vehicle. Ask your insurer before a dog is ever in your car.
  • Your service agreement. A signed agreement covering emergency veterinary authorisation and who pays, key handling and return, cancellation, vaccination expectations, and what happens if a dog bites someone on your walk. Have it drafted or reviewed by an attorney in your state rather than assembled from a template.
  • Workers' compensation and classification. The moment somebody else walks a dog for your business, whether they are an employee or a contractor and whether workers' compensation applies become real questions with state-specific answers.

Do not describe yourself on your site as licensed, insured, bonded or background-checked until every one of those is true and you hold the paper. People handing over house keys check, and being caught overstating it once is the end of the referral chain you spent a year building.
