Your first week

A DJ needs no licence to work, which means the barrier to starting is low and the barrier to being booked twice is entirely about reliability. Your first week is about building the rig, the library and the proof, in that order.

  1. Pick your design. This site ships in three: blue, green and pink. Open your admin, go to Business, and choose one under Site design. Only the look changes — your address, your words, your photos and your bookings all stay exactly where they are, and you can switch back. Pick the one your own artwork and photos sit on comfortably, because they will be on it.
  2. Decide what you are actually selling. Weddings, clubs, corporate events, school dances and private parties are five different businesses with different music, different gear, different hours and different buyers. A site that says "all events" says nothing to any of them. Choose one or two to lead with.
  3. Build the rig, including the spares. Controller, laptop, speakers and stands, the cables, and a wireless microphone — the microphone is the thing new DJs skip and the thing every wedding needs. Then buy the second set of cables and the spare mic batteries in the same trip, because you will not go back for them.
  4. Build the library properly, then back it up twice. Buy your music or use a record pool. Clean the metadata, set your cue points, and keep the whole library on the laptop plus two separate drives. Drives fail during the reception, not at home.
  5. Set the whole rig up in your living room and time it. From the car to the first track playing. Then break it down and time that too. You will quote those two numbers for the rest of your career, and every venue will ask when you need access.
  6. Play one real event, any size. A friend's party, a bar's quiet night, a school function. You need photographs of a full floor and a short video of you working, and neither of those can be faked or bought.
  7. Fill the site with the real thing. Hero photo, your biography, the shows you have actually played, the tracks you have actually released, and your real social links. Demo entries are there so the page is not empty on day one, not so you can leave them.

What to charge

This site carries no rate card, and that is deliberate — the admin's Pricing section will tell you as much. DJ work is quoted per event, because the date, the hours, the venue and what you are being asked to run change the job completely. The site exists to start that conversation, not to publish a number.

Quote the whole commitment, not the hours on the floor. A wedding is the planning calls, the timeline, the load-in, the sound check, the ceremony, the cocktail hour, the dinner, the reception, the breakdown, and the drive both ways. The set is the smallest part of the day. Price the day.

The event types should not cost the same. A club is buying a set. A wedding is buying planning, an MC, a person who keeps a schedule running, and the certainty that nothing goes silent in front of two hundred guests. Corporate is buying discretion and punctuality. Quote them differently because they are different work.

Extras go on the quote as lines. Ceremony sound at a second location, uplighting, an extra speaker for the cocktail hour, a photo booth, fog, travel past a radius you set, and an overnight if the venue is far. Never bundle them invisibly; a customer who cannot see what they are paying for assumes the number is padded.

The overtime conversation happens at 10:45pm, in front of the bride's father: which is the worst possible moment to invent a number. Put the overtime rate in the written agreement, per half hour, and say it again at the planning call. Then when the question comes you answer it in one sentence instead of negotiating in a hallway with the music still running.

A signed agreement and a deposit hold the date. You are taking a Saturday off the market, and the deposit is what makes that reasonable for both of you. Never hold a date on a verbal maybe.

Raise prices for new enquiries only. Anything already signed you honour, even the one you now think you underquoted — that job is a reference.

Finding customers

Bookings in this trade come from two very different places: the people who sell events for a living, and the people standing in the room while you play. Both take patience, and neither is advertising.

  • Venues, coordinators and photographers. These are the strongest channel by a distance, because they recommend the DJ who will not embarrass them in front of their own client. Be early, be tidy, be sober, keep your cables taped down, and introduce yourself properly at load-out. Ask venues what it takes to get on their preferred-vendor list, then be the kind of vendor that list is for.
  • The floor is the audition. Everyone at a wedding is somebody who may plan an event of their own, and they are watching you work for six hours. A card at the booth and a straight answer when somebody asks what you charge does more than any post.
  • A residency on a quiet night. A bar's slow weeknight is regular work, a room to test music in, and a standing reason for people to see you play. Offer to build the night rather than just to fill it.
  • Video of a full floor. Thirty seconds of a packed room with the sound on tells a buyer more than a page of description. Shoot it wide enough to see the crowd, and ask the host before you post anything from a private event.
  • Releases are an audience, not a booking channel. If you produce, your tracks build listeners — a different thing entirely from the trust that books a wedding. Confusing the two is the common mistake: a strong release page does not fill a Saturday calendar, and a full Saturday calendar does not grow a streaming audience. Your site carries both because they feed different rooms.

Keep every enquiry, including the ones that go nowhere. A couple who booked someone else this year has friends getting married next year, and a polite reply to a no is cheap.

Running the work

Arrive early and be quiet by the time guests do. Set up, sound check, and then be standing behind a finished booth well before doors. The whole professional difference between a DJ who gets rebooked and one who does not is visible in the hour before anyone arrives.

Silence is the only real failure: a transition you did not like, a request you turned down, a track that emptied a corner — all recoverable inside a minute. Two seconds of nothing clears a floor and everybody in the room notices. That is the entire reason for the second laptop, the spare cable, the backup drive and a pre-built playlist you can start from a phone. It is not paranoia; it is the job.

Do the planning call and write it down. Timeline, the names you have to announce and how they are pronounced, the first-dance track, the do-not-play list, who cues you, what happens if it rains, and the venue's load-in details — parking, stairs, lift, where the power is and whether it is shared with the caterer. Confirm all of it again the week before.

The do-not-play list matters more than the must-play list. It is the short list of things that will actually upset your client. Take requests through one named person rather than from the crowd, and agree that with them in advance so you can say so politely all night.

Volume is a working relationship. Ask the venue what they cap at, ask the photographer where they need quiet, and keep dinner conversational. A room you blow out at seven has nobody left at ten.

Microphone work is a separate skill. Fewer words, said clearly, on a microphone you tested. Write the announcements down rather than improvising them, and say the names exactly as you were told at the planning call.

Keep the set list from every event. What worked in that room, at that hour, for that crowd. After a year of those, you stop guessing at a new room.

Using your site

Your admin is at /admin on your own address. Everything you change there is live within a minute or two.

  • Site design. This is the only site in the range that ships in more than one design — blue, green and pink. The picker is in Business, under Site design. Switching changes the look only: your address, your writing, your photos and your enquiries stay exactly as they are, and you can switch back any time.
  • Photos. One zone, the hero at the top. Use a photograph of you working with a crowd in it rather than a posed portrait — a buyer is trying to picture their own room, and an empty booth does not help them.
  • Details and hours. Your name, tagline, city, contact and inquiry email live in Business, and the hours you put there show in the footer. In this trade "hours" means when you answer, not when you play — something like "enquiries answered weekday evenings" is more honest and more useful.
  • Pricing. Not part of this design, and the admin will say so. Events are quoted, and the booking form is what starts that.
  • Social links. Add the ones you actually maintain and delete the rest. A dead SoundCloud link does more damage than a missing one.
  • Your biography, releases, shows and video library. These are built into the site rather than edited in the admin today, and they ship with clearly marked demo entries. Send us your real ones — and do it before you send the link to a venue, because a demo show list is worse than an empty one.
  • Reviews. This design does not carry a reviews section, and the admin will tell you so. Wherever you do quote a client, use only words a real person said, with their permission.
  • Marketing kit. Generates a social image and a printable flyer with a QR code pointing at your site. Useful for a venue noticeboard and for the table at a bridal show.
  • Leads. Every booking request from your form is emailed to you and kept as a running list, so a date enquiry is not lost in a busy inbox.

Your site also carries a Terms of Service and a Privacy Policy for your customers, generated from your business details. The Legal section of the admin is where your deposit rule, your cancellation window and your overtime policy belong.

Licences and insurance

This is orientation, not advice. Being a DJ is not a licensed trade in the way that food service or tattooing is — but the music you play, the equipment you carry and the venues you enter all bring obligations of their own, and they vary by state and by venue.

  • Business registration. A registered business name and a local business licence usually apply to you like any other trade, even though the DJing itself needs no permit. This is normally quick and is the first thing to sort out.
  • Music licensing. Playing recorded music in public is licensed through performing-rights organisations, and the obligation usually sits with the venue — but not always, and it can land differently for a private event or for a night you promote yourself. Ask each venue whether they hold the licences, and if you ever run your own event, find out what you need before you sell a ticket.
  • Where your music comes from. Buy it, or subscribe to a record pool. Playing files you did not obtain legitimately is a copyright problem rather than a licensing one, and some agencies and venues will ask you about it directly.
  • Insurance. Many venues will not let you load in without a certificate of general liability, and some require you to name them on it. Equipment cover is a separate question — a homeowner's policy commonly excludes gear used for business, so ask your broker specifically rather than assuming you are covered.
  • Contracts. One written agreement per event: date, address, hours, what you are providing, the deposit, the overtime rate, the cancellation terms, and what happens if the venue loses power. This is the document that settles arguments you cannot afford to have on the night.
  • Sales tax. Whether a DJ service is taxable varies by state, and renting out equipment such as uplighting or a photo booth can be treated differently from the service itself. Ask an accountant once, early.
  • Workers' compensation. The moment you bring a second DJ or an assistant, this becomes a question, and the rules differ by state including for occasional help.

Do not put "licensed and insured" on your site until both are true. Venues and coordinators ask for the certificate, and being unable to produce it is how you lose a preferred-vendor listing before you get one.
