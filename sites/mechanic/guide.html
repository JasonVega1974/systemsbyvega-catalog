Your first week

You have no building, no lift and no shelf of parts. The truck is the shop, the customer's driveway is the bay, and everything you cannot carry is a job you do not take. That constraint is not a weakness — it is the entire offer — but it has to be organised before the first stranger's car goes up on your stands.

  1. Build the truck to a written list, then count it every night. A floor jack rated well past the heaviest vehicle you will touch, four stands, chocks, a torque wrench you trust, a breaker bar, a cordless impact and spare batteries, work lights, cones, a drip mat, and a sealed oil caddy with a lid. Count the tools back into the truck before you pull off somebody's street. A socket left in a driveway is a customer phoning you about their tyre.
  2. Write the no-list and put it where you can read it on the phone. Anything wanting a lift, an alignment rack, a paint booth or a transmission on a jack is not a driveway job. Your site already says this out loud. Saying the same thing on the phone, in the same words, before you have taken money, is what makes people trust the yes when it comes.
  3. Set your radius and your arrival window. Draw the circle on a map and know what falls outside it, because the drive is unpaid time and it is the cost that quietly eats a mobile operation. Quote arrival windows, never arrival times. Somebody waiting from nine to eleven is fine; somebody waiting from nine is furious by nine-twenty.
  4. Learn to quote from a text message. Year, make, model, engine, symptom, and a photograph. Practise on ten common jobs until you can look up the labour time and the parts price and send a flat rate back in minutes. Your whole model is a number texted before the truck loads, so the speed of that reply is the business.
  5. Solve parts before you solve customers. You keep no stock, so a counter you pass on the way, or a supplier who will drop to a residential address, is your parts department. Ask specifically whether they deliver to a driveway, because plenty do not.
  6. Answer the waste question before your first oil change. You leave with the old oil, the filter, the coolant and the dead battery in the back of your truck. That means a sealed container, a secured load and a recycler who will take them from a mobile operator rather than only from a shop with an account. Find that recycler in week one.
  7. Do three real jobs at cost, on driveways you already have permission to be on. Time yourself honestly. Everything takes longer on gravel, in the wind, kneeling, with the light going. Build your flat rates on those times, not on book hours from a shop with a lift.

What to charge

Your site ships with seven flat-rate rows: oil and filter, brakes, battery, starter or alternator, belt and tensioner, a scan-tool diagnostic, and an honest "not on this list" row that quotes by text. Every figure is yours to edit in the admin, along with the note under each card. They are a starting shape for your market, not a recommendation.

Flat rate, not hourly, and this is not a style choice. The customer is standing ten feet away watching you work. An hourly rate turns every dropped bolt into money in front of an audience, and it punishes you for being quick. A flat number agreed before you arrive removes the clock from the conversation entirely.

Put the drive inside the number. A separate call-out line is something to argue about; a flat rate that already covers getting there is not. Set a minimum that makes the shortest job worth loading the truck for, and think hard before agreeing to anything outside your radius that is not stacked with another job the same day.

The range is the difference between a small sedan and a three-quarter-ton truck. That is what the published spread is for, and the note on each card should say so. The individual vehicle gets one number by text before you load up, and that number does not move unless the customer approves a change. That promise is on your page. It only works if you never break it.

Quote the vehicle, never the job title: "brakes" on one truck is an hour and on another it is an afternoon of seized hardware and a torch. Ask for the year, the make, the model and a photograph of the wheel before you send a number. A flat rate you guessed at is a flat rate you will honour at a loss, in somebody's driveway, in the dark.

The diagnostic is charged and waived on the repair. That is the fair version, and it protects you from being the free second opinion for people who intend to do the work themselves. If the battery tests healthy and nothing needs replacing, the diagnostic is still the bill — your page already says that, plainly, and it is one of the most honest things on it.

Several vehicles at one address is one drive and one arrival window. Price the route stop, not the vehicle count, and say so when you talk to a business.

Finding customers

A shop advertises to people who can drive to it. You cannot use those channels, because your customer is specifically the person who cannot, or will not, get a vehicle to a shop. Go where those people are.

  • Apartment complexes, mobile home parks and property managers. These are households with no garage, no driveway of their own and often one car that has to work tomorrow. Get the manager's permission properly and in writing, ask what the rules are for working on a vehicle in the lot, and then ask to leave cards in the office. A dead battery in an apartment lot at seven in the morning is your customer today, and there is no shop in town that can help them.
  • Small fleets that park at one address overnight. A landscaper with three trucks and a trailer, a contractor's vans, a church bus, a farm. Their vehicles sit still all night on private ground, which is the ideal mobile job, and taking one off the road for a day in a shop costs them a crew. Ask for the whole lot as a route stop rather than one vehicle at a time.
  • The "does anyone know a mechanic who can come out" post. It appears in every local Facebook group and every community board most weeks. Answer it like a person, with what the job would probably need and an offer to look — not a copy-paste advert. Also list yourself as a service-area business on your map profile, with the area shown and no street address, because you do not have one and inventing one is how a profile gets pulled.

Pre-purchase inspections are the product only you can sell. Somebody buying a used car off a private seller cannot ask that seller to drive it to a shop, but they can send you to the seller's kerb. It is an hour, it needs no parts, and the buyer who trusts your answer becomes the owner who calls you about the same car later.

Then there is the workplace visit. Being able to say that the vehicle gets fixed between clock-in and lunch, in the staff car park, is a genuinely different pitch from anything a garage can offer. Ask an employer with a big lot whether you can post a notice in the break room.

Running the work

Level ground and stands, with no exceptions ever: nothing goes under a vehicle held up by a jack. A driveway on a slope, a soft verge, gravel that a stand foot sinks into, a car in the street on a camber — all of those are a rescheduled job, not a careful one. The customer will tell you it is fine. They are not the one who will be under it. Have a flat spot in mind you can move to, and be willing to lose the booking.

Text before you leave and text when you are close. You are turning up at somebody's home. The arrival message is not a courtesy, it is what stops a stranger from finding a man with a jack beside their car.

Mat down before anything else comes off the truck. Their concrete stays their concrete. This is written on your own pricing page and it is the single detail that decides whether a homeowner books you again, well ahead of your torque technique.

Never take a part off until the replacement is in your hand. In a shop, a wrong part means a car sits on a lift for a day. In a driveway, a wrong part means somebody's only vehicle is on stands outside their house and they cannot go to work. Confirm the part number against the vehicle in front of you, not against the listing.

Photograph the old part with their car in the frame. Worn pad next to the new one, the split boot, the corroded terminal. Send it as you go. The customer who is at work while you are in their driveway is trusting you completely, and photographs are the only thing you can give them in return.

Weather decides the schedule for you. Brake and belt work in rain or snow is work done badly. A battery, a scan, a fluid check will happen in almost anything. Phone and move the job rather than kneeling in the wet to protect a booking; your page already promises exactly that, and people are far more reasonable about it than you expect.

Leave the space cleaner than you found it. Sweep the rust flakes, pick up the packaging, take the old part or leave it where they asked, roll the mat. You have been working in somebody's home, not in a workshop, and that is the part they will describe to a neighbour.

Restock at the end of the day, not the start of the next one. Fluids, gloves, rags, fuses, batteries for the impact. The morning is for driving, and a truck that is short of something at eight is a job you cannot start.

Using your site

Your admin is at /admin on your own address. Everything you change there is live within a minute or two.

  • Photos. There are slots for a logo, the hero, the owner, and the before and after frames. Replace the hero with your own hands on your own job as soon as you have one. Shoot in daylight in an actual driveway; a mobile business proving it turns up at houses is the whole point of the picture.
  • The before and after slider. It ships with two different vehicles showing the two ends of one brake job, and the note under it says so in plain words. When you replace those frames with a genuine pair from one customer's driveway, edit the note to match. Do not leave a disclosure on the page that has stopped being true, and do not remove one that still is.
  • Pricing. All seven rows are yours: the label, the price text and the note under each card. This site actually renders that note, so use it for the thing you would say on the phone — which engines are the top of the range, what the diagnostic covers, when you need a photograph first. Clear a note and the card simply drops it.
  • Details and hours. You have no shop address and the site is built that way on purpose, so hours and service area do the work instead. Say which towns you actually drive to and be honest about the edges.
  • Reviews. Empty until you add them, deliberately. The site ships with no invented reviews on it. Add real ones as you earn them, with the customer's permission — ask while you are packing up and the vehicle is fixed in front of them.
  • Marketing kit. Generates a social image and a printable flyer with a QR code pointing at your site. Print the flyer for the apartment office noticeboard and the break room; those two places are most of your first year.
  • Leads. Every enquiry from the booking form is emailed to you and kept as a running list. People send symptoms, photographs and an address, which is exactly what you need to quote a flat rate back.

Your site also carries a Terms of Service and a Privacy Policy for your customers, generated from your business details. The Legal section of the admin is where your own clauses go, and a mobile operation has particular ones: what you need at the address, when you will refuse to work on unsafe ground, what happens if a job is abandoned part-way because the vehicle turns out to need a shop, and how the workmanship promise works when the customer supplied the parts.

Licences and insurance

This is orientation, not advice. Being mobile changes the picture in ways that surprise people who have only worked in shops. Check what applies where you are before you take money.

  • Repair registration still applies to you. Where a state registers automotive repair, it generally registers the activity, not the building — a mobile operator is usually not exempt. Find out how your state treats a repair business with no fixed premises, because that is a question with an actual answer.
  • Where you are allowed to work. This is the one nobody expects. Plenty of cities and counties have rules about vehicle repair in a residential driveway or a parking lot, and plenty of HOAs and apartment leases have their own on top. Get the property manager's permission in writing, and know what your local ordinance says before you make it a habit.
  • Commercial vehicle insurance on the truck. A personal auto policy generally does not cover a vehicle used for business, and it certainly does not cover the tools in the back. Tell a broker exactly what the truck does.
  • Liability, and cover for the vehicle you are working on. General liability is the baseline. Separately, you need to know what answers if a customer's car is damaged while it is in your hands, including on a road test — in a shop that cover has a familiar name, and a mobile operator has to ask for it specifically rather than assume it.
  • Certification is not a licence. Technician certification is worth having and is worth nothing as a claim you have not earned. Never imply on your site that you hold one you do not.
  • Waste oil and coolant, and the fact that you transport them. A shop stores waste in a drum and a licensed hauler collects it. You carry it in a vehicle on a public road, which is a different situation with its own rules about containment, quantity and where it may be taken. Sort this properly; it is the most likely environmental problem in a mobile operation.
  • Business registration and sales tax. Register the business and the name. Whether tax lands on parts, on labour or on both varies by state, and being mobile can raise questions about which jurisdiction a job happened in. Ask an accountant once, early.
  • Workers' compensation. The moment there is a second person on the truck this becomes a question, and the rules differ sharply by state.

Do not put "licensed and insured" on your site until both are true. It is the easiest claim in the world to type and one of the easiest to check, and a mobile operator who turns out to be neither has nothing left to trade on.
