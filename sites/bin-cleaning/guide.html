Your first week

This is a route business, not a job business. What you are selling is a stop on a street on a given weekday, and everything you decide in the first week either builds route density or destroys it. Do these in order.

  1. Get the collection calendar before you get a customer. Your city or hauler publishes which streets are emptied on which day. Print it and mark it up. That map is your route: you wash a bin on the day it is tipped, while it is empty and already standing at the kerb. Every other decision hangs off that grid.
  2. Decide the rig before you decide the price. Hot water, a pump, a supply tank, and a tank that catches what comes back off the bin. Whether you lift bins with a hoist or tip them by hand decides how many stops fit in a day and how your back feels in month three. Work out how many bins one tank of water gets you, then plan the day around refills.
  3. Find out where the dirty water is allowed to go. What comes off a trash bin is not rainwater, and a storm drain is the wrong answer in every town. Call public works or the sewer utility, ask what they expect you to do with wash water, and write the answer down before you take money.
  4. Wash your own bin and your two nearest neighbours', free. Photograph the inside with the lid up, in daylight, standing in the same spot before and after. The inside is what sells this. Nobody has ever booked a bin cleaning because of a photograph of the outside of a bin.
  5. Set up recurring billing before your first paying customer. This is a subscription with a route attached, not a series of one-off jobs. Monthly or quarterly, on a card, cancel any time. Start out collecting cash at the kerb and you will spend the whole first year chasing single bins.
  6. Work one collection day on one street. Door-hang the block you just washed, the evening before their pickup day, when a flyer actually gets read. Ten bins on one street is a business. The same ten bins spread across town is a day of driving.
  7. Put your real service days on the site. Set the hours and the service area to the hauler's days rather than to office hours, so a booking arrives already sorted into the route day it belongs to.

What to charge

Your site ships with four rows: one bin, two bins, three bins, and a group rate for four or more or a whole street. Change every figure to your own market — they are a shape, not a recommendation.

You are pricing the stop, not the bin. The wash takes minutes. The driving does not. That is the entire reason the per-bin number falls as the count goes up: a second and third bin at a house you are already parked outside cost you little more than water. Build the tiers so adding the second bin is obviously worth it, and so organising a whole street is obviously worth it.

A bin that has never been cleaned is a different job. Years of packed residue, a lid seal full of grime, sometimes maggots. Decide now whether that is a first-clean line on top of the regular price, or simply what the first visit costs, and say which on the page. Do not discover it at the kerb and swallow it.

The rule that saves route businesses: what happens when the bin is not out, or is still full. Write it into the site's job details before you sign anybody up — that the visit is charged, or moved to the next route day, whichever you choose — then photograph the full bin and text the photo. An argument about a bin you could not wash is the most common way a subscription gets cancelled.

Pick a cadence and defend it. Monthly, every other month, quarterly — whichever you sell has to match the route you can actually drive. Selling three different cadences on one street means driving that street three times as often for the same houses.

Raise prices between seasons, in writing, to the whole route at once. Never on a doorstep, and never for the one customer who happened to ask.

Finding customers

Nothing in this trade rewards a scattered customer list. You are filling in streets, not covering a map. Three channels do that.

  • The kerb you are standing at. A freshly washed bin sitting beside eleven filthy ones is the best advertisement this trade has, and it runs until everybody wheels theirs in. Hang a flyer on the doors either side while you are there, and put a small dated tag on the lid you cleaned so the neighbours know who did it.
  • Subdivision groups and HOA newsletters. Neighbourhood Facebook groups are organised by subdivision, which is exactly the shape you want. Post the inside-the-bin before and after, name the street you were on, and name the collection day you cover. Ask the HOA to run you in the newsletter, and offer a street rate in exchange.
  • Sign a street up before you serve it. Pick a subdivision, flyer it with a start date, and say the route begins once enough houses on that block are in. People will join something starting on their own street far more readily than they will buy a service alone, and you get density on day one instead of building toward it.

Property managers, apartment complexes and small restaurants are the second act of this business. Dumpster pads and commercial containers need heavier gear than a residential rig, so do not promise it before you own it — but the drive that serves a subdivision usually passes several of them.

Keep one spreadsheet: address, collection day, bin count, cadence, first-clean date. That file is the business. If you ever sell this, that file is what you are selling.

Running the work

Wash in an order and never vary it. Knock out what is stuck, hot wash the inside, then the underside of the lid — that is where the smell actually lives and it is the step people skip — then the outside, the lip, the wheels. Deodorise last. Roll it back upright with the lid closed, facing the way you found it.

Never the storm drain. Not the gutter, not the kerb, not "it is mostly water anyway". Storm drains run to a creek in most towns, and this is the one thing that turns a small bin-cleaning outfit into a compliance problem with a name on it. Capture it, haul it, and get rid of it the way your utility told you to.

Know your water before you plan your day. Count how many bins one tank really gets you, then fix your refill points on the map. If you intend to fill from a hydrant, that generally means a metered permit from the utility — ask, do not just open one.

Run the route in driving order. Put the day's stops on the phone in the order you will physically drive them, not alphabetically and not in sign-up order. Re-sorting a route sheet in the truck is how a day loses an hour.

Winter is a real interruption in a cold climate. Below freezing you cannot wash, and water left in a pump or a line splits it. Decide your season, tell the route in advance, and pause the billing rather than let people cancel. A paused subscription restarts in spring; a cancelled one gets shopped.

Leave evidence every visit. The cancellation almost never comes from bad work. It comes from a customer who was at work when you came and forgot the service exists. A dated lid tag, or a text with a photo of their own bin, costs nothing and keeps the subscription in their head.

Using your site

Your admin is at /admin on your own address. Everything you change there is live within a minute or two.

Start in Details. The site ships filled in with a working business's name, phone, email and service area, and none of that is yours until you replace it. Do that before you send the link to anybody.

  • Photos. This niche has a logo slot and a before/after pair, and no hero photograph. The before/after slider on the homepage is the whole argument, so shoot it properly: same bin, lid up, same standing spot, same daylight, dirty one in the before slot.
  • Pricing. Four rows, with the label, the price and the note on each yours to rewrite. Use the note line to carry the per-bin maths, so the reason to add a second bin is on the page instead of in your head.
  • Job details. The included and not-included lists. Your bin-not-out rule, your broken-wheel rule and your no-rush-service rule belong here. Anything you would rather not argue about at a kerb goes here in writing.
  • Details and hours. Put route days in, not office hours. "Tuesday and Thursday routes, booked by text" tells somebody more than "Mon-Fri 9-5".
  • Reviews. Empty by design — the site ships with no invented reviews on it. Add real ones as you earn them, with the customer's permission.
  • Marketing kit. Generates a social image and a printable flyer with a QR code pointing at your site. Print that flyer for the doors either side of every bin you wash.
  • Leads. Every booking from the form is emailed to you and kept as a running list, so nothing is lost when the inbox gets busy.

Your site also carries a Terms of Service and a Privacy Policy for your customers, generated from your business details. Add your own clauses in the Legal section of the admin — the cancellation window, the missed-bin rule, your winter pause.

Licences and insurance

This is orientation, not advice, and the rules vary enormously by state, by city and by water utility. Check what applies where you live before you take money.

  • Business registration. Most places want a registered business name and a local business licence before you trade. Usually quick and cheap, and the first thing to do.
  • Wash water discharge. The one that matters most in this trade. Many cities have explicit rules about wash water from mobile cleaning, set locally rather than nationally. Ask public works or the sewer utility what capture and disposal they expect, and get the answer in writing if you can.
  • Buying water. Filling from a hydrant or a bulk station generally needs a permit and a meter from the utility. Ask before you build a route around it.
  • Vehicle and trailer. A full water tank is heavy. Check your state's rules on trailer weight, registration and what licence class you need to tow it — and check whether your auto policy covers business use, because many personal policies explicitly do not.
  • Liability insurance. You are working on other people's property with hot water and pressure. Talk to a broker before the first paid job and describe the work honestly, wash water included.
  • Sanitising claims. Sanitise and disinfect are regulated words attached to products registered for that use. If you put them on your site, use a product labelled for it and follow the label. Never put a percentage on it.
  • Sales tax. Whether a cleaning service is taxable varies by state. Ask an accountant once, early, rather than guessing for a year.
  • If you are under eighteen. Registering a business, signing a contract and holding a merchant account are all governed by age rules that differ by state. Sort that out with a parent or guardian before you start, not after.

Do not claim on your site that you are licensed, insured or bonded until you are. That claim is checkable, and on a street where you have washed twenty bins it will be checked.
