Your first week

Moving is a labour business with a calendar problem. Leases turn on the first of the month, so the last few days of every month and the whole of summer are the work, and a mid-month week in February is quiet. Everything you build in the first week should be aimed at being ready for the busy days and having something useful to do on the slow ones.

  1. Settle the authority question before you advertise anything. Moving household goods is one of the few local trades with a real registration layer, and it differs depending on whether you stay inside the state or cross a line. Find out what your state requires of an intrastate household-goods mover, and what applies federally the moment a job crosses a border. Do this first, because the answer shapes what you are allowed to put on your own website.
  2. Consider starting labour-only. Loading and unloading customers' rental trucks and portable containers takes no truck, no commercial vehicle, and a much smaller insurance conversation. It is real work with real customers, and it is the cheapest way to learn whether your back and your temperament are suited to this before you sign for a box truck.
  3. Buy pads before you buy anything else. Far more moving blankets than feels reasonable, four-wheel dollies, an appliance dolly with a proper belt strap, shoulder straps, ratchet and cam straps, shrink wrap, tape, floor runners, door jamb protectors, mattress bags, and a small tool bag with an allen set and a crescent wrench for bed frames. A crew with too few pads damages furniture and knows it.
  4. Learn to estimate hours by estimating your own house. Run your own place through the load calculator on your site, write down the number, then actually move something and compare. Estimating hours is the entire commercial skill in this trade and there is no way to learn it except by guessing, timing, and closing the gap. Keep doing it on every job for a year.
  5. Write the clock policy down. When the clock starts, when it stops, the minimum hours, whether travel is inside the rate, what a deposit holds and what happens on a cancellation. Nearly every argument in moving is an argument about the clock, and it is only ever an argument when nobody wrote it down.
  6. Find two people you would trust with a couch and your name. This trade lives or dies on whether a crew turns up at half past seven. Pay properly, feed them, and decide early that you would rather turn down a job than send a crew you are not sure of.
  7. Do three moves for friends at cost, on the clock. Time each phase separately: protection, disassembly, carry, load, drive, unload, rebuild. Those numbers are what make your quotes honest and your bookings fit in a day.

What to charge

Your site prices by crew size and hour — two, three or four movers with a truck, each at its own rate — and the load calculator picks the tier from what the customer says they are moving. All of those rates are editable in the admin. They are a starting shape for your market, not a recommendation.

You are selling hours, so protect the hour. The rate has to carry wages, payroll taxes, workers' compensation, the truck, fuel, pads that wear out, and the drive back to the yard at the end. A rate set by looking at what the cheapest advert in town says is a rate set by somebody else's arithmetic.

Minimum hours are not a trick. A two-hour job still consumes a morning, a crew and a truck. Say the minimum at booking, in the same sentence as the rate, and nobody minds.

A bigger crew usually costs the customer less. Four movers at the higher hourly rate finish a big house in far fewer hours than two movers will, and the furniture takes less punishment. Do that arithmetic out loud at the walkthrough instead of defaulting to the smallest crew because the hourly number sounds friendlier. Customers remember the mover who talked them into the right crew.

The underestimate is the mistake, and it is almost always the same underestimate: the walk. Not the lifting — the distance from the door to the truck, the elevator wait, the second flight, the disassembly nobody mentioned, the stop at the storage unit. Estimate the work, then add the walking. Give the customer a range, put the top of the range on the written confirmation, and let coming in under it be the good news.

Never make an unseen quote binding. Your calculator says plainly that it produces a ballpark, not a quote — repeat that on the phone in your own words. The walkthrough, fifteen minutes in person or on video, is what turns it into a number you will stand behind.

Do not invent surcharges. Your site promises straight hourly with no fuel surcharge, no stair fee and no heavy-item ambush on the final bill. That is a real promise and it is most of why somebody called you. If stairs and a piano make a job harder, the honest answer is a bigger crew agreed in advance, not a line item discovered at the end. Charge packing materials separately by what actually gets used.

Finding customers

Work the calendar before you work the marketing. Month-end and summer weekends book out and should be sold at full crew. Mid-month and winter weekdays are where you go looking for the jobs nobody else wants — in-house moves for a flooring installation, an office that has to move after hours, a senior downsizing on a Tuesday.

  • The people who know before the customer does. Apartment and complex leasing offices, self-storage managers, senior living communities and senior move managers, realtors looking for a closing gift, staging companies, and flooring installers who need a house emptied for a day. They learn about a move weeks before a mover does. Visit in person, leave something they can hand over, and make it easy for them to look good for recommending you.
  • Reviews, asked for on the truck. In no trade do reviews matter more, because a stranger is about to hand you everything they own. Ask at the end, on the spot, while they are relieved and standing in a furnished house — not by email three days later when the relief has worn off. Ask by name, and have the crew lead ask, because the crew lead is who they liked.
  • A Google Business Profile with your own photographs. Your truck, your crew in branded shirts, a wrapped stair rail, a loaded truck packed tight. The searches you want are local and urgent, and the profile with real photographs of a real crew beats a stock photograph every time.
  • Repeat and referral from the move itself. Leave a small stack of cards on the counter at the end. Neighbours watch a move happen from their windows for six hours; a tidy crew and a clean truck is a long advert.

You will be offered leads by broker sites that sell the same enquiry to several movers at once. You can win work there, but understand what it is before you spend: you are bidding against whoever is willing to underquote the job, on a customer who was never yours.

Running the work

The walkthrough is where the day is won. Fifteen minutes, in person or on video. Count what is going, and hunt specifically for the things that ruin a schedule: a piano, a gun safe, a spiral staircase, a long carry from a parking lot, a building elevator that must be booked, a stairwell with a turn that will not take a sofa, a driveway a big truck cannot enter. Ask about the destination too — half the surprises in moving are waiting at the other end.

Protect the building before you touch the furniture. Floor runners down, door jambs padded, stair rails wrapped, before the first box moves. Damage claims in this trade are overwhelmingly about the house, not the contents, and a wrapped doorway also tells the customer in the first ten minutes what kind of crew they hired.

Wrap at the item, carry once. Blanket and shrink the piece where it stands rather than carrying it bare to the truck and wrapping it there. Everything gets padded, including the things that look tough.

Load in tiers. Heavy low and forward over the axle, tiers built wall to ceiling, mattresses and mirrors flat against the side, strap every tier before starting the next. A tight load does not shift, and a load that does not shift is the difference between an uneventful drive and a claim.

Report damage before the truck leaves. A scuff the crew lead points out while you are both standing there is a short, decent conversation. The same scuff found by the customer after you have driven away is a claim and a review. Make it a standing rule that nobody on your crew hides anything, and make it safe for them to say so.

Label the rooms at the destination first. Tape a sheet of paper on each door before the first box comes off the truck. It costs five minutes and saves an hour of "where does this go" — and the customer is paying by the hour.

Big buildings need paperwork days ahead. Many require a certificate of insurance naming the building, and a booked elevator or loading dock window. Ask at booking, not on the morning, because a crew standing in a lobby is billable time nobody enjoys paying for.

Feed the crew. Water in the truck as standard, a real lunch break, and shade in July. Crews that are looked after come back next season, and in a trade where turnover is the default that is a genuine advantage.

Using your site

Your admin is at /admin on your own address. Everything you change there is live within a minute or two.

  • Photos. Replace the hero with your own truck and your own crew as soon as you have both. A packed, strapped truck interior is an unusually persuasive photograph in this trade, because it is the thing customers worry about and never get to see.
  • Pricing. Your crew tiers — the labels, the hourly rates and the "who this fits" line for each — are all editable. The calculator uses those rates, so change them there and the ballpark follows.
  • The load calculator. The list of rooms and heavy items and the settings behind them are yours. Tune it against your own completed jobs as you learn your real pace, and keep the note that says these are ballparks rather than quotes. That note is doing important work.
  • Details and hours. Say your booking lead time honestly — that month-end and summer weekends go weeks ahead and mid-month weekdays are often available in days. It saves you the phone calls you cannot serve.
  • Reviews. They ship empty on purpose, with nothing invented in them. Fill them with real ones as you earn them, with permission, and put the best one at the top.
  • Marketing kit. Generates a social image and a printable flyer with a QR code pointing at your site. This is what you leave at the leasing office and the storage counter.
  • Leads. Every enquiry from your booking form is emailed to you and kept as a running list. Moving enquiries have a date attached and a shopping list of three competitors — call back the same day or the date is gone.

Your site also carries a Terms of Service and a Privacy Policy for your customers, generated from your business details. The Legal section of the admin is where your own clauses go, and in this trade they matter: the clock policy, the minimum, the cancellation window, the list of what you will not move, and how a damage claim is reported and handled.

Licences and insurance

This is orientation, not advice, and the rules vary enormously by state and by city. Moving is the trade in this catalogue where getting it wrong is most expensive, because there is a federal layer as well as a state one. Check what applies where you live before you take money.

  • Business registration. A registered name and a local business licence, as with any trade. This is the easy part.
  • Household goods authority. Moving people's belongings across a state line brings federal motor carrier registration into play — you will hear it called a USDOT number and operating authority — along with prescribed paperwork and disclosures you must give the customer. Staying entirely inside your own state is generally a state matter instead, and many states license, permit or tariff intrastate household-goods movers. Find out which applies to the work you intend to do, before you advertise it.
  • The truck and the driver. Weight ratings decide licence class, medical certification, inspections and marking requirements, and the line moves depending on what you are pulling and how loaded you are. Check the rating on the door jamb of the specific truck against your state's thresholds rather than assuming a box truck is exempt.
  • Insurance, and the difference between insurance and valuation. General liability, commercial auto, and cargo cover on the customer's goods are three separate things. Separately, the liability level a mover offers on the goods is a disclosure obligation in regulated interstate work, and it is not the same as an insurance policy. Use a broker who writes movers and can explain both.
  • Workers' compensation. Moving is heavy physical work and comp becomes a live question with your first employee. It is not a cheap class of work to cover, so get the quote before you hire rather than after.
  • Employees versus contractors. Crews who use your truck, your pads and your schedule generally look like employees. Classification is heavily enforced in labour trades, and calling a mover a contractor does not make him one.
  • Sales tax. Whether moving labour is taxable, and whether packing materials are treated differently from the service, varies by state. Ask an accountant once, early.

Do not claim on your site that you are licensed or insured until you are, and never display a DOT or authority number that is not yours. In this trade those numbers are public, searchable, and operating under somebody else's is treated far more seriously than an exaggeration.
