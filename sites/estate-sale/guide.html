Your first week

An estate sale is rarely a happy occasion. Somebody died, or moved into care, or finally admitted the house is too much. The family calling you is usually an executor or an adult child, and they are handling their parents' belongings for the last time. Handling that well is most of the job. The tables and the price stickers are the easy part.

  1. Write your terms before you walk your first house. One page, plain English: the commission, the minimum, when the family gets the settlement sheet and the money, and — the one everybody forgets — exactly what happens to what does not sell. Almost every dispute in this trade is an argument about the leftovers that nobody wrote down.
  2. Decide what you will not handle. Firearms and ammunition, vehicles with title questions, prescription medication, anything a hazmat contractor should be touching. Write the list, keep it in your folder, and have the name of somebody you refer that work to. Saying "not us, but call him" builds more trust than taking it on.
  3. Learn to read sold prices, not asking prices. Pick thirty things in your own house and price each one against completed sales, not listings. Anyone can find what somebody hopes to get. Knowing what things actually changed hands for is the skill the family is paying for.
  4. Assemble the kit. Folding tables and cloths, a lockable glass case for small valuables, sticker guns in two colours, a jeweller's loupe, a gram scale, clip lamps and extension cords for dark basements, moving blankets, a dolly, a cash box, a card reader, and a roll of blue painter's tape for taping off everything that is not for sale. The tape is not optional — fixtures, the neighbour's ladder and the family's own boxes all get taped and labelled before strangers walk in.
  5. Run one sale at cost. A friend downsizing, a relative's garage. Do the whole process — walkthrough, sort, stage, price, advertise, sale, haul-out, settlement sheet — and time each part. You will discover that staging takes days, not hours, and it is much better to discover that on a job where nobody is paying you.
  6. Build your photo set and your listings. Estate-sale shoppers find sales on the sale-listing sites, and they decide from photographs. Shoot the staged rooms from the doorway with the lights on and the curtains open, then shoot the twenty best individual items on a clean cloth. Those same photos go on your own site under Admin, Photos.

What to charge

Your site ships a percentage model: a commission of 35% and a $1,500 minimum. Both figures are yours to change in the admin. They are a starting shape for your market, not a recommendation.

The commission is what makes this trade honest. You do not know what a house will bring, the family does not know, and nobody who tells them otherwise should be trusted. A percentage means you are paid for the same outcome they are, and you can say that out loud at the kitchen table. What you never do is guess a total in front of them. Not a range, not a "houses like this usually". You have no way to know and it will be quoted back to you.

The minimum is what stops a small house eating a fortnight. The work of staging, researching, advertising and staffing a sale barely shrinks when the contents do. The minimum is a floor, not a fee — you charge the greater of the two, and you say so before you sign.

Put the unsold items in writing, every time: who decides what gets donated, who does the hauling, whether the clean-out is inside the commission or billed separately, what the deadline is for the family to come back for something, and who receives the donation receipt. Say it at the walkthrough and print it in the agreement. This one paragraph prevents nearly every argument this trade produces.

Price the clean-out separately and say so early. Emptying a house after the sale is a truck, a crew and a landfill ticket. Families almost always want it. Quote it as its own line rather than absorbing it and resenting it on the Monday.

If you ever buy contents outright, say what you are doing. A buyout is a different transaction and your interests stop being aligned the moment money changes hands that way. Offer it as an alternative, in writing, alongside the commissioned sale — never as a favour discovered halfway through the walkthrough.

Finding customers

This trade has two audiences and they are completely different people. The family hires you once, maybe twice in a lifetime. The shoppers come to every sale you run, all year. You have to market to both, and most operators only remember the first.

  • The referral trades. This is where the work comes from. Probate and elder-law attorneys, realtors who list vacant and inherited houses, senior move managers, retirement communities, hospice social workers, and the junk-haul firms who get called for jobs that should have been a sale. Introduce yourself in person, bring photographs of a staged house and a copy of your one-page terms. What they need to know is that you will not embarrass them in front of a grieving client. Say that plainly.
  • The sale-listing sites. Shoppers, dealers and pickers plan their weekend from those listings, and they choose on photographs. List early in the week, post a lot of pictures, and lead with the things collectors drive for — the tools, the jewellery, the mid-century furniture, the signed art. A thin listing gets a thin crowd, and a thin crowd is a poor settlement sheet.
  • Your own buyer list. Collect emails or phone numbers at the checkout table of every sale and send one message a week: where the next sale is, what is in it, three photographs. Those regulars turn up in the first hour and buy the difficult things. Building that list is the most durable asset in this business, and it belongs to you, not to a listing site.

The sale itself also sells the next job. Half the people walking through a house on a Saturday have a parent with a house of their own. Keep cards at checkout, keep the sale calm and orderly, and hand a card to any neighbour who comes over to see what is going on.

Running the work

Discretion is the product. You will open drawers containing letters, medical papers, cash in a coat pocket, a photograph the family has never seen. None of it is a story you tell. Everything of value or of a personal nature is bagged, labelled with the room it came from, and handed to your point of contact before the sale — documented, in front of them. Do that once and the family will recommend you for twenty years.

Never take anything home. Not the thing they said you could have, not the thing nobody wanted. If a family insists, put it on the settlement sheet at a price and pay for it like anybody else. The appearance of it is worse than the value of it, and in a small town appearance is all there is.

Work the house room by room. Like with like, everything up off the floor, cabinets open, lamps on in dark corners. Check every pocket, every book, every tin. The kitchen becomes glassware, the garage becomes tools, the bedroom becomes linens and jewellery. A sorted house prices far faster and reads as competent to everyone who walks in.

The family should not be there on sale day. Say it kindly and say it early: watching strangers haggle over your mother's dishes is the worst hour of anybody's week, and a family member on the floor undoes your pricing within twenty minutes. Offer them a private walkthrough the evening before instead, so they see the house staged and beautiful rather than picked over.

Control the door. One way in past a person, one way out past the checkout table. Cap how many are inside at once and run a numbered list outside if there is a queue. Small valuables live in the locked case and come out one at a time. Post your discount schedule — half price on the last day, or whatever you choose — before the sale opens, so the whole weekend is not a negotiation.

Mind the street. Warn the neighbours a few days ahead, keep driveways clear, and take your signs down the same evening. A sale that ruins a cul-de-sac on a Saturday is remembered longer than one that went well.

Settle fast. Itemised sheet, in the format you promised, within the number of days you promised, with the payment. Hold the client's money separately from your own operating account so that deadline is never a cashflow question.

Using your site

Your admin is at /admin on your own address. Everything you change there is live within a minute or two.

  • Photos. Replace the hero with a staged room of your own as soon as you have run one sale. Doorway angle, lights on, curtains open, nothing on the floor. The stock photo that ships is honest placeholder work and nothing more.
  • Pricing. The commission and the minimum are editable, and the note beneath them is yours to rewrite. Keep the note about what the fee covers — staffing, staging, pricing, running the sale — because that is the paragraph families read twice.
  • Details and hours. Walkthrough times matter more than office hours here. "Walkthroughs weekday evenings, sales Friday and Saturday" tells a working executor exactly when to call you.
  • Reviews. Empty until you add them, deliberately — the site ships with nothing invented on it. Add real ones only, with the family's written permission, and ask whether they want their name on it. Many will not, and that is the trade you are in.
  • Marketing kit. Generates a social image and a printable flyer with a QR code pointing at your site. The flyer is what you leave with an attorney, a realtor or a retirement community office.
  • Leads. Every enquiry from your booking form is emailed to you and kept as a running list. Return these fast — a family calling about a house is usually calling three companies the same afternoon.

Your site also carries a Terms of Service and a Privacy Policy for your customers, generated from your business details. The Legal section of the admin is where you add your own clauses — and this is the trade where you should use it. Your unsold-items policy, your settlement timeline and your list of what you will not sell belong on the page as well as in the signed agreement.

Licences and insurance

This is orientation, not advice, and the rules vary enormously by state and by city. Check what applies where you live before you take money.

  • Business registration. Most places want a registered business name and a local business licence before you trade. Quick, cheap, first.
  • Sales tax. An estate sale is retail selling, so tax is a live question in most states — whether you need a seller's permit, whether you collect on the family's behalf, and whether an "occasional sale" exemption covers a household but not the company running sales every weekend. This is the thing operators in this trade most often get wrong. Ask an accountant once, early, before your first sale rather than after your tenth.
  • Who actually has authority to sell. Confirm the person hiring you is the owner, or the executor or personal representative with the paperwork to prove it. Get one named point of contact in writing, and be careful with estates where several heirs each have an opinion — that is where the trouble in this trade lives.
  • Things that cannot simply be put on a table. Firearms and ammunition, prescription and controlled medication, ivory and items from protected species, certain feathers and taxidermy, recalled cribs and car seats, counterfeit goods. Some are restricted, some are outright illegal to sell, and the categories differ by state as well as federally. Learn the list that applies where you work, set those items aside at the walkthrough, and tell the family why.
  • Pricing is not appraisal. A formal appraisal for probate, tax or insurance purposes is a credentialed exercise and a different document from your sale pricing. Do not call your pricing an appraisal, and refer families who need one to somebody qualified.
  • Insurance and bonding. You are inviting crowds into a house you do not own and handling other people's property and money. Talk to a broker about general liability and about cover for goods in your care, and ask what bonding means in your state — attorneys and property managers frequently ask for it.
  • Workers' compensation. The moment you put a second person on a crew this becomes a question, and the rules differ sharply by state, including for casual weekend help.

Do not claim on your site that you are licensed, insured or bonded until you are, and do not describe yourself as an appraiser or a certified anything you have not earned. Those claims are checkable, and the professionals who refer you work will check them.
