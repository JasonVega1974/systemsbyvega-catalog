Your first week

Holiday lighting is the most seasonal business in the catalog. Almost all of the money is decided between September and the third week of November, and a week spent waiting in October is a week you cannot get back. Do these in order.

  1. Decide what you will not do. Write it down before your first call: no roofs above two storeys without staging, no tile or slate, no electrical work beyond an existing exterior outlet. It is far easier to say this at the start than to reverse it from a ladder in December.
  2. Price three houses on paper. Take three homes on your own street, measure the roofline you would light with a measuring wheel or a phone map, and work out what you would charge. You need to be able to quote without hesitating.
  3. Buy for one house, not ten. One spool of C9 lead wire, a bag of bulbs, a box of shingle tabs and gutter clips, a timer. You are buying enough to do the first job and to have something real to photograph.
  4. Light your own house, or a friend's. This is your entire portfolio for the first month and it costs you materials only. Photograph it at dusk, not at night — the sky still has colour then and the bulbs read warm against it.
  5. Put those photos on your site. Admin, Photos, replace the hero. The stock photo that ships with the site is honest placeholder work; a real local roofline you can point at is worth more than any of it.
  6. Tell fifty people you are doing it. Neighbours, the school run, your church, your old trade contacts. In a seasonal business the first year is almost entirely people who already know your name.
  7. Book the installs, then buy the rest. Order materials against jobs you have said yes to. Buying a garage full of stock in September and hoping is how people lose money in this trade.

What to charge

Your site ships with three tiers: a small home, a large home, and commercial by quote. Change the figures to your market — they are a starting shape, not a recommendation, and the right number depends on what your town pays.

Price the season, not the day. The single most useful thing about this trade is that one price can cover design, install, every service call, takedown and storage. It stops the argument about whether a blown bulb in December is billable, and it is the reason a customer comes back next year instead of shopping around.

The mistake almost everyone makes first: quoting the install and forgetting the takedown. January work is real work — cold, short days, and no new money coming in. If takedown is not inside your price, you will resent it by the second week.

Charge for the walk if you are driving far. A free quote inside your town is a reasonable cost of doing business. A free quote forty minutes away is a day gone if they say no.

Quote in writing before you order anything. Custom colours, unusual roof pitch and anything above two storeys are separate lines, not goodwill.

Finding customers

Three channels do nearly all the work in this trade. They are not the same three as a year-round service business, because your buying window is six weeks long.

  • The street you just lit. The strongest channel by a wide margin. When you finish a house, the neighbours can see it from their windows. Put a small yard sign out while you work, knock the four nearest doors before you leave, and hand over a card. Nothing else you do converts like a lit roofline forty feet away.
  • Nextdoor and local Facebook groups, in September and October. Post the photographs of the house you lit, say which town you cover, and say when your calendar closes. Do not post in December asking for work; by then the people who wanted lights have them.
  • Property managers and small commercial. Storefronts, an HOA entrance, a dental practice, a car lot. They book earlier than homeowners, they renew without being asked, and one of them is worth several houses. Walk in during business hours in early autumn with photographs and a card.

Two things worth doing once and keeping: a spreadsheet of every house you lit with the design and the runs you cut, and a note in your calendar for late August to phone every one of them. A second season is a phone call, not a sale.

Running the work

Equipment you actually need. A stable extension ladder rated well above your own weight plus tools, a ladder stabiliser that stands the top off the gutter, commercial-grade C9 lead wire and bulbs, shingle tabs and gutter clips, a bulb tester, wire cutters, a headlamp, and gloves that still let you feel a clip. Everything else can wait.

Cut and label the runs. Measure each roofline, cut that run to length, and label it — "front gable", "garage eave". Coil each labelled run into its own bag at takedown. The second year on that house becomes a morning instead of a day, and that is the whole economics of this business.

Never staple. Clips only. A staple through lead wire is a fire risk and a hole in somebody's roof, and it is the one thing that will end you in a small town.

Weather is a safety call, not a preference. Iced roofs and high wind mean you do not go up, and no customer is worth arguing about it. Tell them the same day and rebook at the front of the queue — people are far more reasonable about this than installers expect.

Service calls. Expect them, and price them in. A run goes dark, a timer drifts, wind pulls a section loose. Going out the same week, without a bill, is what makes somebody book you again — the visit costs you an hour and buys the next season.

Raising prices. Do it between seasons, in writing, when you send the August phone-round. Never mid-season, and never on a customer standing in front of you.

Using your site

Your admin is at /admin on your own address. Everything you change there is live within a minute or two.

  • Photos. Replace the hero with your own work as soon as you have any. Shoot at dusk while the sky still has colour.
  • Pricing. The three tiers and every line in them are yours to rewrite. Say what is included so the season-long promise is on the page and not just in your head.
  • Details and hours. Put your real seasonal hours in. "Books September to November, installs November, service through December" tells someone more than "Mon-Fri 9-5".
  • Reviews. Empty until you add them, deliberately — the site ships with no invented reviews on it. Add real ones as you earn them, with the customer's permission.
  • Marketing kit. Generates a social image and a printable flyer with a QR code pointing at your site. Print the flyer for the doors you knock after an install.
  • Leads. Every enquiry from your booking form is emailed to you and kept as a running list, so nothing is lost when an inbox gets busy.

Your site also carries a Terms of Service and a Privacy Policy for your customers, generated from your business details. You can add your own clauses — a deposit rule, a cancellation window — in the Legal section of the admin.

Licences and insurance

This is orientation, not advice, and the rules vary enormously by state and by city. Check what applies where you live before you take money.

  • Business registration. Most places want a registered business name and a local business licence before you trade. This is usually quick and cheap and is the first thing to do.
  • Liability insurance. You are working at height on other people's property. Talk to a broker about general liability cover before your first paid job, and be honest with them about ladder work — a policy bought on a vague description is a policy that may not answer when you need it.
  • Workers' compensation. The moment you put a second person on a ladder, this becomes a question. The rules differ sharply by state, including for casual seasonal help.
  • Electrical work. Plugging into an existing exterior outlet is generally not licensed work. Adding a circuit, a new outlet, or anything inside the panel generally is. Know where that line sits in your state and stay well on the safe side of it.
  • Sales tax. Whether a seasonal service is taxable varies by state, and whether the materials are taxable can differ from the labour. Ask an accountant once, early, rather than guessing for a whole season.

Do not claim on your site that you are licensed, insured or bonded until you are. That claim is checkable, and in a small town it will be checked.
