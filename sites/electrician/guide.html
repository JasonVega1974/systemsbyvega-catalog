Your first week

You can already pull a service, calculate a load and make up a panel that an inspector will not comment on. That is not what this week is for. This week is about becoming findable, quotable and reachable — the three things a good electrician working alone is usually worst at.

  1. Write down the work you take and the work you hand off. Residential service, small commercial, panel changes, EV and generator work — pick. Then name the jobs you will refer out: a service upgrade needing a new mast, three-phase, fire alarm, anything you would be learning on somebody else's money. The list takes ten minutes and it saves you an entire bad month.
  2. Sort your permit and utility paperwork before the first job. Get the login for the permit portal, learn how your jurisdiction wants a panel change-out filed, and find out how a meter release and a reconnect actually get scheduled where you work. One afternoon on the phone. Doing this on the morning of a shutdown is how a one-day job becomes three.
  3. Set the diagnostic fee and what it applies to. The site ships a $95 service call — a first-hour diagnostic, credited toward the repair if they go ahead that day. Decide whether that is your number and whether the credit is same-day only, then say it on the phone every time.
  4. Price the four jobs you can quote from the driveway. The site's pricing block has four rows, and they are the four that people shop: troubleshooting, EV charger, generator hookup, panel upgrade. Work out your real ranges — including the permit, the inspection trip and the drywall you will not be repairing — and put them up.
  5. Stock the van for what fails, not for what is interesting. AFCI and GFCI breakers for the two panel brands the houses around you actually have, receptacles, devices, wire, boxes, a spare of whatever you used last. If your area is full of one obsolete panel brand, know now where you can get a breaker for it.
  6. Call the electrical contractors in town. Not for a job — to be their overflow. A shop booked three weeks out turns away small service calls every day and would rather hand them to a licensed van than lose the customer. This fills a solo calendar faster than any advertising you can buy.
  7. Photograph one finished panel. Dead front off, breakers dressed, conductors bent square, directory typed and legible. That single photo tells another electrician you are careful and tells a homeowner nothing at all — which is fine, because the homeowner is looking at whether you own a camera and a clean shirt.

What to charge

Your site ships four price rows, each a range rather than a fixed figure: troubleshooting and repairs, EV charger installs, generator hookups, panel upgrades. Rewrite them to your market. Ranges are the honest shape here — the real cost of the job is decided by what is behind the wall, and everyone in this trade knows it.

Quote the range on the phone, the number after you look. Say the range plainly, say what moves it, and book the visit. "Between this and this, depending on whether the panel has room and how far the run is" is a professional answer. "It depends" is not.

Price the unknowns as their own line. Aluminium branch wiring, a service with no equipment ground, knob-and-tube in the attic, a panel buried behind finished drywall, a meter base that has been rained into for a decade. Any of these turns a clean job into a different job. Name them in the quote as conditions that change the price, before you find them.

Never quote a panel change without taking the cover off and looking outside. The number is decided by the service conductors, the meter base, the grounding electrode, the mast, and whether the utility needs to be involved. A quote given from a photo the customer texted you is a quote you will be renegotiating in their driveway with the power off.

Permit and inspection time is billable and gets its own line. Filing, waiting for the inspector, the return trip for a correction. Burying it in the labour rate means the customer thinks it is free and you think you are slow.

Do not compete on being cheapest. The EV customer holding three quotes is usually comparing three different jobs — one includes a load calculation and a panel that can actually take the circuit, one is a 6-gauge run to a full panel and a hope. Explain the difference in one sentence in your quote. That sentence wins the job more often than a discount does.

Finding customers

Residential electrical work mostly arrives because somebody else told the customer to call an electrician. Your job is to be the name in that person's phone.

  • Home inspectors. The best single referral source in residential electrical, and the one most electricians never call. Their reports create the work: double-taps, an ungrounded receptacle, a federal-era panel, no AFCI where a buyer's lender wants one. Every one of those items needs an electrician on a closing deadline. Take three inspectors to breakfast, ask what they flag most, and be the one who turns up in two days with a written letter of what was corrected.
  • The trades whose work needs a circuit. HVAC contractors need disconnects and dedicated circuits for every changeout. Remodelers need rough-in and a licensed name on the permit. Pool, spa, generator and solar installers need a service and a panel that can carry what they just sold. Plumbers need tankless-heater feeds. Each of these people has the same problem — the electrician they used stopped answering.
  • Property managers and small commercial. Parking lot lights, exit and emergency lighting, a retrofit that has to happen after closing, breakers that trip in the same suite every month. It is scheduled, invoiced, repeat work, and night or early-morning access is a feature you can charge for rather than a hardship.

One habit worth starting now: after every job, photograph the finished panel directory and text it to the customer. It costs you nothing, it is genuinely useful to them, and it is the thing they forward to a neighbour when the neighbour's lights flicker.

Running the work

Tell them exactly when the power goes off, and for how long. Home offices, medical equipment, aquariums, somebody working nights and sleeping days. A shutdown that arrives as a surprise is the complaint, not the outage itself. Confirm the window the day before.

Test before you touch and again before you leave. Write the readings on the invoice — voltage at the panel, at the affected circuit, the ground and neutral you verified. It takes a minute and it is the difference between a service call and a diagnosis somebody can act on later.

Photograph every wall before you open it. And keep the cuts small and rectangular, in a place a patch can be made honest. Say plainly in the quote that you leave clean holes and do not do drywall finish, then leave clean holes.

Leave the directory legible or the job is not finished. A typed, accurate panel schedule is the thing every future electrician, inspector and homeowner sees first, and it is the cheapest possible signature on your work. Handwritten in pencil, half-abbreviated, is how the last guy left it — which is exactly why you were called.

Schedule the inspection when you book the job. And never let a wall close before the rough is signed off, no matter who on the site is in a hurry. You are the one whose licence is on the permit.

Change orders in writing before the extra work, not after. A text message saying "found aluminium branch wiring on this circuit, adding pigtails, adds this much — reply yes" is a change order. It has ended more disputes than any contract clause.

Keep the truck stock honest. Restock at the end of the day, not at the start of the next one. A solo van lives or dies on not driving to the supply house at ten in the morning with a customer waiting.

Using your site

Your admin is at /admin on your own address. Everything you save there is live within a minute or two.

  • Photos. Two slots: the hero and you. Swap the hero for your own work as soon as you have a panel you are proud of. The owner photo matters more than electricians expect — the whole promise of this site is that one person quotes the job and does the job, and a face is what makes that believable.
  • Pricing. Four rows, and on this build each row is a label and a figure — there is no bullet list under them. That means the label has to carry the meaning: "Panel upgrade — most 100A to 200A changeouts" says more than "Panel upgrade" and costs you nothing.
  • Details. The owner block is where you write who you are in a short paragraph — years in the trade, the licence class, what you like doing. This build also has social links, so put the profile where you actually post finished work and leave the rest empty.
  • Reviews. Empty by design; the site ships with nothing invented. Add real ones as you earn them, with permission. The best time to ask is standing in the kitchen the moment the thing they lived with for two years stops doing it.
  • Marketing kit. Generates a social post and a printable flyer, each with a QR code to your site. The flyer is what you leave with a property manager or pin in a supply house.
  • Leads. Enquiries from the form go to the inquiry email you set in the Business section. The first time you change that address the form service sends it an activation link — click it, or the enquiries stop without any warning.

The breaker panel on the home page is interactive: a visitor clicks a breaker and sees what that circuit feeds. It is there to make a homeowner comfortable with a panel rather than frightened of it, which is the conversation you are about to have on the phone anyway. Leave it alone and let it do that work.

Your site also carries a Terms of Service and a Privacy Policy for your customers, generated from your business details. Add your own clauses in the Legal section — how change orders are approved, what you do and do not patch, when payment is due.

Licences and insurance

Electrical is one of the trades where the licence decides who may legally do the work at all, and you are presumably already inside it. This is orientation only, not advice, and the details vary by state and by the local authority having jurisdiction. Confirm all of it with your own board.

  • Licence class versus contractor registration. States typically license individuals in tiers — apprentice, journeyman, master — and separately register the contractor who may sell work to the public and pull permits in their own name. A journeyman card alone does not always let you take the money. Find out which registration your board wants for the work you intend to sell.
  • Permits, the AHJ and the adopted code cycle. Which code edition your jurisdiction has adopted, and its local amendments, is a real question with a real answer — get it from the building department rather than from a forum.
  • Your licence number in advertising. Many states require it on advertising, vehicles and written proposals. Check what yours requires and put it where required, including on this site.
  • Bond and insurance. Contractor registration often requires a surety bond. General liability is a separate policy, and a personal auto policy generally will not cover a work van — ask a broker about commercial auto, and be accurate with them about what you do.
  • Workers' compensation. The moment you have an apprentice or a helper this becomes a live question, and the rules differ by state, including for part-time and casual labour.
  • Specialty endorsements. Low voltage, fire alarm, solar and certain commercial work are frequently licensed separately from a general electrical licence. Do not sell them on the strength of the card you have.

Do not write "licensed, bonded and insured" on your site until all three are true and current. A state licence lookup is public, it takes a customer thirty seconds, and the one who checks is always the one you are already having a problem with.
