Your first week

A collision shop is the heaviest business in this catalogue to stand up, and almost none of the first week is about finding customers. Wrecked cars find shops. What decides whether you survive the first six months is the building, the paint account and the paperwork. Do these in order.

  1. Decide what leaves the property. Structural work on a unibody without measuring equipment, aluminium-bodied vehicles, ADAS calibration after a windscreen or a bumper cover comes off, wheel alignment. Write the sublet list on the wall. Every shop sublets something; the ones that get hurt are the ones that decide halfway through a repair.
  2. Open the paint account first. Your jobber sets up the mixing bank, the colour tools and the training that comes with the line. It takes longer than you expect and nothing gets refinished until it is done, so start it before you start anything else.
  3. Sort waste and air before the booth runs. Solvent, used thinner, contaminated rags, booth filters and the spent coatings themselves all have rules attached to them. Find the licensed hauler and answer the permit question in week one, not after somebody knocks on the roll-up door.
  4. Open three parts accounts. A dealer wholesale counter, an aftermarket distributor and a recycler. Ask each one what time of day they deliver. Their delivery time is your completion date, and you will be promising completion dates to people who are without a car.
  5. Learn to write the estimate before you learn to sell it. Line items, labour hours, paint and materials, parts type, and a line noting what teardown may find. An estimate an adjuster cannot read is an estimate an adjuster rewrites.
  6. Set the photo intake. The VIN plate, the paint code sticker in the door jamb, four corners, one wide shot and one tight shot of the damage. Most first contacts now arrive as photos on a phone, and a photo estimate without the paint code is half an estimate.
  7. Repair one car and photograph every gate. Teardown, bare metal, the sealed booth, the polish. The reveal slider on your site is a drawing. A real sequence out of your own bay replaces it and beats it.

What to charge

Your site publishes no prices, and that is deliberate. Collision is quoted per vehicle, and the admin's pricing panel says exactly that instead of showing you rows to fill in. Resist the pull to post a "dents from" figure. One number on a page invites an argument you cannot win from a photograph.

Set your rates once, before the first estimate. A collision estimate is not one price. It is hours at a body rate, hours at a refinish rate, hours at a frame rate where that applies, paint and materials, and parts. Decide each of those deliberately and then quote from them every time. Shops get into trouble by inventing a rate per car depending on how the customer looks.

The estimate is not the bill, and everybody should know that on day one. Teardown finds what the outside of a panel was hiding. The second document is a supplement, it is completely normal, and the customer should hear that word from you at drop-off rather than from an adjuster later. Blueprint properly at teardown and you write one supplement. Guess, and you write five.

Charge for teardown when the car does not proceed. Taking a bumper assembly apart to find out what is behind it is real work. Say so in writing before you start, so the customer who decides to trade the car in instead is not surprised.

Never waive a deductible: a customer will ask, sometimes in a friendly way, for you to bury their deductible somewhere in the estimate. That is treated as insurance fraud in many places, it is checkable against your own paperwork, and it has cost people their shops. The answer is no, said early and pleasantly, before it turns into a negotiation.

Aftermarket, recycled and OEM parts price differently and sit differently in a panel gap. Put the parts type on the estimate in writing, explain it in plain words at approval, and let the customer choose. The argument you are avoiding is the one that otherwise happens at pickup.

Finding customers

Nobody wakes up wanting a body shop. Your customers arrive at the worst moment of their week, usually pointed at you by somebody else, and that somebody is who you should be spending your time on.

  • Local insurance agents, not adjusters. The agent is who a driver phones first, still standing at the kerb, and the agent's answer to "who should I take it to" travels for years. Adjusters rotate through territories; the agent with the storefront on Main Street does not. Walk in during quiet hours with photographs of finished work and a card.
  • Tow operators. The driver hooking up a wrecked car often decides where it spends its first night, and a car sitting in your lot is a repair you are likely to write. Know the local operators by name, be reachable after hours, and never make a tow driver wait on you.
  • Shops that do not do body work. The mechanical garage down the road turns away every dented panel that rolls in, and so does the tyre place and the glass van. So do small used-car lots that need recondition work before a car goes on the front line. That last one is steady, unemotional and invoiced, which is a useful counterweight to claim work.

When hail comes through, out-of-town crews set up in a parking lot for three weeks and then leave. Your whole argument against them is that you are still here next spring when a repair needs looking at again. Say that plainly and locally; do not run them down.

One thing worth repeating until it is reflex: the choice of shop belongs to the customer. Your site already says so. Say it the same calm way on the phone, without making the insurer sound like an enemy, and you will keep cars that would otherwise drift somewhere else.

Running the work

Blueprint at teardown. Pull the car down far enough to see everything the impact touched, then write the whole repair at once: parts, paint, sublet, hours, completion date. A car taken apart in stages generates a supplement every time somebody finds something new, and each supplement is another wait for approval with your bay full.

Do not tear a car down until the parts are on the shelf: a stripped car waiting on a back-ordered quarter panel is a hostage. It occupies a bay you cannot sell, it cannot be driven home, and there is nothing to tell the owner except sorry. Order, confirm the parts arrived and are the right ones, then pick up a wrench.

Bag and tag by panel. Every fastener, clip and bracket goes into a labelled bag for the panel it came off. Reassembly is where a good repair turns into a rattle, and a rattle brings the car back.

Never spray to a code alone. Paint fades on the car and the code was written at the factory. Spray out a card, let it down against the actual panel in daylight, tint until it disappears, and blend into the neighbouring panels so there is no edge to find. A colour that is nearly right is worse than one that is obviously wrong, because the customer finds it a week later in a car park.

Booth discipline is most of the job. Filters changed on a schedule you write down, air pressure checked, tack rag before base, and nobody walking through in a wool jumper. Dust is the difference between a repair that reads as factory and one that reads as bodywork.

Calibration is not optional any more. If a windscreen, bumper cover, mirror or grille came off a car with cameras and radar behind them, find out what needs recalibrating, get it done, and put who did it and when on the paperwork. This is the most common way a modern collision repair goes wrong after the customer has driven away.

Cycle time is your floor space. A car standing still is a bay you cannot use. Phone the day you learn about a delay, not the day the customer phones to ask, and have a written storage policy so a finished car nobody collects does not become your problem forever. Then walk every finished car with its owner outside, in daylight, panel by panel, before the keys change hands.

Using your site

Your admin is at /admin on your own address. Everything you change there is live within a minute or two.

  • Photos. Replace the hero with a panel out of your own booth as soon as you have one worth showing. There are owner, before and after slots as well. Shoot finished paint outdoors under an overcast sky rather than under booth lights, which flatter everything.
  • The repair reveal. The drag divider on your home page is an illustration of the five stages, not a photograph of a real car, and the note underneath it says so. Leave that note where it is. It is the difference between an explainer and a claim about work you have not done yet.
  • Pricing. There is none, on purpose, and the admin's pricing panel tells you so rather than offering you rows to fill. Everything you would have put there belongs on the estimate you hand to a person.
  • Details and hours. Hours matter more here than in most trades, because people turn up unannounced with a crumpled car and no plan. Say plainly when somebody can walk in for an estimate.
  • Reviews. Empty until you add them, deliberately. The site ships with no invented reviews on it. Add real ones as you earn them, with the customer's permission, and ask at the walkaround when the car looks its best.
  • Marketing kit. Generates a social image and a printable flyer with a QR code pointing at your site. The flyer is for the parts counter, the tow yard and the insurance agent's front desk.
  • Leads. Every enquiry from your form is emailed to you and kept as a running list. Collision enquiries arrive with photographs attached and at odd hours, which is exactly when a fast reply wins the car.

Your site also carries a Terms of Service and a Privacy Policy for your customers, generated from your business details. The Legal section of the admin is where your own clauses go: authorisation to repair, your storage policy, the teardown fee, and the wording of your workmanship promise.

Licences and insurance

This is orientation, not advice, and collision repair is one of the more regulated trades in this catalogue. The rules vary enormously by state and by city. Check what applies where you are before you take money.

  • Repair facility registration. Many states register collision and auto body shops specifically, separately from a general business licence, and attach rules about written estimates and written authorisation before work begins. Find out whether yours does, first.
  • Insurance claim handling. What a shop may say and do around a claim is separately regulated in many states, including rules on deductibles, referral arrangements and what may be offered as an inducement. Read your state's rules once, properly, rather than picking them up from other shops.
  • Air quality and coatings. Spray booths, ventilation and the solvents in modern coatings sit under environmental rules, often at state or local level, and a permit can be part of it. Ask before the booth runs, not after.
  • Hazardous waste. Waste paint, thinner, solvent, contaminated rags, spent filters, and the used oil, coolant and undeployed airbag units that come off a wrecked car all have handling and disposal rules attached. A licensed hauler and a paper trail is the shape of the answer nearly everywhere.
  • Painter safety. The isocyanates in two-pack clear are a respiratory hazard with specific respirator and exposure requirements attached to them. This one is worth reading properly for your own sake, whoever is holding the gun.
  • Insurance on your side. General liability is the baseline. The one shops forget is cover for customers' vehicles in your care, custody and control, which is a separate thing from liability and is what answers when a car in your lot is damaged or stolen. Talk to a broker who has written a body shop before.
  • Workers' compensation. The moment there is a second person in the shop this becomes a question, and the rules differ sharply by state.
  • Liens and abandoned vehicles. Every state has a process for an unpaid repair and for a car nobody comes back for. Learn yours before you need it, because eventually you will need it.

Do not claim on your site that you are licensed, insured, certified or approved until each of those is true. The certifications in this trade are real, checkable credentials held by named people and named facilities, and a shop advertising one it does not hold gets found out quickly.
