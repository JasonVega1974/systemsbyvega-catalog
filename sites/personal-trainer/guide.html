Your first week

There is no licence to train people, which sounds like an advantage and is actually the hardest thing about starting. Nothing external forces you to be competent, insured or careful, so the whole burden of proof lands on you. Spend the first week building the things a client cannot see and an insurer will ask for.

  1. Hold a current certification before you take money. Personal training is generally not a state-licensed trade, but check your own state before you assume it. Certification is what gyms, insurers and informed clients ask to see either way. Pick a body with independent accreditation rather than a weekend course with a nice logo, and keep the certification current — an expired card is worse than no card, because "certified" then becomes a claim you cannot back.
  2. Get CPR and AED certified, and keep it current. Every facility will ask for the card, most insurers will ask for the card, and it is the one qualification you might genuinely need on a training floor.
  3. Buy liability insurance before the first session, and read what it covers. Ask specifically about the ways you actually plan to work: sessions in a rented gym, sessions in a park, sessions in a client's garage, and online programming for someone you never physically see. Those are four different exposures and a policy written for one does not automatically answer for the others.
  4. Settle where you will train, on paper. A rental or independent-contractor agreement with a gym, a permit if you intend to run sessions on public park land, or a clear-eyed look at insurance and zoning if you are training people at your own house. Do this before you book a client into a location you cannot legally use.
  5. Get the intake and the waiver written properly. A health-history questionnaire covering conditions, medications, injuries, surgeries and an emergency contact, plus a question about medical clearance. And a waiver drafted or reviewed by an attorney in your state — a template pulled off the internet is a template pulled off the internet, and this is the document that matters on the worst day of your career.
  6. Program three real people for free, for a month. Not friends who will be polite — people who will actually show up and tell you when a session was bad. You are buying two things: the practice of writing a program for a body that is not yours, and photographs and video you have permission to use.
  7. Write your scope line on nutrition down, now. Decide in advance the sentence you will say when a client asks you to write them a meal plan. It will be asked in week one, and improvising an answer is how trainers end up somewhere they should not be.

What to charge

Your site ships with three tiers: a small-group session rate, a 1:1 session rate, and a monthly rate for online programming. Rewrite every figure for your own market — but keep them on the page.

Posting the price is a competitive advantage in this trade. Most trainers hide behind "book a free consultation to discuss investment", and everyone who reads that knows what it means. Yours are posted. Leave them posted; the people who would have been scared off by the number were going to be scared off by it later anyway, after wasting an hour of your time.

Small group is the only lever you have. A 1:1 hour is capped at one hour of you, forever, no matter how good you get. A capped small group is the same hour serving several people, and if the cap is small enough it is still coaching rather than a class. That cap is a promise — hold it, because the day it slips to eight the product has changed.

Online is a different product, not a discount. You are selling the programming and the check-in, not the hour. Price it monthly, define exactly what a month includes — how many check-ins, what response time, how many form-check videos — and then hold that line, because unlimited access sold monthly is the fastest way to resent a client.

The cancellation policy you do not enforce is worse than none: your site states a notice window for rescheduling. Enforce it evenly, from the first client, including the friend and including the person you like most. Trainers lose their schedules by making exceptions, and once a client learns that a late cancel costs nothing, your calendar stops being a calendar. Say it kindly, say it once, and do not relitigate it in the moment.

Prepaid blocks are a schedule tool with a catch. Selling sessions ahead fills your week and improves the client's adherence, and it also means you have been paid for work you have not done. Track what is outstanding and remember whose money it still is. Note that in some states how you sell prepaid fitness packages is itself regulated — see the legal section below before you build a package ladder.

Discount the hour, not the work. If you need to fill a ten in the morning, price that slot differently and say why. Cutting your rate to win a client teaches them what your rate really is.

Raising rates. New clients pay the new number from today. Existing clients get written notice and a stated date, and it is fair to hold them at the old rate for a defined period. Do not announce it in the last minute of a session.

Finding customers

Your site is organised around four goals — strength, a first 5K, postpartum, and training over fifty — and that is not decoration. Each of those has a different room full of people in it and a different person who refers them. Chase them separately rather than advertising "personal training" at everybody.

  • Physical therapists, and everyone adjacent to them. The best referral in this trade by a distance. A PT discharges someone who is out of pain but nowhere near ready to train alone, and that person needs supervision from somebody who will not undo six weeks of work. Introduce yourself in person, bring an actual written program so they can see how you think, and tell them explicitly what you will not do with a post-rehab client. They are putting their own reputation on your hands; the refusals are what make you referrable. The same relationship works with pelvic-floor PTs, midwives and OB offices for postpartum clients, where medical clearance comes first and always.
  • Be visible being good at it. If you train in a gym, the floor is your shop window — members watch how you coach, whether you are on your phone, whether your client is doing something that looks thought about. For the running side, the equivalent is the local run club and the small-race volunteer table: pace the back of the group, be the person who talks to the nervous first-timer, and do not sell anything for the first month.
  • Ask an existing client for one name, not for "referrals". "Referrals welcome" gets nothing. "Who do you know who has been saying they want to start?" gets a name. A partner rate that lets somebody bring a friend is the easy version of the same thing, and two people who train together cancel far less than one who trains alone.

Online is a real channel here but only one form of it works: showing the coaching. A short video explaining why you changed somebody's setup, with the reasoning said out loud, does more than any number of motivational posts. Before-and-after photographs of clients need separate written permission every single time, and a body someone sent you privately is not content.

What your site does is convert the referral. Somebody has been told your name and lands on the page: they pick their goal, the programs and the FAQ reframe themselves around it, and the booking form arrives with the goal already attached. That means you know before you call whether you are talking to a nervous first-time lifter or somebody chasing a 5K — start the conversation there.

Running the work

Assess first, load second. The temptation on session one is to make somebody sweat so they feel they got value. Resist it. Watch them squat, hinge, press and walk, find out what they can already do and what hurts, and build from there. A first session that leaves a beginner unable to sit down for three days does not produce a second session.

Write the program by hand and keep every session logged. What was loaded, what moved well, what did not, what you changed. Four weeks later you will need to know exactly what happened, and "I think we did three sets" is not a coaching record. This is also what your site is promising — programs written for the person, not a template with their name on it — so it needs to be true.

Have a plan B for a bad day, every session. A client turns up with a stiff shoulder, four hours of sleep, or a headache. The skill is not cancelling and not pushing through: it is regressing the session on the spot so they still train. A trainer who can redesign a session in ninety seconds keeps clients for years.

Coach in one sentence. Pick the single cue that fixes the biggest problem in the lift, say it, and let them do the rep. Three cues at once is noise, and counting reps out loud is not coaching. Keep the standards physical too — phone away, floor clear, plates racked, spot from where you can actually help. It looks like tidiness and it is really about whether people believe you are paying attention.

Know exactly where your scope ends: you do not diagnose, you do not treat, and you do not write meal plans. If a client reports pain, the exercise stops and they go to a physician or a physical therapist — you can absolutely keep training everything that does not hurt while they do. On food, general information about habits is a different thing from prescribing a diet, and dietetics is a regulated profession in many states with the line drawn by state law rather than by you. Build a relationship with a registered dietitian and refer. Nothing here costs you a client; the trainer who says "that is not mine, here is who to see" reads as more competent, not less.

Waivers and intake are signed before the first rep. No exceptions for a friend, a drop-in, or somebody who came along with a client. If a health history flags something serious, medical clearance comes first — and on the postpartum side that clearance is not negotiable and not something you can talk anyone past.

The first four weeks decide whether they stay. Not the programming — the ordinary things. Being there early, knowing their name and their kid's name, texting back the same day the way your site promises, remembering what they said hurt last Tuesday. Beginners quit because they feel like an appointment, not because the program was wrong.

Use the waitlist rather than overbooking. Your site has a switch that turns the booking form into a waitlist with a note when you are full. Use it. Taking a client into a slot you cannot properly serve costs you two clients — them, and whoever they tell.

Using your site

Your admin is at /admin on your own address. Everything you change there is live within a minute or two.

  • Photos. Two slots plus the gallery tiles. Replace the stock images with your own coaching as soon as you have any: a real bar, a real client mid-set, your actual training space. Get written permission for any photo a client appears in, every time, and never use a client's body as a before-and-after without it. Shoot from the side at chest height — the angle that makes a lift readable, not the one that flatters the room.
  • Packages. The three tiers and every line inside them are yours. Say what is included precisely — the group cap, what a month of online actually buys, whether text coaching is in or out — because those lines are what people compare, and vagueness reads as expensive.
  • Goals. The goal picker drives the programs shown, the proof points and which FAQ entries appear, and it hands the chosen goal to the booking form. Rewrite the four goals to the people you actually want to coach. If you do not train postpartum clients, remove it rather than leaving it there hoping — everything on that picker is a claim that you can coach it well.
  • FAQ and availability. Each FAQ entry is tagged to a goal, so a nervous first-time runner sees the running answers and not the barbell ones — put your real answers to the questions a first phone call actually brings, particularly about experience and about pain. Separately, a setting closes the booking form and turns it into a waitlist with your own note; flip it when you are full rather than letting enquiries land in a calendar with no room in it.
  • Reviews. Empty until you add them, deliberately — nothing invented ships on this site. Add real ones only, with written permission. In fitness the useful review is not "she is great", it is the specific one about being nervous at the start; ask for that one.
  • Marketing kit and leads. The kit generates a social image and a printable flyer with a QR code; the copies worth printing are the ones for a PT clinic's front desk and a run club noticeboard. Every enquiry from the booking form is emailed to you and kept as a running list, with the goal they selected attached. Answer the same day — your site says you text back the same day, so that has to be true.

Your site also carries a Terms of Service and a Privacy Policy for your customers, generated from your business details. Use the Legal section of the admin for your own clauses — the cancellation window, how sessions expire, and a plain statement that training is not medical care and does not replace advice from a physician. Your signed waiver is a separate document and a website disclaimer is not a substitute for it.

Licences and insurance

Read this as orientation only, not as advice. The headline is unusual for this catalog: personal training is not a licensed occupation in any US state. That cuts both ways — there is no licence to obtain, and there is also no licence to claim. Everything else below varies by state and by facility, so check locally before you take a client.

  • Certification is not a licence. Say "certified" only while you hold a current certification, name the body if you name anything, and never phrase it in a way that implies the state has approved you. Choose a certifying body with independent accreditation, because that is what gyms and insurers recognise, and keep up the continuing education that keeps it current.
  • Liability insurance and waivers. Trainer liability cover is the baseline and every facility will ask for a certificate. A waiver and health-history form signed before the first session is the other half — and waivers do not cover everything, their enforceability varies by state, and they do not survive being drafted badly. Get yours written or reviewed by an attorney where you live.
  • Scope of practice — nutrition. Dietetics and nutrition counselling are regulated in many states, and where the line falls between general nutrition information and prescribing an individual diet is set by state law, not by industry custom. Some states are considerably stricter than others. Find out where yours sits, keep well clear of the line, and refer to a registered dietitian.
  • Scope of practice — injury. Assessing, diagnosing or treating an injury is physical therapy or medicine. You train around pain and refer out; you do not rehabilitate, and you do not offer an opinion on what is wrong.
  • Where you train. A gym will want an independent-contractor or rental agreement and proof of insurance. Commercial activity in a public park frequently requires a permit from the parks authority. Training clients at your own home raises zoning questions and homeowner's insurance exclusions that are easy to discover too late.
  • Selling prepaid packages. Several states regulate prepaid fitness or health-club service contracts, with requirements that can include registration, bonding and mandatory cancellation rights for the buyer. Whether that applies to an independent trainer selling session blocks depends on your state's own definitions. Ask before you design a package ladder around large prepayments.
  • Business registration and tax. A registered business name and a local licence are the normal starting point. Whether training services or memberships are taxable varies by state — ask an accountant once, early.
  • Client health information. Intake forms hold health history. Store them securely, keep them out of a shared inbox, and do not repeat anything from one to anybody.
  • If you add a coach. Employee-versus-contractor classification and workers' compensation become live questions the moment somebody else coaches your clients, and the answers are state-specific.

Do not describe yourself on your site as certified, licensed or insured until each one is true and current. There is no such thing as a licensed personal trainer in the United States, so any wording that hints at one is both false and pointless — and a client who checks a certification number and finds it lapsed will tell the gym before they tell you.
