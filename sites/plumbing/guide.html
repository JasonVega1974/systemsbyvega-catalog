Your first week

You already hold the licence and you already know how to solder a joint and set a closet flange. None of that is what this week is about. This week is about who answers the phone, what a call costs before you put the van in gear, and how somebody standing in two inches of water finds you at six in the morning.

  1. Decide your after-hours rule before the first 11pm call. The site ships a 24/7 emergency line. Decide whether that is true, what after-hours costs, and who covers Sunday. A number nobody answers does more damage than no number at all — the customer who gets voicemail at midnight tells people.
  2. Set the trip fee and put it on the page. The site ships an $89 service call, waived if they book the repair. That is a shape, not a recommendation. Pick your own number, and write down exactly what it buys: the drive, the diagnosis, and a written quote.
  3. Write a flat-rate sheet for the twenty calls you actually get. Fill valve, wax ring, angle stop, disposal, cartridge swap, kitchen faucet, tank-to-bowl kit, main line clear, water heater changeout. Price them once at your own table so you are never doing arithmetic on your knees in somebody's bathroom.
  4. Stock the van for your town's housing stock, not for a catalogue. Find out what the tract builders here used — which cartridge brands, whether the neighbourhoods from the eighties are polybutylene, where the galvanized still is. Carry those. A second trip to the supply house eats the job.
  5. Open the accounts and find out who is open Saturday. A supply house account, a card reader in the van, and the name of the counter man who will pull you a fifty-gallon on a weekend. That one relationship is worth more than any tool you will buy this month.
  6. Photograph three finished jobs. A repipe with the runs labelled, a water heater sitting square on a pan with the straps on and the T&P piped down, an under-sink before and after. Put a work light in the cabinet — cabinet interiors photograph as a black hole otherwise.
  7. Phone the trades who already know your work. Builders, property managers, realtors, the HVAC techs you have shared jobsites with. Tell them you are taking your own calls now. The first month is people who have watched you work, not people who found you.

What to charge

Your site ships with three tiers: a service call, a range for standard repairs, and a starting figure for major work — repipes, heaters, sewer lines. Rewrite all three to your market. They are a starting shape, not a recommendation, and the right numbers depend on what your town pays and what your supply house charges you.

Flat rate, not hourly, for repair work. The customer knows the number before you open anything, and you stop being punished for being quick. Hourly billing punishes the plumber who has done a thousand cartridge swaps and rewards the one who is still slow at them. Keep hourly for open-ended work — diagnostics on an old house, chasing a leak through a ceiling — and say so up front.

The mistake nearly everyone makes in their first month on their own: giving a number over the phone to end an uncomfortable call. You have not seen the access, the shutoff, or what the last guy did with a shark bite and a prayer. Give the trip fee and a range, then go and look. A number you take back is worse than a number you never gave.

Quote the diagnosis, then quote the work. A camera down the drain or an hour tracing a slab leak is billable on its own. When you know what is actually there, the repair gets its own written number. Two quotes, in that order, is how you avoid eating the discovery.

Decide the after-hours multiplier when you are calm. Say it out loud on the phone before you drive. Price negotiated at two in the morning with water running is a conversation you will resent by nine.

Say what the warranty covers. The middle tier ships with a 90-day workmanship warranty. Workmanship means your joint, your seal, your install — not the fixture the customer bought online, which carries whatever the manufacturer gives it. Put that distinction on the page and you will never have the argument.

Finding customers

Plumbing splits into two kinds of work that arrive by completely different routes, and you need both. The emergency comes from strangers in a hurry. The steady work comes from people who have your number already.

  • The phone, answered. Emergency plumbing is bought in the first ten minutes. Somebody with a burst supply line calls the first three numbers they see and hires whoever picks up with a real arrival window. Stand up your Google Business Profile in week one — with water on the floor people tap the map pin, not a website. If you are under a house when it rings, call back within ten minutes with a time, not a promise to call back later.
  • The trades that hit the line they cannot cross. HVAC techs run into gas and condensate. Remodelers and handymen run into rough-in. Appliance installers find an angle stop that will not close and a dishwasher they now cannot fit. Restoration companies get called to a flood at 3am and need somebody to stop the water before they can start drying. Every one of them needs a plumber on speed dial. Be the one who picks up, and they will hand you work every week without you asking twice.
  • Property managers, small landlords and realtors. This is the unglamorous half of the work, and it is what keeps the calendar full between emergencies: turnovers, running toilets, heaters at end of life, and sewer scopes before a closing. Invoiced, repeat, scheduled at your convenience. Walk into the three property management offices in your town with a card and your licence class, and ask what their current plumber does badly.

One list is worth keeping from day one: every water heater you install, with the date and the model. Put a note in your calendar for when that tank is getting old, and call. Nobody else is going to call them, and a heater replaced on a Tuesday by appointment is better work than the same heater failing on a Saturday.

Running the work

Find the shutoff first, and test it. Before you open anything, locate the main and the fixture stops and confirm they actually hold. If the main will not close, that is its own job and its own conversation, and it is far better to have it at the start than mid-repair with water going somewhere.

Photograph before you touch. The cabinet floor, the ceiling stain, the crawlspace, the water heater's condition. It takes forty seconds and it settles every later argument about damage that was already there.

Camera before you quote a drain. Roots, a belly, a broken clay hub and a wad of wipes all feel similar on a cable and cost wildly different things to fix. Put the screen in front of the customer while it is down there. People approve work they have seen.

Never leave without watching your own work run. Pressure up, run the fixture, and stand there for ten minutes with your hand on the joint. A callback for a weeping connection costs you a drive; a slow leak inside a wall costs you a claim, a ceiling, and the customer. Ten minutes of standing still is the cheapest insurance in this trade.

Pull the permit at booking, not after. Water heaters, gas lines, repipes and sewer work commonly need one, and the inspection is far easier to schedule at the front of a job than to arrange retroactively when a house sells.

Protect the house and leave it cleaner. Shoe covers, a drop cloth, towels down before the trap comes off, and the old heater out the way you came in. Wipe the cabinet before you close it. The review you get is about the floor, not the fitting.

Triage honestly. Hold a slot open each day for emergencies. Water actively running gets it; a dripping tub does not, and telling somebody that plainly with a real appointment on Thursday is respected more than a vague "later today" that turns into tomorrow.

Using your site

Your admin is at /admin on your own address. Everything you save there is live within a minute or two.

  • Photos. Two slots: the hero and you. Replace the hero with your own work — a heater install or a repipe reads better than a stock kitchen. Put a real photograph of yourself in the owner slot. People are deciding whether to let you into the house at eleven at night.
  • Pricing. The three tiers are yours: the label, the figure and the note under it. Say what the trip fee buys and what "standard repair" means, because the range on its own invites a phone call you did not want.
  • Details. This build has a job-details block — what is included and what is not, one per line. Use it. Diagnosis and a written quote in; drywall repair, permit fees and customer-supplied fixtures out. That list prevents more arguments than anything else on the page.
  • Reviews. Empty until you add them, deliberately — the site ships with nothing invented on it. Ask when the water is running again and they are visibly relieved. That is the moment, and it does not come back.
  • Marketing kit. Generates a social post and a printable flyer, each with a QR code to your site. Print the flyer for the doors either side of a repipe — those houses have the same pipe in the same wall.
  • Leads. Enquiries from the form go to the inquiry email you set in the Business section. The first time you change that address the form service sends it an activation link — click it, or the enquiries silently stop.

Two things on the page are worth understanding before a customer asks you about them. The drip calculator estimates wasted water at an average utility rate; it says nothing about what you charge, and the note under it saying so should stay. The emergency/scheduled toggle changes the message under the form so an emergency is pushed to the phone instead of a web form — which only works if you answer.

Your site also carries a Terms of Service and a Privacy Policy for your customers, generated from your business details. Add your own clauses in the Legal section — the after-hours rule, a deposit on major work, what the workmanship warranty covers.

Licences and insurance

You are already in the part of this catalog where the licence decides who may legally work. This is orientation only, not advice, and the specifics vary sharply by state and sometimes by city. Confirm everything below with your own board before you take money.

  • Your licence class and the work you sell. Most states license plumbers in tiers — apprentice, journeyman, master — with hours and exams behind each, and separately register the contractor who may sell work to the public. Holding a journeyman card is not always the same as being allowed to contract in your own name. Check which registration your board wants for the work on your price sheet.
  • Your licence number in advertising. Many states require it on advertising, on vehicles, or on written quotes. Find out what yours requires and put it where it is required, including on this site.
  • Permits and inspections. Heaters, gas, repipes and sewer typically need a permit and an inspection. The rules are local, the inspectors are local, and knowing both is part of the job.
  • Separate endorsements. Gas fitting, medical gas and backflow testing are frequently their own certification rather than part of a plumbing licence. Do not sell backflow testing on the strength of a plumbing card.
  • Bond and insurance. Contractor registration often requires a surety bond. General liability is separate again, and a personal auto policy generally will not cover a work van — talk to a broker about commercial auto before you are driving with tools in the back.
  • Workers' compensation. The moment you put a helper in the van this becomes a live question, and the rules differ by state including for casual labour.
  • Sales tax. Whether parts and labour are taxed the same way varies by state. Ask an accountant once, early, rather than guessing across a year of invoices.

Do not put "licensed, bonded and insured" on your site until all three are true and current. In this trade it is checkable in about a minute, and a customer with a claim will check.
