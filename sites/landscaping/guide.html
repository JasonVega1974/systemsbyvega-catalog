Your first week

Maintenance landscaping is a route business. What you are building is not a list of customers, it is a sequence of stops tight enough that the mower is running most of the day and the truck is barely moving. Almost every decision in the first week is really a decision about the route, so make them in that order.

  1. Draw the circle before you sell anything. Pick the neighbourhoods you can cover without crossing town, and be ruthless about it. A customer fifteen minutes outside your circle costs you a slot you cannot fill, whatever they pay. Start with the streets you already live on and fill in outward.
  2. Sort out where the green waste goes. Clippings, leaves, branches and bed debris have to end up somewhere, and a residential transfer station may not accept a commercial load. Find the site, learn the hours and the charge, and find out whether brush and grass are handled differently. This is the unglamorous thing that quietly decides whether your fall cleanups work.
  3. Buy for the route you actually have. A commercial walk-behind rather than a homeowner rider, a trimmer, a stick edger, a backpack blower, a trailer with a ramp and a locking box, marked fuel cans, spare line, and more spare blades than you think. A dull blade tears grass instead of cutting it and the whole lawn goes brown at the tips three days later, so blades get swapped, not sharpened mid-week.
  4. Mow a yard with a stopwatch, then price it. Not by square footage — by minutes on site plus drive. A small yard with six gates, a dog, a trampoline and beds along every fence takes longer than a big open one. Do five real yards this way and you will never guess again.
  5. Decide what is in each plan and what is explicitly not. Your site ships three monthly plans; the feature list under each is the contract in practice. Write the exclusions down too — tree work above head height, hauling a customer's junk, hand-weeding an acre of gravel — because the plan customers who cost you money are the ones who kept adding to it politely.
  6. Leave the spraying alone until you are licensed. Mowing is largely unregulated; applying pesticides and herbicides for hire is not, in most places. It is fine to spend your first season not offering it and referring it out. It is not fine to spray anyway.
  7. Sign the first block, not the first ten customers. Door hangers on the two streets either side of the first yard you win, and a start date you can actually keep. Density on one street beats ten scattered lawns every week of the season.

What to charge

Your site ships three monthly plans — Basic Mow & Edge at $120/mo, Full Yard Care at $210/mo and Estate Grounds at $385/mo — plus a season-by-season list of one-time services. Everything, prices and feature lists alike, is editable in the admin. They are a starting shape for your market, not a recommendation.

Monthly beats per-cut, and it is not close. Grass in May needs cutting twice a week; grass in a hot dry August barely grows. A per-cut price makes your billing lurch with the weather and gives the customer a reason to skip. A flat monthly figure over the season smooths both sides and gets you paid on the same day every month. Work out the monthly number from the visits a whole season actually needs, then divide it across the billing months — not by multiplying a per-cut price by four.

Price the minutes, not the acreage. Obstacles, gates, slopes, bed edges and how far you have to carry the trimmer are what set the time. Two yards of identical size can be twenty minutes apart, and the one that looks easy from the street is often the slow one.

Do not buy the route with a low price. It is the most tempting mistake in this trade and the hardest to undo — customers won on price leave on price, and you cannot raise a whole route by a third in year two without losing half of it. Set year one at the number you would still want to be charging in year three, and win the early customers on showing up when you said you would instead.

The seasonal services are the easiest selling you will ever do. Spring cleanups, mulch, aeration and overseed, leaf haul-away, winterisation, dormant pruning — sold to a route that already knows your truck. Quote mulch and materials by the yard after you have measured the beds, on delivered cost plus install, never as a flat number over the phone.

Honour the skip week. Your site promises skip weeks for vacation with no penalty. That is a real promise and customers do take it. Build it into the monthly figure at the start rather than resenting it in July.

Raise prices once a year, in February, across the whole route. In writing, before the season starts, with the new plan sheet attached. Never mid-season, and never one customer at a time.

Finding customers

The channels that matter here are the ones that add stops to a street you are already on. Reach is worth very little in this trade; density is worth everything.

  • Door hangers on the day you mow, on the two streets either side. This is the whole game. The yard is freshly cut, the stripes are visible, your trailer is on the street and your equipment is out. A neighbour reading your card on that particular afternoon is looking at your work while they read it. Nothing else in landscaping converts like it, and it costs printing.
  • February and March, not June. People decide who is cutting their grass before the grass starts growing. Everything you send in high summer is arriving after the decision. Spend the late winter on the route: last year's customers get their renewal and the new plan sheet, and the streets you want get their first hanger.
  • Small commercial and property managers. An HOA common area, a church, a small office park, an apartment complex on your existing route. They sign for a season or a year and pay monthly, which is exactly the shape you want. Know what you are taking on: they will want a certificate of insurance, they often pay on thirty or forty-five day terms, and the standard they hold you to is higher than a homeowner's.
  • Realtors, for the pre-listing cleanup. Fast, one-off, well paid for the day, and it happens in your circle. The good agents come back with the next listing, and some of those houses turn into route customers after they sell.

Keep the truck and trailer lettered and clean. It parks on a residential street for an hour at a time, several times a week, in exactly the neighbourhoods you are trying to win — that is advertising you have already paid for.

Running the work

Fix the route order and keep the same crew on it. Your site promises the same crew every visit, and the reason it matters is not sentiment — it is that the crew learns the yard. Sequence the stops so you never cross your own path, and resist reshuffling for one customer's convenience, because a route reshuffled once gets reshuffled every week after.

Keep a note on every account and make the crew read it. The gate code, which gate actually opens, the dog's name and whether he is friendly, where the sprinkler heads sit in the turf, which bed is the customer's own planting and must not be touched, and where the clippings must not go. That note is what makes a new crew member's first visit look like the fiftieth.

Send the text before every visit, without fail. It is on your page as a promise and it is the single cheapest thing you can do to reduce phone calls, missed gates and dogs in the yard. It is also the thing that slips first when you get busy — automate it, or put it in the crew lead's start-of-day routine so it is not a decision anybody makes.

Raise the deck in the heat. Scalped lawns in July are the most common complaint in the trade and they are entirely your fault. Cut high when it is dry and hot, cut lower in the cool of spring and fall, and never take more than about a third of the blade at once.

Blow toward the yard, never the street. Clippings in the gutter go straight into the storm drain, and that is the kind of thing a city writes to you about. Blowing into a neighbour's driveway is worse — that neighbour was a customer you had not signed yet.

Rain pushes the route, it does not cancel the week. Publish that rule before the first wet week so it is a policy rather than an argument, and keep a half-day of slack at the end of the week to absorb it. Mowing a saturated lawn tears the turf and leaves ruts you will be apologising for in August.

Everything the day needs lives on the trailer. Fuel, two-stroke mix, oil, string, a spare blade set, a belt, zip ties, water. A truck that has to go back to the shop mid-route costs the afternoon.

Lock the box and photograph the serial numbers. Trailers and equipment get stolen out of driveways and job sites, and a serial number written down somewhere is the difference between a police report and a recovered mower.

Plan for winter in August. The mowing season ends and the calendar goes quiet fast. Fall cleanups, leaf haul-away, gutters, dormant pruning, winterising irrigation and — if you want it — snow. Snow removal is real work in a month with no mowing, but it is also a four in the morning obligation to every customer who signed for it. Decide before you sell it, not after the first storm.

Using your site

Your admin is at /admin on your own address. Everything you change there is live within a minute or two.

  • Photos. Replace the hero with your own work as soon as you have any. Shoot a lawn you have just finished, from a low angle down the length of the stripes, in late afternoon light — flat overhead sun at midday makes a good cut look like nothing at all.
  • Pricing. The three plans, their prices and every feature line under them are yours to rewrite. Spend real time on the feature lists: that is where the customer decides which plan they are on and where you decide what you have agreed to do.
  • The seasonal wheel. Your site turns to the current season and shows the services and prices for it. Keep all four seasons filled in and priced even in July — a customer looking in summer is often planning the fall cleanup, and an empty winter panel makes you look like a mowing outfit rather than a year-round one.
  • Details and hours. Say your season honestly. "Weekly service April to October, snow response through the winter" tells someone more than office hours ever will.
  • Reviews. Empty until you add them, deliberately — the site ships with nothing invented on it. Add real ones as you earn them, with permission. The review to ask for is from the customer whose yard you rescued in the spring.
  • Marketing kit. Generates a social image and a printable flyer with a QR code pointing at your site. Print it as the door hanger you leave either side of every yard you cut.
  • Leads. Every enquiry from your booking form is emailed to you and kept as a running list. In March that list is your season — work it the same day.

Your site also carries a Terms of Service and a Privacy Policy for your customers, generated from your business details. Use the Legal section of the admin for your own clauses: the rain policy, what the skip week actually allows, the annual price review, and how a plan is cancelled.

Licences and insurance

This is orientation, not advice, and the rules vary enormously by state and by city. Check what applies where you live before you take money.

  • Business registration. A registered name and a local business licence are usually the first step, and general mowing and maintenance is largely unlicensed work in most places.
  • Pesticide and herbicide application. This is the licensed line in landscaping and it is a bright one. Applying pesticides or herbicides for hire generally requires a commercial applicator licence with testing, record keeping, and sometimes posting and notification duties — and some fertiliser applications are regulated too. It is entirely separate from a business licence. Find out what your state requires before you offer any treated service, and do not let a customer talk you into "just this once".
  • Contractor licensing for the built work. Installing irrigation, retaining walls, drainage, lighting or hardscape can cross into licensed contractor territory, frequently at a dollar threshold on the value of the job. Maintenance usually does not. Know where that line sits in your state before you quote a patio.
  • Water and irrigation rules. Backflow devices often must be tested by a certified tester, and watering restrictions during a dry year can change what you are allowed to run. Both are local.
  • Green waste and burning. What the transfer station takes, whether commercial loads are handled separately, and whether burning brush is permitted at all in your county — and in what season.
  • Noise and equipment hours. Many cities restrict when powered equipment may run, especially at weekends. Your route start time is a compliance question as well as a scheduling one.
  • Insurance. A stone thrown by a mower deck through a window or a car door is the classic claim in this trade, and a string trimmer against a young tree trunk kills it. Talk to a broker about general liability, and about cover for property in your care, before your first paid visit.
  • Workers' compensation. The first hire makes this a live question, and landscaping is not a cheap class of work to cover. The rules differ sharply by state, including for seasonal help.
  • Trailer and vehicle. Trailer registration, weight limits and whether a lettered commercial truck and trailer combination needs additional registration where you operate.

Do not claim on your site that you are licensed, insured or certified until you are, and be especially careful with the word "certified" next to anything sprayed. That is the claim in this trade that a state agency will act on rather than shrug at.
