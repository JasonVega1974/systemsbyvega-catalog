Your first week

This is not a service business with a truck. It is an asset business. The boxes are the capital, the truck is the delivery system, and the two questions that decide everything are where a box goes when it is working and where it sits when it is not. Answer the second one before you spend a dollar on the first.

  1. Find the yard first. Empty cans need somewhere legal to live — commercial or industrial ground, a surface that will take the weight, a gate, and enough room for a truck to swing and to stack boxes two high. Not your driveway, and not a residential lot, because that ends with a zoning letter. Land is the constraint that will cap how many boxes you can own, so settle it before you buy a single one.
  2. Open the landfill or transfer station account before you own a can. Their gate is your cost of goods. Learn the per-ton rate, the hours, which days they close early, what they refuse outright, and every material that carries its own separate rate — mattresses, tires, appliances with refrigerant, roofing shingles, concrete, e-waste. Ask what happens if a load arrives contaminated. Your whole price sheet is downstream of that conversation.
  3. Settle the driver and vehicle question before you buy the truck. A roll-off truck's weight rating decides what licence class you need, what registration and inspection regime applies, and what paperwork has to be in the cab. Find out what your state and the federal rules require for the exact truck you are looking at, from the registry rather than from the seller.
  4. Buy boxes and truck in the right ratio. One truck can serve many cans, and the number of cans is what decides how many jobs can be live at once. Starting light on boxes is the most common way to be busy and stuck at the same time — every delivery has to wait for a pull.
  5. Learn to set a box down without damaging a driveway. Plywood under the wheels and under the rails, every time, on every residential drop. Practise on your own drive until the placement is straight and square first time, because the second attempt is where the concrete gets scarred.
  6. Write your terms and put them on the page. Included tonnage, the overage rate, the rental period, the extra-day charge, the rush fee, the prohibited items, and what happens when you arrive and cannot place the box. All of it in writing, all of it said on the phone at booking.
  7. Photograph one clean placement. Yellow box, square on the plywood, driveway intact, morning light. That single photograph does more for a homeowner's confidence than any paragraph you could write, and it goes on your site under Admin, Photos.

What to charge

Your site ships flat rates by size — 10 yard $299, 15 yard $349, 20 yard $399 — with 2 tons included, $65 per ton over, a $45 rush fee, and rental periods of 3, 7 or 14 days with extra days charged on. Every one of those numbers is yours to set in the admin. They are a starting shape, not a market rate.

The included tonnage is the most important number on your page. The rest of your cost is roughly fixed — drive out, drive back, the box being out of circulation. What varies is weight, because weight is what the gate charges you for. Read the scale ticket on every single load for your first months and write down what each job type actually weighed. That is how you learn where to set the number, and it is knowledge nobody can give you.

Density is what bites. A 10-yard box of broken concrete outweighs a 20-yard box of household clutter without looking remotely as full. That is why heavy material — concrete, dirt, brick, roofing shingles — gets its own rules: a dedicated box, a fill line, or a separate price. Decide that policy before a customer fills a 20 with dirt, because at that point you have a box you cannot legally haul.

Flat rate is a promise about the price, not about the load. Say the included tonnage and the overage rate in the same breath as the price, every time, on the phone and on the page. Then put the scale ticket in the invoice when there is an overage. Operators who quote the flat number alone and mention the tons afterwards are the reason your customers arrived expecting quote games in the first place.

Rental days are inventory, not a courtesy. A full box sitting on a driveway on day twelve is capital that cannot work. That is what the rental period and the per-day charge are for — not to punish anyone, but because the box is the business. Explain it that way and people are reasonable about it.

Charge for the trip you make and cannot complete. Cars parked in the spot, a locked gate, a driveway nobody measured. That is a truck, a driver and an hour gone. Put a dry-run charge in your terms and mention it when you book, never afterwards.

Finding customers

Two completely different customers rent boxes. Homeowners rent once and then disappear for years. Contractors rent all season. You need both, and you find them in entirely different places.

  • Answer the phone. This is not a marketing tip, it is the trade. Somebody searching for a dumpster has a demolition crew booked or a driveway full of debris, and they need a box tomorrow. They call three numbers and hire whoever answers and can give a real price without a callback. Every hour your phone rolls to voicemail is work handed to a competitor. If you are driving, get somebody answering.
  • The trades, at the supply house, early. Roofers, remodelers, flooring installers, restoration companies, deck builders. One roofing crew books boxes week after week through a season; a homeowner books once. Be at the roofing supplier or the lumber yard at six in the morning with cards and your price list on one page. Then keep a list and text them — "I have a 15 free Thursday" gets answered.
  • The box is a billboard on a driveway for a week. Your name and number on both sides, big enough to read from across the street, and repainted before it gets shabby. A safety-yellow box parked in a neighbourhood where the neighbours are also thinking about their garage is the cheapest advertising in this trade — but only if the number is legible and somebody picks up.
  • Property managers, realtors and junk haulers. Turnovers, evictions, pre-listing clear-outs and estate clean-outs are steady and unglamorous. Junk-hauling outfits that do not own boxes are a particularly good relationship — they bring you the container work and keep the labour.

Keep a simple record of every rental: address, size, days, what went in it, what it weighed. That file tells you which contractor to call in March and what to quote the next roof tear-off, and it is worth more each year you keep it.

Running the work

Look up before you back in. Overhead power and service lines are the thing that hurts people in this trade, and a raised roll-off bed is tall. Walk the spot, look up, then look up again from where the bed will be. If it is close, it is a no — reposition or refuse the placement.

Walk the placement with the customer, every time. Where exactly, which way the door faces, and what is underneath: septic tank and leach field, sprinkler heads, a sewer lateral, a gas meter, pavers, a decorative drive. Then lay the plywood. Then photograph the spot before the box lands and again after you pull it. That pair of photographs is the entire answer to "your truck cracked my driveway", and you will need it eventually.

Empty boxes never sleep in a neighbourhood. Not on a residential street overnight because it saves a trip, not in a customer's cul-de-sac because tomorrow's job is nearby. An idle can on a public street is a complaint, sometimes a citation, and always the wrong kind of local reputation. Empties belong on your yard or in the next customer's driveway.

Give the loading rules at drop-off, out loud. Level with the top rail and no higher, weight spread rather than piled at the door end, doors closed and pinned. An overfilled box cannot be tarped and cannot legally leave, and explaining that on the driveway on day one is much easier than refusing to haul on day seven.

Tarp every load, every trip. No exceptions for a short run to the gate.

Route in pairs. The efficient move is a swap: pull a full box and set an empty on the same stop, or cluster a pull and a delivery on the same street. Plan the day around the gate's hours, because a full truck and a closed transfer station means the box sleeps loaded.

Maintenance is not a chore, it is the business. Cables, hydraulic hoses, pins, tires, the tarp system, the hook. When the truck is down, every live rental stops and every booking slides. Keep a service log, do the inspection walk daily, and fix the small thing on a Tuesday rather than the big one on a Friday.

Using your site

Your admin is at /admin on your own address. Everything you change there is live within a minute or two.

  • Photos. Replace the hero with your own truck and your own boxes as soon as you have them. A clean placement on a residential driveway is the shot — homeowners are afraid of the concrete, and that photograph answers the fear.
  • Pricing. The sizes, their labels and their prices are yours to edit, and so are the dimensions, the "fits about this many pickup loads" line and the best-for text under each. Keep the included tonnage and the overage visible next to the price rather than tucked into terms further down.
  • The size calculator. Your site lets a visitor pick a size, a rental length and rush delivery and see the exact number. That is the whole promise of the brand, so keep the inputs matching what you will actually honour on the phone. A calculator that disagrees with your dispatcher is worse than no calculator.
  • Details and hours. Delivery windows and the cut-off for next-day matter more than office hours. Say when you stop taking tomorrow's bookings.
  • Reviews. Empty until you add them, deliberately — the site ships with nothing invented on it. Add real ones as you earn them, with permission. Ask the contractor, not the homeowner; the contractor writes the review that convinces the next contractor.
  • Marketing kit. Generates a social image and a printable flyer with a QR code pointing at your site. Print it as your one-page price sheet for the supply-house counter.
  • Leads. Every enquiry from your booking form is emailed to you and kept as a running list. Treat a form fill exactly like a missed call — same day, or it is gone.

Your site also carries a Terms of Service and a Privacy Policy for your customers, generated from your business details. Use the Legal section of the admin for the clauses this trade needs: prohibited materials, the fill line, the dry-run charge, and who is responsible for checking whether a permit is needed to put a box on the street.

Licences and insurance

This is orientation, not advice, and the rules vary enormously by state and by city. Check what applies where you live before you take money.

  • Business registration. A registered name and a local business licence are usually the first step. Hauling itself is unlicensed in many places — the regulation in this trade attaches to the truck and to the waste, not to the trade name.
  • The truck and the driver. Weight ratings are what decide it: licence class, medical certification, federal and state motor carrier registration, apportioned plates and fuel reporting if you cross state lines, annual inspections and hours rules. Get the answers for the specific truck before you buy it, from the agencies rather than from the dealer.
  • Placement in the street. A box on a public street, alley or parking lane generally needs a permit from the city, and often reflective markers or lights overnight. On a private driveway it usually does not. Cities differ sharply and some require the permit in the hauler's name. Learn the process in every town you serve, and decide in writing whether you or the customer pulls it.
  • Landfill and transfer station rules. Paint and solvents, batteries, tires, appliances containing refrigerant, anything with asbestos in it, e-waste, sometimes mattresses. Some of these are separate-fee items and some are illegal to bury at all. Your prohibited-items list should mirror your gate's rules exactly, and it changes — check it annually.
  • Insurance. General liability, commercial auto on a heavy truck, and cover for the boxes themselves. Use a broker who writes for haulers rather than a personal-lines agent, and describe the work honestly — a policy sold on a vague description is a policy that may not answer.
  • Workers' compensation. The first employed driver or yard hand makes this a live question, and the rules differ by state.
  • Sales tax. Renting tangible property can be taxable in states where a pure service is not, and the delivery charge is sometimes treated differently from the rental. Ask an accountant once, early.

Do not claim on your site that you are licensed, insured or DOT-registered until you are. Numbers on the side of a truck are checkable in a public database, and the contractors who would be your best customers are exactly the people who know how to check.
