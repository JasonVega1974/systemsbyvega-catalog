Your first week

Window cleaning has the lowest cost of entry of any trade on this platform and the narrowest margin for sloppiness. The customer inspects the work by standing still and looking at it, in daylight, with nothing else to look at. Your first week is about technique and about learning to count panes, in that order.

  1. Buy a proper channel, and buy spare rubber with it. A squeegee, two or three channel widths, a washer sleeve, a bucket, a scrim or waffle-weave towel and a detail cloth. The rubber is the entire trade: a nick in the edge draws a line down every pane after it, and the fix is to flip it or change it, not to wipe harder. Carry spare rubber from day one.
  2. Learn the straight pull before you try to fan. Wash the glass properly first — the squeegee removes water, the sleeve does the cleaning. Then overlapping straight pulls top to bottom, wiping the blade after each, and a dry detail cloth around the edges and into the corners. Fanning is faster and looks impressive and it is a month three skill.
  3. Do your own house, then a friend's, and count as you go. Count every pane the way you will bill it, write the number down, then time the job. Two houses is enough to know what your own quote means in hours.
  4. Write your pane-counting rule and never deviate from it. A pane is one piece of glass you can see through. A double-hung is two. A slider is two. A grid sash is one, not nine, because the bars are frame. This rule is already on your site — read it, agree with it or change it, and use whatever it says on every quote.
  5. Decide your ladder ceiling. Second-storey exteriors, yes. Anything over a roofline, anything needing you to walk a roof, anything on a slope where a ladder cannot stand square — get looked at in person or refused. Write it down before your first quote.
  6. Walk a block of storefronts. Before you have any residential work, walk into ten small shops on one street and ask who does their windows. Route work is what makes a window cleaner's week predictable, and the conversation costs you an afternoon.
  7. Photograph one window properly. Same sash, soaped and streaking in one frame, squeegeed and dry in the next, from the same standing spot with the same thing visible through it. That pair goes into the before/after slider on your site.

What to charge

Your site ships with four rows priced by pane count — up to fifteen, sixteen to thirty, thirty-one to fifty, and then a per-pane rate for larger homes and storefront glass. Change the figures to your own market. The structure is the useful part.

Pane count is the only unit a customer can check. Square footage means nothing to a homeowner, hourly rates make them watch the clock, and "it depends" loses the job to whoever gave a number. Counting panes lets you quote from photographs or over the phone, and it lets them verify that the price was fair after you have gone.

Interior and exterior are two different jobs sold as one. The exterior is quick and can be done without anybody home; the interior means drop cloths, moving furniture, blinds, pets and shoes off. That is why the exterior-only route rate on your site is lower per pane. Keep those two numbers distinct and never let a full-service quote get talked into an exterior price.

Quote the extras before you touch the glass: hard-water spotting from a sprinkler, construction overspray, paint flecks and stickers are separate processes, not part of a wash. Walk the house at the quote and point at them, out loud, with the customer standing next to you. If you clean around them silently, the finished job gets judged on the spots you were never paid to remove.

Screens and tracks are where the value actually is. Anybody can wipe glass. Pulling the screens, washing both faces, vacuuming the track and drying it is the work that keeps the glass clean past the first rain — and it is the step most cheap quotes leave out. Say it is included in yours, in that much detail, and you stop competing on the number alone.

Charge a minimum. Three windows at the far end of the county is a drive, a setup and a pack-down whatever the pane count says.

Finding customers

Two very different customers buy this work, and you need both. Storefronts give you a predictable week. Houses give you the bigger single tickets. The channels for each are not the same.

  • Walking storefronts, in person, mid-morning. The single most reliable way to build a window-cleaning round. Not email, not a flyer through the door — walk in when it is quiet, ask for whoever handles the building, and quote the front glass on the spot. Shops on the same street go on the same weekly or monthly run, which means several stops without moving the van.
  • The twice-a-year callback. Residential window cleaning is bought on a rhythm: once after spring pollen, once before the holidays. Put every house you clean into a calendar with a reminder six months out, and phone them. A second clean is a phone call, not a sale, and a round of those calls is the closest thing this trade has to a reliable diary.
  • Builders, painters and estate agents. Post-construction cleanup is its own kind of work — the glass is covered in mortar dust, stickers and overspray, and general contractors need it done before handover on a date they cannot move. One builder can be worth a season of houses. Estate agents want the same thing before listing photographs.

Leave a small dated sticker in a bottom corner of one pane, or on the meter box, with your name on it. Windows are the one trade where the customer is going to be looking directly at your work every day for six months, and the sticker is what turns "we should get these done again" into a call to you.

Running the work

Never work a pane in direct hot sun. The soap dries ahead of the blade and you will chase streaks around the glass all morning. Follow the shade around the house — start on whichever elevation the sun has left — and the same work comes out clean in half the passes.

Do not put a blade on glass you have not tested. Tempered glass and glass with fabrication debris scratch under a razor, and the scratches are permanent, visible, and a whole window to replace. If a scraper is genuinely needed, test a small area at the bottom corner, wet, and look at it before you go further. When in doubt, do not scrape — tell the customer what the mark is and what removing it would risk.

Learn the water-fed pole for upstairs. Purified water dries without spotting, which is what lets you clean a second-storey exterior from the ground and walk away without touching the glass. It is safer than a ladder, faster than a ladder, and it is the single piece of gear that changes what jobs you can take. Ladders remain the answer where a pole cannot reach or the ground will not stand it — with standoffs, so nothing rests on a gutter.

Number the screens as they come out. A piece of tape with a number on the frame and the same number written on your sheet against the room. Screens are not interchangeable, a screen put back in the wrong opening either rattles or will not seat, and hunting for the right one at four o'clock is a miserable end to a good day.

Inside the house, you are a guest first and a cleaner second. Cloths under every sill before the bucket comes in, blinds lifted and returned to exactly where you found them, shoes off or booties on, and ask about pets at the door rather than finding out when one goes out through an open slider.

Walk it at low sun and let them point. Finish the job and then walk the outside with the customer when the light is raking across the glass, which is when every flaw shows and when they were going to look anyway. Redo whatever they point at, right then, with the kit still out. Getting there first is the difference between a callback and a referral.

Using your site

Your admin is at /admin on your own address. Everything you change there is live within a minute or two.

  • Photos. A hero and a before/after pair. The pair has to be the same window, same standing spot, minutes apart — soaped and streaking, then dried — because the whole point of the slider is that nothing else changed. A pair from two different windows reads as a trick and people notice.
  • Pricing. Four rows keyed to pane bands, with the label, the price and the note on each yours to edit. Keep the pane-counting rule visible on the page: it is what makes a flat quote checkable, and it is why a visitor trusts the number before they have spoken to you.
  • Details and hours. Say what your season actually is. If freezing weather stops you, say so, and say that a weather move costs the customer nothing. That sentence prevents the phone call you would otherwise get every January.
  • Reviews. Empty by design — the site ships with no invented reviews on it. Add real ones as you earn them, with the customer's permission.
  • Marketing kit. Generates a social image and a printable flyer with a QR code pointing at your site. Take the flyers on the storefront walk so you leave something behind when the manager is out.
  • Leads. Every enquiry from the quote form is emailed to you and kept as a running list, with the pane band they picked already attached. That is most of a quote before you have called them back.

Your site also carries a Terms of Service and a Privacy Policy for your customers, generated from your business details. Add your own clauses in the Legal section of the admin — your access and cancellation terms, and what you do and do not warrant on scratched or already-damaged glass.

Licences and insurance

This is orientation, not advice, and the rules vary enormously by state and by city. Check what applies where you live before you take money.

  • Business registration. Most places want a registered business name and a local business licence before you trade. Usually quick and cheap, and the first thing to do.
  • Liability insurance. You are on ladders, over other people's landscaping, and inside their houses handling glass. Talk to a broker before your first paid job and be specific about ladder work and interior access. Commercial customers will ask for a certificate before they let you on site, and some will want to be named on it.
  • Working at height. Rules on ladders, ropes and anything suspended differ by state and get much stricter above a certain height or on commercial buildings. Anything involving rope descent is a specialist, regulated activity — do not drift into it because a customer asked nicely.
  • Keys, alarm codes and being alone in a house. Commercial and recurring residential customers will eventually offer you access. Decide how you hold keys and codes and write it down. Some customers will ask about background checks; answer honestly rather than claiming a screening process you do not run.
  • Workers' compensation. The moment somebody else goes up a ladder for you, this becomes a question, and the rules differ sharply by state including for part-time help.
  • Sales tax. Whether a cleaning service is taxable varies by state. Ask an accountant once, early.

Do not claim on your site that you are licensed, insured or bonded until you are, and do not say "guaranteed streak-free" unless you intend to go back for free every time somebody says it is not. Write the guarantee you will actually honour.
