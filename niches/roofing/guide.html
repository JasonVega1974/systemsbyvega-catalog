Your first week

You already know how to dry in a roof and where water actually gets in. What you are building this week is the part of a roofing company that is not roofing: how a job is scoped in writing, how the tear-off leaves the property, and who calls you before they call the out-of-town crew whose truck appeared after the last storm.

  1. Decide now whether you do insurance work. Storm and claim work is a different business sitting inside the same trade — adjusters, scopes, supplements, deductibles, and a customer whose real counterparty is their carrier. It can fill a calendar for a year after one hailstorm. It also carries rules that will end a roofing company that treats them casually. Pick, in writing, and build the rest of the week around the answer.
  2. Sort disposal before you sell a tear-off. A dumpster account or your own dump trailer, the transfer station's hours, and what they charge for shingles. Nothing makes a first replacement go sideways faster than a full trailer on a Saturday afternoon and a nowhere to put it.
  3. Settle the labour question honestly. Your own crew or subcontracted crews, and the paperwork behind whichever you choose. Workers' compensation and certificates of insurance live here, and roofing is the trade where that question is asked hardest. Get the answer before the first tear-off, not after an injury.
  4. Set a repair minimum and hold it. A roofer who will drive across town for a two-shingle repair spends the season on ladders for nothing and never gets to the work that actually needs doing. Price the smallest job you are willing to do, including the drive and the ladder set, and say the number on the phone.
  5. Build the inspection kit and the report that comes out of it. Camera, chalk, a moisture meter, ladder standoff, harness. Then decide the fixed order you photograph in — elevations, ridge, valleys, penetrations, flashing, gutters, attic — so every report looks the same and nothing gets missed on the roof you were rushing.
  6. Inspect one roof and write the report you would hand a stranger. Your own house or a friend's. Photographs, findings, what is urgent and what is not, and a scope with real line items. That document is your actual product; the shingles are just what it results in.
  7. Put your own roofs on the site and open the storm inspection offer. Replace the stock hero with a roof you finished. Then tell the realtors, agents and property managers you already know that free storm inspections are open and you are local.

What to charge

Your site ships three tiers: a range for single-issue repairs, a range for a full replacement on a typical house, and a storm and insurance tier that is deliberately scoped after inspection rather than priced on the page. Rewrite the figures to your market — they are a starting shape, not a recommendation.

Price the scope, not the roof. A written scope names the tear-off layers, the underlayment, ice-and-water where it goes, drip edge, valley treatment, every flashing and boot, ridge and intake ventilation, nails per shingle, cleanup and haul-away, and the warranty. Two quotes that differ by thousands are almost always two different scopes. Write yours out and hand it over, and you stop being compared on a single number.

Put a unit price on decking replacement in every proposal. "Replacement sheathing at this much per sheet, billed only if needed, photographed before it is covered." Rotten decking is the surprise on nearly every tear-off, and it is the single most common place a roofing job turns into an argument. Agreed in advance it is a line item. Discovered on day two with no price attached, it is you either eating it or looking like you invented it.

Measure before you price a replacement. Off the roof, off a drawing, or off an aerial report — but measured. Pitch, layers, penetrations, story height and access all move the number, and eyeballing from the driveway is how a roofer ends up paying to finish somebody's house.

Insurance work is priced to the scope, not discounted from it. You are doing the work the scope describes, supplementing what the scope missed, and documenting both. If your written scope is thinner than the adjuster's, you have given away the difference.

Never offer to absorb, waive or "take care of" a customer's deductible. Many states prohibit it outright and carriers treat it as fraud. Check what applies where you work, but the safe answer is always no — and being the roofer who says that plainly, out loud, is worth more with a homeowner than the discount ever was.

Do not chase the storm-chaser price. The crew working out of a rented truck is quoting a number they only have to honour until they leave town. You are quoting a roof you will still be standing under in five years, in a town where people know your name. Say that in the estimate, in one sentence.

Finding customers

Roofing demand arrives in two shapes: the slow, steady stream of old roofs and leaks, and the flood after weather. You need a way to catch both, and the second one brings competition into town within a couple of days.

  • The street you are already working on. When your dumpster is in a driveway, the houses either side almost certainly have the same age roof and took the same hail. That is the day to knock — not a month later. Yard sign up while the crew works, door hangers on the nearest dozen houses, and a face-to-face offer of a free look while your ladder is already on the truck.
  • Realtors, home inspectors and property managers. A roof line on an inspection report has a closing date attached to it, which means somebody needs an answer in days, not weeks. The roofer who returns that call the same afternoon and puts findings in writing gets every one of that agent's future clients. This is the channel that carries you through the seasons with no storms in them.
  • Insurance agents and adjusters, if you took the storm work. Not to get referrals handed to you, but to be known as the local roofer who documents properly, meets the adjuster on the roof, and does not pad a scope. That reputation travels between offices quickly, in both directions.

Being visibly local is your entire advantage over the out-of-town crews after a storm, so make it concrete rather than a slogan: a real address, a real face on the site, the town named in plain words, and a story about a roof you did three years ago that you can still walk somebody past.

Repairs and gutter work are the small door into everything else. Somebody who calls about a leaking boot today is somebody whose roof you will replace in two years — but only if you turned up for the small job without acting like it was beneath you.

Running the work

Get on the roof. Diagnosing from the driveway is guessing, and homeowners can tell. Where a roof genuinely should not be walked — wet, steep, brittle tile, storm-damaged decking — say why, and use a drone or a pole camera instead.

Give the customer the photographs. Every one, in order, with a plain sentence under each. This is the single habit that separates roofers people trust from roofers people are wary of, and it costs you the time it takes to send a link.

Tarp the same day for an active leak. Even when the repair is weeks out. A tarp is a promise you can keep inside an hour, and it buys you enormous patience on everything after.

Protect the property like the review depends on it, because it does. Plywood over the condenser, tarps on landscaping and the pool, the driveway kept clear for their cars, and a magnet sweep at the end of the day and again in the morning. The complaint you will get is never about the shingles. It is about a nail in a tyre.

Watch the weather like a foreman, not an optimist. Never open more roof than you can dry in before dark or before the front arrives. Rescheduling a tear-off costs a day. Rain in an open house costs you the whole job and more.

Have a callback rule and honour it fast. Any leak reported on work you did gets a person on the roof the same week, no argument about whether it is yours. Half of them will not be. Going anyway is what a workmanship warranty means in practice, and it is why the neighbours keep calling you.

Do the boring details right. Flashing rather than caulk, pipe boots replaced rather than reused, valleys done the way the manufacturer specifies, intake ventilation actually open. Those are the things that make a roof last and that nobody can see from the ground — which is exactly why the roofer's character is the product being sold.

Using your site

  • Photos. Two slots: the hero and you. Replace the hero with a finished roof of your own, shot from the ground in low, raking light — late afternoon shows the shingle texture and the ridge line, while midday flattens everything into a grey rectangle. Put your face in the owner slot; homeowners are choosing who to trust on their roof.
  • Pricing. Three tiers, each with a label, a figure and a note. Keep the third tier scoped-after-inspection rather than priced — an insurance job cannot honestly carry a number on a web page, and saying so is a credibility point, not a gap.
  • Details. The owner block is where the local part goes: how long you have roofed here, what you have seen this valley's weather do. Hours live in the Business section — and if storm season means you answer at seven in the morning, say that.
  • Reviews. Empty by design; nothing on this site is invented. Ask on the day the crew leaves, while the yard is clean and they are relieved.
  • Marketing kit. A social post and a printable flyer, each with a QR code to your site. Print the flyer as your door hanger for the street you are already working on — that is the highest-value paper you will ever hand out.
  • Leads. Enquiries from the form go to the inquiry email you set in the Business section. The first time you change that address the form service emails it an activation link — click it, or the enquiries stop with no warning.

The self-check on the home page is doing more than it looks like. It asks four questions — missing shingles, granules in the gutters, daylight or stains in the attic, storm in the last year or two — and when the visitor clicks through to the form, their answers are pasted into the message as a summary. Read that summary before you call back. A lead that says granules yes and daylight yes is a different phone call, and a different first appointment, than one that answered no to everything.

Your site also carries a Terms of Service and a Privacy Policy for your customers, generated from your business details. Add your own clauses in the Legal section — your workmanship warranty period, deposit terms, what happens if the decking is worse than it looked.

Licences and insurance

Roofing is licensed in most states and regulated hard in the ones that get storms. This is orientation only, not advice, and the details vary by state and by city. Confirm all of it with your own board before you sell work.

  • Contractor licensing or roofing registration. Most states require a licence or a registration to contract roofing work for the public, often with an exam, an experience requirement and a dollar threshold above which it is mandatory. Some cities add their own. Find out which apply to you and keep them current.
  • Bonding. A surety bond is commonly part of licensing or registration in this trade. It is not insurance and does not protect you — know the difference before you describe yourself as bonded.
  • General liability and workers' compensation. You are working at height, over other people's homes, with crews. Talk to a broker who writes roofing specifically, and describe the work honestly — a policy sold on a vague description is the policy that will not answer. Workers' compensation is a live question the moment anyone else is on your roof, subcontractors included, and roofing is one of the trades where it is checked.
  • Certificates from your subs. If you use subcontracted crews, collect current certificates of insurance and keep them on file. An uninsured crew on your job becomes your problem in every sense that matters.
  • Insurance and storm-work rules. Several states regulate roofing contractors specifically around claims: what you may say about a claim, contract cancellation windows after a storm, and the absolute prohibition on absorbing a deductible. If you take storm work, read your state's rules yourself, in full, once.
  • Permits and inspections. Replacements typically require a permit and a final inspection. Pull it at the start of the job.
  • Drone use. Flying a drone as part of a commercial inspection falls under federal rules for commercial operation. Check what is required before you charge for photographs taken from one.

Do not put "licensed, bonded and insured" on your site until all three are true and current. In a trade that attracts out-of-town crews after every storm, being verifiable is your advantage — and claiming it before it is true throws that advantage away.
