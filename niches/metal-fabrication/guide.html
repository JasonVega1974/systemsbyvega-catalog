Your first week

You can already lay a bead, read a print and fit a frame square. Fabrication businesses do not fail on welding. They fail on quoting — on the fit-up hours nobody counted, on steel bought at last month's price, and on the custom job that turned into three redesigns for the same money. This week is about the paperwork side of a shop.

  1. Split the business into shop work and rig work, and price them apart. Work that comes to you and work you drive to are different businesses with different costs. Decide what the truck carries, how far it goes, and what the minimum is for rolling it out — travel, fuel, generator time and the hour you lose loading.
  2. Set the shop rate, the material markup and the minimum charge. Three numbers, written down once. Without a minimum you will spend a Tuesday driving across the county to weld one broken bracket, and that Tuesday is gone.
  3. Open the steel account and learn what today's steel costs. Prices move. Get a service centre account, find out their delivery days, ask about the remnant rack, and put a rule on your own quotes — thirty days, then it gets re-priced. Quoting from a number you remember from last spring is the classic way to build a gate for free.
  4. Write the quoting sheet you will use for every job. Material weight and cost, cutting, fit-up, welding, grinding, finish, hardware, delivery and install, plus shop overhead and consumables. Fit-up and finish are where the hours actually go, and they are the two lines every new shop leaves off.
  5. Build three pieces for the portfolio and photograph them properly. A gate hung on somebody's driveway, a firepit lit at dusk, a close bead on a repair. Fabrication sells off photographs more than any other trade in this catalog — put the work on a plain background or on the customer's property, never on a cluttered bench, and get down to its level rather than shooting from standing height.
  6. Sort hauling and install before you sell a big build. Trailer, straps, how a sixteen-foot gate gets to a site and who lifts it. Decide whether your price includes setting posts and hanging it, because "delivered" and "installed" are two very different numbers and customers assume the second.
  7. Tell the county you are here. Farms and ranches, equipment dealers, truck and trailer shops, fence contractors, the machine shop that does not weld. Say the two things that matter: the rig comes to you, and one welder does the job start to finish.

What to charge

This site does not publish prices, and that is the right call for fabrication — no two jobs are alike enough to put a number on a web page. The Pricing panel in your admin says as much and leaves nothing to fill in. Everything below is about how you arrive at the number you give in person.

Quote from something you can hold to. A sketch with dimensions, a photograph with a tape measure in it, or your own measurements. If the customer gives you the numbers, write on the quote that you are building to the dimensions they supplied. That sentence has saved a great many gates.

Charge for design time on custom work. Drawing, sizing material and cut lists is real work, and giving it away means you have effectively made a free drawing for whoever they show it to next. A small design fee, credited against the build if they go ahead, is fair to both sides.

Take a deposit that covers material on anything custom. Especially anything with a name, a brand or an address cut into it — nobody else on earth wants that gate. Steel bought for a job that evaporates is money sitting in your rack for years. Material up front, balance on delivery, and say it plainly when you quote.

Price repair work honestly, including when the answer is no. Some repairs cost more than the part is worth, and telling a farmer that straight in September is how you get every job he has in October. Have a minimum for a repair call-out and say it before you drive.

Do not compete with an import. A stamped-metal firepit from a big box store is not the thing you make, and the customer who wants that price is not your customer. Sell the difference — plate thickness, a weld that will not open, and the fact that a person in this county made it and will fix it.

Finding customers

Fabrication work does not arrive the way service-trade work does. Nobody has an emergency gate. What they have is a broken machine in a field, an idea they cannot describe, or a picture they saw and want in steel.

  • Agriculture, equipment and truck shops. The steadiest work in this trade and the least visible from outside it. Broken loader arms, cracked implements, corral panels, trailer frames, hitches and racks. When something breaks mid-harvest it does not wait, and the rig that turns up the same day owns that customer permanently. Walk into the equipment dealers, the truck shops and the trailer places and leave a card with the words "mobile" on it.
  • Your own work standing on the roadside. A driveway gate is a billboard at the end of a driveway, and it will be there for twenty years. Put a small, tasteful maker's mark on the frame — a plasma-cut name plate on the hinge post is enough — and photograph every gate the day it hangs.
  • Local groups and marketplace listings for the retail side. Firepits, address signs, tables, shelving and brackets fill the slow weeks and get your name in front of people who did not know they could buy custom steel locally. Post the photographs, name the town, price them properly. This is a shop window, not a discount rack.
  • Other welders and shops. The shop with no TIG capability, the one that will not do aluminium, the one whose CNC table is booked. Overflow runs both ways and costs nothing to set up — go and introduce yourself to the people you were told were the competition.

Keep a running job list with numbers on it, and photograph everything you deliver. In fabrication your portfolio does the selling that a price list does in other trades, and a job you did not photograph is a job that will never sell you another one.

Running the work

Measure the opening yourself. Every time, for anything that has to fit something else — gates, railings, replacement panels, a deck on somebody's trailer. Customer measurements are given in good faith and are wrong often enough that a shop which builds to them without checking will eventually rebuild a gate at its own expense.

Tack, check, then burn out. Everything tacked and squared, diagonals measured, hinges dry-fitted before a single final weld. Once it is welded out, square is a negotiation with a grinder.

Weld the sequence, not the seam order that feels natural. Stagger, alternate sides, and let the work cool between passes. Heat is what racks a gate, bows a table top and pulls a frame out of true, and it happens silently while you are pleased with how the beads look. A piece that ends up twisted is a piece you build twice.

Finish is what the customer actually sees. They cannot judge penetration. They can absolutely judge grind marks, spatter and mill scale. Prep it, prime it, paint or powder coat it properly — bare steel in the path of a sprinkler is rusty before the invoice is paid, and that rust will be described as your workmanship.

Treat off-site hot work like the fire risk it is. Ask what is on the other side of the wall, what is in the tank, and where the hay, fuel and paint are. Extinguisher on the ground, spark blanket down, and a fire watch after you stop — a fire that starts an hour after you drove away is still yours.

Number every job and keep its paperwork together. The sketch, the cut list, the material, the photographs of the finished piece. When somebody calls for a second gate to match the first one, three years later, that folder is the whole reason you can say yes without re-measuring the county.

Stay inside your certification. Structural and pressure work frequently requires a welder qualified to a specific procedure, and it is inspected by people who ask for the paper. Turning that work down is not a weakness — quietly taking it and hoping is the thing that ends shops.

Using your site

Your admin is at /admin on your own address. Everything you save there is live within a minute or two.

  • Photos. Two slots: the hero and you. Sparks make a good hero, but a finished piece in daylight sells better than a photograph of an arc. Put your face in the owner slot — this site's whole pitch is one welder from start to finish, and that is not believable without a person attached to it.
  • Pricing. There is nothing to fill in. The panel tells you the site is quote-based, which matches how you actually work. Your quoting happens in person, on paper, over a tape measure.
  • Reviews. This build has no reviews section on the public page, and the admin panel says so rather than pretending otherwise. Customer words still earn you work — put them in your marketplace listings and your social posts instead, with permission.
  • Details. The owner block is the important one here. Write who you are, what you have been welding on for how long, and what kind of work you like best. Buyers of custom steel are choosing a person, not a company.
  • Marketing kit. A social post and a printable flyer, each with a QR code to your site. The flyer belongs on the noticeboard at the feed store, the equipment dealer and the trailer shop.
  • Leads. Enquiries from the form go to the inquiry email you set in the Business section. The first time you change that address the form service emails it an activation link — click it, or the enquiries stop with no warning at all.

Two parts of the page deserve a word. The job board ships with example tickets — job numbers, material specs, category chips — to show the shape of a portfolio; editing those tickets is a change to the page rather than an admin field, so treat them as a template for how yours should read: material, process, size, delivered. And the capability panel lists processes — TIG, MIG, stick, oxy and plasma cutting. Those are capabilities, not credentials, which is exactly why they are safe to publish. Do not add certification language to your site unless you hold the certification and can produce the paper. It is the one claim in this trade that gets checked by people who know what they are looking at.

Your site also carries a Terms of Service and a Privacy Policy for your customers, generated from your business details. Add your own clauses in the Legal section — the deposit rule, how long a quote stands, what "delivered" includes and what installation costs.

Licences and insurance

Welding sits differently from the other licensed trades: the work itself is usually not licensed, but pieces of it are certified, permitted or regulated in ways that catch people out. This is orientation only, not advice, and it varies by state and by city. Check locally before you take money.

  • Business registration. A registered business name and a local business licence are usually the first step, and a shop premises brings zoning and fire-code questions with it — hot work, oxygen and acetylene storage, ventilation. Ask the fire marshal rather than guessing; they would rather tell you first.
  • Welding certification. There is generally no licence to weld, but structural, pressure and code work commonly requires a welder qualified to a specific procedure, tested and documented. That certification belongs to the person and to the procedure, not to the shop, and it expires. Never advertise a certification you do not currently hold.
  • Contractor licensing when you install. Fabricating a railing and installing it on somebody's building can be two different regulatory questions. Structural work, work attached to a building and anything a building department inspects may pull you into contractor licensing or permits. Ask before you quote the install.
  • Mobile work and commercial auto. A welding rig is a commercial vehicle carrying compressed gas. A personal auto policy generally will not cover it. Talk to a broker about commercial auto, inland marine cover for the equipment on the truck, and what your liability policy says about hot work off your own premises.
  • Liability and hot work. Be explicit with your broker about cutting and welding on customer property. Fire exclusions in a policy bought on a vague description are exactly the kind of surprise you cannot afford.
  • Trailers you build. A trailer built for somebody else usually has to be titled, and many states require an inspection, a verified weight and an assigned identification number before it can be registered. Find out what your state requires before you build one, not when the customer is at the DMV counter.
  • Workers' compensation. Live the moment you have a helper, and the rules differ by state including for part-time and casual labour.
  • Sales tax. Fabricated goods, installed work and repair labour are often taxed differently from each other, and a resale certificate for the steel you buy is usually part of the picture. Ask an accountant once, early.

Do not put "certified", "licensed" or "insured" on your site until each one is true and current. In fabrication those words are read by people who understand precisely what they mean, and being caught wearing one you have not earned costs more than the work it would have won.
