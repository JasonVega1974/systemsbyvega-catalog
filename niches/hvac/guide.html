Your first week

You can already read a superheat, find a bad capacitor with your eyes and size a system properly. This week is not about the mechanical side. It is about the thing that makes a heating and air business survive its first summer: a list of people who have already paid you to look at their system, so July is not the only month anybody calls.

  1. Decide what you service and what you install. Which brands you will hang, whether you are a dealer for anything, and whether you touch commercial rooftops or stay residential. Then get the supply house accounts open and find out who has a counter open on a Sunday in July — because that is the day you will need a motor.
  2. Get the refrigerant side straight before the first job. Your Section 608 card where the work needs it, recovery machine, recovery cylinders, scales, and a record you actually keep. This is the part of the trade with federal teeth in it, and it is not a corner anybody gets to cut.
  3. Price the maintenance plan first, before the repairs. The site ships three tiers — a one-off tune-up, a two-visit season pass, and a whole-home plan for a bigger house or a second system. The plan list is the business. Repairs are what happens to people on the list.
  4. Build a flat-rate repair book. Capacitor, contactor, ignitor, flame sensor, blower motor, condensate pump, thermostat swap, a pound of refrigerant. Price them once at the kitchen table so you can hand somebody a number at the furnace instead of saying you will call back — because the shop that calls back tomorrow has already lost the job.
  5. Stock the van for the two weeks a year that matter. The first heat wave and the first hard freeze are when everything fails at once and everyone's shelf is empty. Common capacitors, contactors, universal ignitors, a couple of blower motors, filters in the four sizes your area's builders used.
  6. Book the shoulder season, right now. Whatever month it currently is, the call you should be making today is to everyone you know: get on the schedule before the weather turns. Spring for cooling, autumn for heating. An operator who only works during extremes is an operator with no calendar in April.
  7. Photograph one clean install. A line set run straight and secured, a condenser sitting level on a pad, a return that is actually big enough. Homeowners cannot judge a braze joint, but they can tell tidy from careless in a photograph, and that is genuinely the judgement they are making.

What to charge

Your site ships three plan tiers and a stated promise that repairs are always quoted flat and approved before the work starts. Rewrite the figures to your market. The structure is the valuable part: a low-friction way to meet a customer, a plan that brings you back twice a year, and a bigger plan for a house with two systems.

The tune-up is not the product. The list is. A tune-up is how you get inside a house, put your hands on the equipment and learn its age. Everything after that — the plan, the repair, the eventual replacement — comes from having been there once. Price the tune-up so that booking it is easy.

Quote repairs flat, and get the yes before the wrench. The site says you do this, so do it. Diagnose, write the number, show it to them, wait. It ends the entire genre of complaint this trade has a reputation for.

Never quote a replacement off the sticker on the old unit. The old system was sized by whoever installed it, and there is a decent chance they guessed too. Do a load calculation, look at the ductwork, and put the reasoning in the proposal. Selling a bigger unit because the customer asked for "more powerful" gives them short cycling, a clammy house and a grudge with your name on it.

Charge the diagnostic on no-heat and no-cool calls, and say so when they ring. Told on the phone it is normal. Discovered at the door it is a fight. Whether it credits toward the repair is your call — decide once and never improvise it.

Price after-hours before the weather makes you. The rate for eleven at night in January should be set in October, in writing, and quoted calmly when the phone rings.

What the plan really sells is a place in the queue. During a heat wave, when everybody is booked a week out, plan members go first. Say that plainly on the page — it is the only benefit that a customer feels the moment they need it.

Finding customers

This trade has a demand curve shaped like a barbell: two frantic stretches and a lot of quiet in between. The channels that matter are the ones that fill the quiet.

  • Your own maintenance list, phoned twice a year. This is the channel. Every tune-up either turns into a plan member or it was a one-time transaction you paid to acquire. Call the list in early spring and early autumn and book them before the weather does it for you. Keep each customer's filter size and equipment age in that list — it makes the visit fast and it gives you a genuine reason to call.
  • Being reachable on the first bad day. The first 100-degree day and the first hard freeze generate more calls than any other days of the year, and most shops stop answering by noon. Whoever picks up and gives an honest window — even a bad one — books the work. Clear those days in advance and keep your phone answered.
  • Realtors, home inspectors and property managers. "System at end of expected life" on an inspection report is a job with a closing date attached. Landlords with a handful of units need somebody who will go out for a tenant without drama. Both want the same thing: a reply today and an invoice that matches the quote.

The fourth channel, worth a mention because it is easy to miss: builders and remodelers adding a mini-split to a garage conversion, a shop, or an addition. Small, clean, schedulable installs — exactly the work you want in the middle of a mild October.

Ask for the review while the house is coming back to temperature and they are standing there grateful. Not by email two days later.

Running the work

Take readings and write them down where the customer can see them. Delta-T across the coil, static pressure, refrigerant pressures, amp draw against the nameplate. Putting those numbers on the invoice is what separates a technician from a parts changer, and it is what makes the next recommendation believable.

Look at the ductwork on every visit. Most comfort complaints that get blamed on the equipment are a return that is too small, a duct disconnected in the crawlspace, or a system fighting a house that leaks. Fixing the box while ignoring the ducts is how you get a callback on a brand new system.

Do not condemn a heat exchanger without documenting it. Photographs, combustion analyser and CO readings, and the reason written plainly. This is the single accusation the trade gets hit with hardest — that a cracked heat exchanger is invented to sell a furnace. Document it and you can defend it. Call it without proof, even honestly, and you are indistinguishable from the people who do.

Triage by who has nothing. No heat in a freeze and no cool in a heat wave outrank everything, and inside that, elderly customers and infants come first. Say the order out loud to the people you are moving down the list. Almost everyone accepts it.

Protect the house. Drop cloth at the furnace, shoe covers upstairs, and take the old equipment away the same day. A condenser left on the side of the house "until next week" undoes a good install.

Teach the thermostat before you leave. Five minutes, with them holding it. Half of the after-hours calls you would otherwise get in the first month after an install are somebody fighting a schedule they did not understand.

Leave the filter answer behind. Write the size on the unit, on the invoice, and in your list. Then remind them when the season changes. It is a small thing that customers remember for years.

Using your site

Your admin is at /admin on your own address. Everything you save there is live within a minute or two.

  • Photos. Two slots: the hero and you. This site is built on a light background, so a bright, calm photograph works far better here than a dark mechanical-room shot. A finished install, a comfortable room, or your van in daylight. Put your own face in the owner slot.
  • Pricing. The three plans are yours — the label, the figure and the note. Keep the promise that repairs are quoted flat and approved first, because it is the most persuasive thing on the page and the easiest to keep.
  • Details. Use the owner block to write the plain version of who you are and how long you have been doing this. Hours belong in the Business section — and if you take after-hours calls, say what after-hours means.
  • Reviews. Empty until you add real ones, deliberately. Nothing on this site is invented, and the moment somebody's air comes back on is the moment to ask.
  • Marketing kit. A social post and a printable flyer with a QR code to your site. The flyer works best in early spring and early autumn: a plan offer, left with a property manager or dropped on the street you just worked.
  • Leads. Enquiries from the form go to the inquiry email you set in the Business section. The first time you change that address the form service emails it an activation link — click it, or nothing arrives and you will not be told.

The house diagram on the home page is a system map: a visitor clicks the furnace, the ducts, the thermostat or the attic and gets the symptoms, what a visit covers and what it costs. It is fixed copy in this design rather than something you edit in admin, and it is doing a specific job — teaching somebody enough vocabulary to describe their problem before they call you. Read what it says so you are not contradicted by your own home page.

Your site also carries a Terms of Service and a Privacy Policy for your customers, generated from your business details. Add your own clauses in the Legal section — what the plan includes, how it renews, your after-hours rate and your cancellation window.

Licences and insurance

Heating and air is among the most heavily regulated trades in this catalog, with a federal layer on top of the state one. This is orientation only, not advice. Confirm everything with your own state board and with the EPA rules as they apply to your work.

  • Refrigerant certification. Handling refrigerant requires EPA Section 608 certification, and it is federal — it does not vary from state to state. Recovery, record keeping and the sale of certain refrigerants are all regulated. Keep your card, keep your cylinders straight, and keep the records.
  • State contractor licensing. Most states license HVAC contractors, often in classes, sometimes with a mechanical or gas endorsement layered on. Some license the individual and separately register the business that sells the work. Check which one your board wants for the jobs on your price sheet.
  • Gas and combustion. Gas piping, venting and combustion appliance work are frequently licensed or permitted separately from the mechanical work around them. Know exactly where that boundary sits where you work.
  • Permits and inspections. Changeouts, new installs and gas work commonly need both. Pull the permit at the start of the job, not when the customer is selling the house three years later.
  • Bond and insurance. Contractor registration often requires a surety bond. General liability is separate, and a personal auto policy generally will not cover a work van — talk to a broker about commercial auto and describe the work accurately.
  • Workers' compensation. Live the moment you take on a helper or an apprentice, and the rules differ by state, including for seasonal labour in your busy months.
  • Manufacturer warranty registration. Not a legal requirement, but it behaves like one: equipment warranties commonly depend on registration and on installation by a licensed contractor. Register every unit you install, the week you install it.

Do not put "licensed, bonded and insured" or any certification claim on your site until it is true and current. Both the state licence and your certification status are checkable, and the customer who checks is the one already unhappy with you.
