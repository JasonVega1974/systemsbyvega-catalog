Your first week

Mobile detailing lives or dies on two things nobody thinks about until the first booking: where the water comes from and where it goes. Everything else is technique you can improve. Solve the van first.

  1. Carry your own water and your own power. A tank, a pump, a pressure washer that will draw from the tank, and an inverter or a generator. The moment you ask a customer for a hose and an outdoor socket you are a man with buckets, not a service that comes to them, and half the reason they booked you has gone. Work out how many cars one tank actually does before you book two in a morning.
  2. Decide what happens to the runoff. Soapy water running down a driveway into a storm drain is the one real regulatory exposure in this trade and it varies by city. Learn the local answer, and learn rinseless and waterless methods properly so you have a way to work on a driveway that drains straight to the street.
  3. Buy for one process, not for a shelf. Two buckets with grit guards, a foam cannon, a wash mitt, more drying towels than you think, wheel brushes and a barrel brush, an iron remover, clay, a wet-dry vacuum with proper crevice tools, brushes for vents, and an extractor if you are selling interiors. Keep the glass towels separate and never let them touch a dressing.
  4. Detail the worst car you can borrow. Not your clean one. A dog owner's estate car with sand in the seat rails is the portfolio, because nobody can see the work in a car that was almost clean already. Photograph it half done: a split line down the middle of a bonnet, or one seat vacuumed and one not, says more than a finished car ever will.
  5. Time yourself against the packages. Your site publishes roughly one hour, roughly three and roughly five. Those are promises about somebody's afternoon. Run a normal sedan through each one with a timer going, and adjust the packages in the admin until the times are true for how you actually work.
  6. Write your intake questions down. Vehicle size, pets, smokers, children, when it was last cleaned, and photographs of the boot, the back seat and the footwells. Ask every time, in the same order. Almost every detail that goes badly starts with a question you did not ask.
  7. Book your first jobs in one neighbourhood on one day. Ask friends, and ask them to ask a neighbour. Three cars on one street is one tank, one setup and three sets of neighbours watching you work, which is how this business grows in the first month.

What to charge

Your site ships with three packages and a list of add-ons, plus a note about larger vehicles. Every figure is yours to edit in the admin. They are a starting shape for your market, not a recommendation.

A package is a time budget with a name on it. You are selling hours in a driveway, so the honest way to price is to know what each package costs you in time on a normal car and to hold that line. When a package regularly overruns, the package is priced wrong; do not fix it by working faster on the bit that shows least.

The add-ons are what keep the base price honest. Pet hair, headlight restoration, odour treatment, extraction, engine bay. They exist so that the standard detail can stay a standard price instead of being padded to cover the worst car you might meet. Quote them as add-ons out loud, before you start, never as a surprise on the invoice.

The interior is where the money is lost: pet hair, a smoker's headliner, spilt milk under a child seat and sand in every seam are each double the labour of the job somebody thought they were booking, and nobody volunteers any of it on the phone. Ask for photographs of the back seat, the footwells and the boot before you quote, every single time. If they will not send them, quote a range and say why.

Size is a real cost, not an upsell. A three-row SUV that a family lives in is a different job from a commuter hatchback. The site already carries a note about larger vehicles; keep it, say the number on the phone, and never discover it in the driveway.

Do not sell a coating until you can do one. Ceramic and paint protection work is a separate craft with a separate preparation standard and a warranty attached, and a badly applied coating has to be polished off. It is a fine thing to add later and a terrible thing to learn on a paying customer's bonnet.

Charge for the drive when you leave your area, and be willing to say no to the booking forty minutes out that does not stack with anything else that day.

Finding customers

You spend three hours parked at a kerb doing visible, satisfying work. That is an advertisement most trades would pay for, and it is the first channel.

  • The street you are already parked on. A tidy van, a sign, and a card through the letterbox either side before you pack up. People walking past will stop and ask what you charge — have a straight answer and a way to book them on the spot. Then cluster: when somebody on that street books, offer their neighbour the same day.
  • Anybody about to sell a car. Private sellers listing on Marketplace, small used lots that need recondition work, and people handing back a lease. This work is unemotional, repeats, and comes with a deadline that makes the decision easy. Small lots in particular will keep you busy on the days homeowners will not.
  • Video of the work itself. Detailing is one of very few trades where the process is watchable: the foam, the first pull of an extractor, the pet hair coming off a seat in a sheet. Short clips, shot on a phone on a tripod, showing the dirty water. Post them to local groups and to your own page. Nothing you write will do what thirty seconds of an extractor does.

The thing worth building deliberately is the repeat schedule. A customer who has the Express Refresh on the same driveway every month or every quarter is the most valuable thing in this business, because the car never gets bad again and each visit is short. Ask at the end of a full detail: "do you want me back in eight weeks to keep it like this?" Then put it in the diary while you are standing there.

Office parks and business lots are worth a walk-in. Cars sit still all day, the owners are inside, and you can do several without moving the van.

Running the work

Wheels first, then top down. Wheels and arches before anything else, because you never want that brake dust and iron running down a panel you have already washed. Then pre-rinse, foam, contact wash from the roof down, and dry before you open a door.

Never wash in direct sun on hot paint. Everything dries before you can rinse it and you spend the rest of the afternoon chasing water spots and streaks. Park in shade, work early, or cool the panel first. This is the single most common reason a detail looks worse in the finished photograph than it did in person.

Decontaminate before you touch the paint with a machine. Iron remover, then clay, then polish. Dragging a pad across bonded grit is how you put the swirls in that you were hired to take out. And learn machine polishing on a scrap panel from a breaker's yard before you learn it on somebody's car — paint can be burnt through on an edge in seconds and it does not grow back.

Interiors run dry to wet. Everything out, everything vacuumed, seat rails and seams last, then chemicals and steam, then extraction. Do it in the other order and you are spreading mud. Leave the plastics matte, not shiny: a greasy dashboard reads as cheap and glares at the driver at sunset.

Extraction needs drying time you have to plan for. A shampooed carpet in a cold driveway at five in the evening is a car that smells of damp in the morning and a phone call you do not want. Book wet interior work early in the day, open it up, and use an air mover if you own one.

Walk the car with the owner in daylight with the doors open. Before you take payment. Anything you missed gets fixed in two minutes while you are still there, instead of appearing in a review three days later.

Look after the towels. Wash them separately, no fabric softener, colour-coded by use, and bin any that hit the ground. A dropped towel picks up grit and the next panel it touches gets scratched. It is the cheapest way in this trade to do expensive damage.

Using your site

Your admin is at /admin on your own address. Everything you change there is live within a minute or two.

  • Photos. Replace the hero with your own work as soon as you have something worth showing. Shoot in shade or under an overcast sky, not in direct sun, and get low so the reflections in the paint run along the panel. There are owner and before and after slots too.
  • The results strip. It ships empty on purpose, with a line saying photographs land there as jobs come in, and that is an honest empty state rather than somebody else's portfolio. Fill it with your own cars, and only your own.
  • Pricing. The three packages, the times on them and the note under each one are yours to rewrite, and so is the line about larger vehicles. The add-on list is what stops the base price being a lie, so keep it visible and keep it current.
  • The zone panel. The five detail zones on your home page — paint, glass, interior, wheels, trim — are what the customer clicks through to understand what a detail actually includes. That text is your standard written down. When you change how you work, change it there too.
  • Details and hours. Put your real working hours in, and be honest about the season. Say which towns you drive to, because a mobile business is judged first on whether it comes to their street.
  • Reviews. Empty until you add them, deliberately. The site ships with no invented reviews. Add real ones as you earn them, with the customer's permission, and ask at the walkaround while they are still looking at the car.
  • Marketing kit. Generates a social image and a printable flyer with a QR code pointing at your site. Print the flyer for the letterboxes either side of every car you do.
  • Leads. Every enquiry from the booking form is emailed to you and kept as a running list. Reply asking for the interior photographs before you quote.

Your site also carries a Terms of Service and a Privacy Policy for your customers, generated from your business details. The Legal section of the admin is where your own clauses go: your cancellation window, what you need at the address, and what happens when a car turns out to be a bigger job than the photographs showed.

Licences and insurance

This is orientation, not advice. Detailing is one of the less regulated trades as a trade, which makes it easy to assume there is nothing to check. There are three or four things, and they matter. Check what applies where you are before you take money.

  • Business registration. A registered business name and a local business licence is usually the whole trade requirement, and it is quick. Do it before you take money rather than after.
  • Where the water goes. This is the real one. Wash water carrying soap, brake dust and road grime into a storm drain is a discharge, and cities differ sharply on what they allow, what they require you to contain, and whether a mobile washer needs to register at all. Ask your city, and have a way of working — mats, berms, or rinseless methods — that keeps you legal on a driveway that runs to the street.
  • Cover for the car itself. General liability is the baseline, but the cover people forget is for a customer's vehicle while it is in your care, including when you move it. You will be putting a machine polisher on paint you did not pay for. Say that to the broker in those words.
  • Commercial auto on the van. A personal policy generally does not cover a vehicle used for business, and the tank, the generator and the equipment in the back are a separate conversation again.
  • Chemicals. You are carrying acids, solvents, degreasers and compressed products. Keep the safety data sheets, store them properly, and know what you are meant to do about a spill in somebody's driveway before you have one.
  • Noise and neighbours. Generators, pressure washers and extractors at eight in the morning run into noise ordinances and HOA rules. Worth knowing your local hours before somebody else tells you them.
  • Workers' compensation. The moment there is a second person working alongside you this becomes a question, and the rules differ sharply by state.

Do not claim on your site that you are licensed, insured, certified or an approved installer of anything until each of those is true. Coating manufacturers publish their accredited installers, so that particular claim is checkable in about ten seconds.
