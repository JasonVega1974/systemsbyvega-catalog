An irrigation system is plumbing, not decoration

Under the grass, a sprinkler system is pipe, valves, wire and pressure — and it behaves like any other utility: it works when it is sized right, and it fails in ways you can trace. So we start at the meter, measure what the property will actually give us, and build the zones around that number instead of around a catalog page. When something breaks two seasons later, the same map tells us where to dig.

How a system goes in

Meter first, trench last

Walk & measure

We meet you on the property, read static pressure at the hose bib, time the flow into a bucket, and sketch the turf, beds and hardscape the zones have to respect.

Design the zones

Heads get grouped so every zone runs at a pressure it can actually hold. Rotors stay off the drip beds, the shady strip stops sharing a valve with the south lawn, and nothing doubles up just to save a valve.

Trench, plumb & wire

Mainline in, valves in a box you can find again, lateral pipe pulled to depth, and every valve wire tagged at both ends. We photograph the trenches open, so the as-built we hand you is a record and not a sketch.

Tune & program

We run each zone with you standing there, adjust arcs and swap nozzles until the spray patterns overlap, then set a seasonal controller schedule and write it inside the door of the box.

The irrigation year

Three visits the valley actually needs

Spring

Start-up

Charge the mainline slowly so the surge does not blow a fitting, then walk every zone looking for what the winter moved.

Summer

Mid-season tune

Arcs drift, soil settles and nozzles wear. A pass through the zones in July catches the dry corner before the lawn shows it.

Fall

Blow-out

Compressed air pushed through each zone in turn until the heads run dry, valves drained, and the backflow assembly cleared.

Asked at the truck

Questions we get on the first walk

Do I need to be home for the walk?+
For the first walk, yes — we need to find the meter, the point of connection and the outlet the controller will live near, and you know your property better than we do. After that, most repair visits happen without you.
How deep does the pipe go?+
Lateral lines run below the reach of an aerator and the mainline runs deeper still, so a fall blow-out is what protects the system rather than depth alone. We tell you the depths we pulled on your ground and write them on the as-built.
What about the backflow device?+
An irrigation line drawing from a public main needs a backflow assembly, and most valley districts want it tested once a season by a tester on their own list. We install, repair and winterize the assembly, and we will point you at your district's test requirement — the test itself is theirs to run.
Can you work on a system somebody else installed?+
Most of our repair work is exactly that. We start by finding the valve boxes and running each zone, because an unlabeled system is a map problem before it is a plumbing problem.
When should the system be blown out?+
Before the first hard freeze settles in — in the Payette Valley that usually means late October into November. We start booking blow-outs in September and work the route in order.
Do you do drip, or only spray heads?+
Both. Beds and garden rows generally want drip on their own valve, not a rotor borrowed from the turf zone beside them, and converting a bed off spray is one of the more common jobs we do.
