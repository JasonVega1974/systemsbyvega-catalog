Your first week

General contracting is the one trade in this catalog where the work you are selling is mostly other people's hands. You already know how to build. The company you are starting this week is a scheduling, scoping and money-handling operation with a tool belt attached, and every problem you will have in your first year comes from one of those three, not from carpentry.

  1. Write down what you self-perform and what you sub. Concrete, decks, fences, tile, punch lists — whatever your own crew actually does well. Then name the trades you will bring in. That single list decides your pricing, your schedule and how many jobs you can have open at once, and it is the document you will keep rewriting for a year.
  2. Build the sub bench before you need it. An electrician, a plumber, an HVAC tech, a tile setter and a drywall finisher who answer their phones. Collect a current certificate of insurance and a licence number from each one and keep them in a folder. An uninsured sub on your job is your liability, and you will not think to ask on the morning you are desperate.
  3. Get a contract you will actually use. Scope, allowances, a payment schedule tied to milestones, change orders in writing, cleanup, who pulls the permit, and what happens if the customer's selections arrive late. Have a lawyer read it once. A contractor working off a one-page estimate is a contractor who will lose an argument he was right about.
  4. Price two real jobs on paper with today's supplier numbers. Not last year's. Take a bathroom and a patio, price them line by line — material, labour, subs, dump fees, permit, overhead, contingency — and then look at what you would have quoted from memory. The gap between those two numbers is the single most useful thing you will learn this week.
  5. Set the deposit and the draw schedule. How much up front, what each milestone releases, and how much is held to the final walk-through. Check what your state allows before you decide the deposit — several cap it — and then write it into the contract.
  6. Shoot the before photographs on the very next job. Before you move a single tool. Your site has a drag-across before-and-after slider on the front page, and it is the strongest thing on it. You cannot go back and take a before.
  7. Tell the people who feed this trade you are taking work. Past customers, realtors, property managers, the supply house counter, the cabinet showroom. Contracting referrals come from people who are standing next to a homeowner who has just realised they need somebody.

What to charge

Your site ships three tiers as bands rather than prices — a refresh, a rebuild and a remodel, each with a rough duration and a list of the work that lives in it. Rewrite them to your market. Bands are the right shape here: they filter the enquiries before they reach your phone, which is most of what pricing on a contracting website is for.

Bid the scope, not the customer's budget. Somebody who tells you they have fifteen thousand dollars has told you nothing about what the job costs. Price it properly, show the number, and let them decide what to cut. Contractors who quietly build a job down to fit a budget end up eating the difference in the last two weeks.

Change orders in writing, approved before the work happens. Every time. This is the discipline that decides whether a remodel makes money, and it is the one that feels rudest to enforce when you are standing in a kitchen with a customer you like. A text message saying what changed, what it adds, and "reply yes to go ahead" is enough. Verbal change orders are gifts you did not intend to give.

Put allowances in the contract with real numbers. Tile at a stated price per square foot, a fixture allowance, a cabinet allowance. Then when the customer falls in love with something three times that, it is an easy conversation and a line item instead of an argument. Selections that were never priced are how the material budget you costed carefully disappears into somebody else’s tile choice.

Price the parts nobody bids. Dump runs and disposal, protection and daily cleanup, permit and inspection time, the two hours a week you spend on the phone with suppliers, and the drive. On a small job these are the whole margin.

Do not bid against a handyman. If the job is small enough that a one-man operation with no insurance and no overhead can do it, either take it as a relationship job at your real price or refer it out. Winning it at their price teaches a customer exactly the wrong thing about what you cost.

Finding customers

Contracting work comes from proximity and from other people's mouths. Almost none of it comes from advertising, which is why the channels below are all about being standing somewhere useful.

  • The street you are already on. A truck parked outside a house for a week, a dumpster, a new driveway, a fence going up — that is your billboard, and it is only up for a few days. Yard sign on day one, and knock the four nearest doors before the job finishes. Exterior work especially: the neighbours have been watching a stained driveway or a grey fence for a year and now they have watched somebody fix one.
  • Realtors and property managers. Pre-listing punch lists, turnover work between tenants, and repairs that a closing depends on. It is deadline-driven, unglamorous, repeat, and it pays on invoice. The contractor who answers the phone and shows up when he said gets all of an agent's work, because the alternative is an agent explaining to a seller why the closing slipped.
  • Suppliers and showrooms. The counter staff at your tile, cabinet, flooring and lumber suppliers are asked "do you know a good contractor?" constantly. They refer the person who does not make them look bad — who orders early, picks up on time, and does not send customers in confused. Introduce yourself as somebody they can send people to, then behave that way for a year.
  • The small job as a door. Pressure washing, a fence repair, a punch list off somebody's fridge. It gets you inside the house of the person who will be planning a kitchen in two years, and it costs you a day. Do it properly, photograph it, and leave.

Keep one spreadsheet: every customer, what you did, when, and what they said they wanted to do next. "You mentioned the deck after the patio" is a phone call in March that most contractors never make.

Running the work

Do not open more jobs than you can finish. Two half-torn-out kitchens is how a good contractor gets a bad reputation in a small town, and it happens because saying yes felt easier than scheduling honestly. Finish, then start.

Sequence for the subs and the inspections, not for yourself. The electrician and the plumber need the space in the right order, the rough inspection has to pass before anything closes, and every one of those has a lead time. Build the schedule backwards from the inspection dates and tell the subs their dates a week out.

The complaint is almost never about the work. It is about silence. A homeowner living in a construction site with no idea what happens tomorrow gets anxious, and anxious customers become difficult ones. Send one end-of-day message with a photograph and one sentence about tomorrow. It takes ninety seconds and it is the highest-return habit in this entire trade.

Protect the house properly. Floor protection on the path in, a zip wall between the work and the living space, dust control at the source, and one designated bathroom — or a porta-john outside for the crew. People forgive a slow job. They do not forgive drywall dust in the bedroom.

Manage cash so the job funds itself. Bill on milestones, order material against draws, and never fund one job out of another job's deposit. That is the exact mechanism by which contracting businesses collapse, and it always starts as a temporary measure.

Walk the punch list with the customer, in writing. Both of you with a pen, room by room. Agree the list, agree the date, and hold enough of the final payment that finishing it matters — but not so much that they are holding you hostage.

Own your subs' work in front of the customer. They hired you, not the tile setter. Fix it, then sort it out privately with the sub. A contractor who blames a subcontractor in a customer's kitchen has told that customer he is not in charge of his own job.

Using your site

Your admin is at /admin on your own address. Everything you save there is live within a minute or two.

  • Photos. Four slots on this build: the hero, you, and a before and after pair that feed the drag slider on the front page. The slider only works if the two photographs line up — same spot, same height, same angle, ideally the same time of day. Mark where you stood. A before-and-after that jumps around when you drag it looks like two different houses.
  • Pricing. The three bands are yours: label, range and the note underneath it. The note is where the duration goes — "days, not weeks" tells a homeowner more about which band they are in than the dollar figure does.
  • Details. The owner block is the most valuable text box on this site. The whole promise of the page is that the person who answers the phone is the person running your job, so write that in your own words, badly rather than slickly.
  • Reviews. Empty by design; the site ships with nothing invented. Ask at the punch-list walk, while you are both looking at finished work.
  • Marketing kit. A social post and a printable flyer with a QR code to your site. Print the flyer for the doors near a job while your truck is still in the driveway.
  • Leads. Enquiries from the form go to the inquiry email you set in the Business section. The first time you change that address the form service emails it an activation link — click it, or the enquiries stop and nothing tells you.

Two things on this site are part of the page design rather than admin fields: the FAQ block and the three-step "how most projects run" section. They are written to say what a careful contractor would say — that licensed trades get licensed subs, that the price comes in writing after a walk-through — so read them once and make sure you actually work that way. If you need the wording changed, that is a request rather than a setting.

Your site also carries a Terms of Service and a Privacy Policy for your customers, generated from your business details. Add your own clauses in the Legal section — the deposit rule, the change order rule, your warranty period, and what happens when selections arrive late.

Licences and insurance

General contracting is regulated in most states, and it carries the extra complication that several trades inside your scope are licensed separately from you. This is orientation only, not advice. Confirm everything with your own state board and building department.

  • Contractor licensing or registration. Most states require a licence or registration to contract for the public, frequently above a dollar threshold, and often with an exam, an experience requirement and a financial test. Some states license by classification — residential, commercial, specialty. Cities sometimes add their own registration on top. Find out which apply to the work on your price bands.
  • The trades you cannot cover with your own licence. Electrical, plumbing, mechanical and gas work are separately licensed in most places, and being the general contractor does not extend your licence over them. Use licensed subs, keep their numbers on file, and let the permit reflect who actually did what.
  • Bonding. A surety bond is commonly required as part of licensing or registration. It protects the customer, not you. Know the difference before you write the word "bonded" anywhere.
  • General liability and workers' compensation. Talk to a broker who writes contractors, and be accurate about what you self-perform. Workers' compensation becomes a live question the moment you have employees, and in many states uninsured subcontractors can be treated as your employees for exactly this purpose — which is why the certificate folder matters.
  • Permits and inspections. Structural, electrical, plumbing and mechanical work commonly need them. Whoever pulls the permit carries the responsibility, so never let a customer pull an owner-builder permit to save money on your job.
  • Contracts, deposits and cancellation rights. Several states regulate home improvement contracts specifically: required written terms, deposit caps, and a cancellation window on contracts signed in the customer's home. Read what applies to you once, properly.
  • Liens and lien waivers. Understand how mechanic's liens work in your state, what notices are required and when, and collect lien waivers from subs and suppliers as you pay them. A sub who was not paid can lien your customer's house, and the customer will hold you responsible.
  • Sales tax. Whether materials and labour are taxed differently on improvement work varies by state. Ask an accountant once, before your first remodel, not after a year of invoices.

Your site ships with the licence line blank on purpose, and it stays off the page until there is a real number to put in it. Do not write "licensed, bonded and insured" anywhere until all three are true and current — it is public, checkable, and the person who checks is the person already unhappy with you.
