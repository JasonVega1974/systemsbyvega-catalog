Your first week

This is a business-to-business contracting business that happens to involve cleaning. Your buyer is a facilities manager, an office manager or a property manager. They are not buying a clean building — they are buying not having to think about the building, and they will judge you on paperwork and consistency long before they judge you on a floor.

  1. Learn to measure a building before you learn to bid one. Cleanable square footage, not the number on the lease. Walk it room by room and write down what actually changes the work: restroom count and fixture count, floor types and where they change, how many desks make trash, whether there is a kitchen, and how many entrances hold dirt in winter. That sheet is the bid.
  2. Build your own production rate, on a real building. Clean one office end to end and time it — trash, restrooms, vacuuming, floors, glass, separately. Square feet per hour, measured by your own hands with your own equipment, is the only honest basis for a price. Rates quoted at you by a supplier or a forum are somebody else's building and somebody else's crew.
  3. Write the scope document you will attach to every contract. Every task with a frequency beside it: nightly, weekly, monthly, quarterly. This is the whole product. A janitorial relationship dies from scope drift, and the only defence is a document both sides agreed to that anybody can pick up and check.
  4. Get your paperwork before you get your first meeting. A registered business, a W-9 ready to hand over, and a conversation with an insurance broker about what commercial buildings will ask you to produce. Many buyers cannot legally let you through the door without a current certificate on file, and "I can get that" loses to the company that attached it to the email.
  5. Decide how you will handle keys and alarms, in writing, first. Who gets access, how it is signed out and back in each shift, who is on the alarm call list, and what happens when somebody sets it off at eleven at night. A false alarm on your second week is survivable. Not having an answer for how it happened is not.
  6. Walk into ten buildings and ask one question. Not a pitch — ask who handles the cleaning contract and when it comes up for renewal. Write the answers down. This is a long sales cycle: the building that says no in March is the building that calls in November, and only if you wrote the name down.
  7. Put your real service window on the site. After close, overnight, early morning, weekends. A facilities manager filters on that before anything else, because a crew in the building during business hours is a different conversation with their staff.

What to charge

Your site ships with four rows priced per square foot per month — nightly, three nights, a weekly reset, and a deep clean or post-construction job quoted after a walkthrough. Change every figure to your own market. The per-square-foot monthly structure is what makes this comparable to what a facilities manager already has on file.

Price the month, invoice the month. Commercial cleaning is a contract with a monthly figure, not a stack of visits. It makes the buyer's budgeting simple, it makes your own side of the arrangement steady enough to schedule people against, and it ends the argument about whether a five-week month costs more.

Bid from your own measurements or do not bid. Square footage is the biggest single driver, but restrooms, floor type and trash volume move a price a long way from what area alone suggests. A building with a lot of tile and eight restrooms is not the same job as an open office of the same size. Never quote a building you have not walked.

Scope creep is what kills janitorial contracts, not price. "While you are here, could you also..." arrives politely and never stops. The answer is never no — it is "yes, here is a change order". Put the added task on paper with a frequency and a price, get it agreed, and add it to the scope. Doing it free once makes it part of the contract in everybody's mind except yours.

Consumables are a decision, not an afterthought. Paper, liners, soap. Either the building supplies them or you do and itemise them at cost — but say which in the contract. Liners in particular are sized to the cans in that building, and a beginner who agreed to supply them without checking discovers it as an unbudgeted standing order.

Keep floor care off the base rate. Stripping and waxing hard floors, carpet extraction, high dusting and window work are periodic projects priced separately and scheduled separately. Folding them into a monthly figure hides real cost inside a number the buyer is comparing to somebody who did not include them.

Do not chase the lowest bid. In this trade the cheapest contract is usually the one that was underscoped, and the building that hired on price alone will be re-tendering it within the year — which is your opening, not your model.

Finding customers

Nobody searches for a janitorial contractor at midnight the way a homeowner searches for a plumber. Commercial work is found on a timetable, through people, and it takes months. All three channels below are about being known before the contract comes up.

  • Property management companies. The relationship that moves the most work in this trade, by a distance. One property manager may be responsible for a dozen buildings, which means one conversation can put you in front of a portfolio instead of a suite. Ask what they hate about their current arrangement and answer that specifically.
  • Walk-ins, in daylight, with a renewal question. Go into office parks, clinics, credit unions and light industrial buildings and ask who handles the cleaning contract and when it renews. Keep the list in a spreadsheet with the renewal month and call two months before it. A contract that is not up is not lost, it is scheduled.
  • General contractors, for post-construction cleans. Final cleans before handover are one-off, on a hard deadline, and awarded by a superintendent who needs somebody reliable more than somebody cheap. They also put you in a finished building at the exact moment its new tenant is deciding who cleans it.

Your local chamber and the referral groups that meet at seven in the morning are worth more here than they are in any consumer trade, because the people in the room are the people who sign facilities contracts.

Keep every building you ever walked in one file: name, contact, square footage, current provider, renewal month, what they complained about. That file is what makes the second year easier than the first, and it is the only asset in this business that compounds.

Running the work

Leave evidence in the building, every visit. A dated sign-off sheet on a clipboard in the janitorial closet, initialled, with a line for anything that could not be done and why. It is the single cheapest thing you can do and it answers the complaint this trade always gets: nobody knows who was here last night or what they did.

Run keys on a log and treat it seriously. Access issued to named people, signed out and signed back each shift, alarm codes never shared informally, and a written process for what happens the day somebody leaves. A building that trusts you with its keys is a building that renews without shopping.

Understand the payment gap before you sign your first building. You pay people on your payroll schedule. A commercial customer pays on their accounts-payable cycle, after an invoice, on terms they set — and the gap between those two dates is the thing that catches new janitorial companies, not a lack of work. Ask about invoicing terms and who approves the invoice during the walkthrough, at the same time you ask where the janitorial closet is.

Night work is a supervision problem. Your crew works when you are asleep and nobody is watching, which means quality is whatever your system enforces rather than whatever you would do yourself. Walk buildings unannounced, on a rotation, at the hour the work actually happens. Inspect against the scope document, not against your mood.

The same crew, named, is your actual product. Rotating strangers through a secure building is what facilities managers are trying to escape. Assign people to buildings and keep them there. When the assignment has to change, tell the customer before it changes rather than letting them discover a new face on the camera.

Restrooms decide the contract. Every complaint that reaches a facilities manager comes from an employee, and employees complain about restrooms and about their own desk area. Whatever else gets tightened up, those get done properly every visit — including the consumables count, so nobody ever runs out on a Monday morning.

Handle a complaint within one visit. Acknowledge it in writing the same day, fix it before the next scheduled clean, and write on the sign-off sheet that it was closed out. A commercial customer does not expect perfection. They expect a documented response, because that is what they in turn have to show their own boss.

Using your site

Your admin is at /admin on your own address. Everything you change there is live within a minute or two. Remember who reads this site: a facilities manager checking whether you are a real company before they reply to your email.

  • Photos. A hero and a before/after pair. Shoot commercial interiors, not domestic ones — a workstation, a lobby, a restroom, a break room. Get written permission from the building before you photograph anything inside it, and keep client names, signage and screens out of frame.
  • Pricing. Four rows, with the label, the price and the note on each yours to edit. The per-square-foot figures also drive the estimator block on the homepage, which reads the rates straight out of these rows — change a price here and the estimate moves with it, so there is never a second set of numbers to keep in step.
  • Job details. What is included and what is not. This is where the base scope lives and where you say plainly that floor care, carpet extraction and post-construction work are quoted separately. Written boundaries are the thing this buyer is actually shopping for.
  • Details and hours. State the office hours and state the service window separately. "Office weekdays, crews on site after close" answers the first question a facilities manager has.
  • Reviews. Empty by design — the site ships with no invented reviews on it. Commercial references are worth more than star ratings, and they need explicit permission: ask the facilities manager before you quote them, and never name a building's security arrangements.
  • Marketing kit. Generates a social image and a printable flyer with a QR code pointing at your site. Print it as the leave-behind for the walk-in visits, so the office manager has something to hand their boss.
  • Leads. Every walkthrough request from the form is emailed to you and kept as a running list. Reply the same business day and put the walkthrough in the diary on the call — the sales cycle is long enough already without a week lost to email.

Your site also carries a Terms of Service and a Privacy Policy for your customers, generated from your business details. Add your own clauses in the Legal section of the admin — change-order handling, access and key terms, notice periods, and how invoices are raised.

Licences and insurance

This is orientation, not advice, and the rules vary enormously by state and by city. In this trade the requirements are also set by your customers, which is a second layer on top of the law. Check both before you sign anything.

  • Business registration. Most places want a registered business name and a local business licence before you trade. Commercial buyers ask for the entity name and a W-9 as a matter of routine, so have both ready.
  • Liability insurance and the certificate. Commercial customers will ask for a certificate of insurance and many will ask to be named as an additional insured on the policy. That is a specific thing your broker does, not a phrase you can write on a letterhead. Talk to a broker about what commercial contracts in your area ask for before you bid one.
  • Janitorial bonding. Buyers ask about it because your people are alone in their building with their property. It is a separate product from liability cover. Ask a broker what it actually covers before the word appears anywhere on your site.
  • Employees versus contractors. The decision that shapes everything else in a janitorial company. Whether the people cleaning your buildings are legally employees is determined by tests set in law, not by the label on an agreement, and it drives payroll tax, workers' compensation and what your insurance actually covers. Get this answered by an accountant before your first hire.
  • Workers' compensation. Usually triggered by having employees at all, with thresholds that differ sharply by state — and commercial customers routinely require evidence of it before they let a crew in the building.
  • Chemical safety and training. Federal workplace rules on hazard communication apply to the products your crew uses. Keep safety data sheets accessible, label secondary containers, and train people before they use anything stronger than general-purpose cleaner.
  • Specialised environments. Medical, dental and food premises carry requirements beyond ordinary office cleaning, sometimes written into the customer's own compliance obligations rather than into cleaning law. Ask the customer what they are obliged to require of you and put it in the scope.
  • Sales tax. Whether commercial cleaning is taxable varies by state and can differ from residential. Ask an accountant once, early.

Do not claim on your site that you are licensed, insured or bonded until you are, and do not describe your staff as screened, certified or trained to a standard you cannot produce paperwork for. A facilities manager will ask for that paperwork before signing, and asking is the entire point of their job.
